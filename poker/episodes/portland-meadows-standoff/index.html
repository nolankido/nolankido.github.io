Portland Meadows Stand Off: A-K Facing Two Shoves

Published November 4, 2026 · Nolan Kido · Archived-source video companion

A-K on the button. Then the action changed.

I began this Portland Meadows Evening Stand Off hand with about ten big blinds and A-K on the button. I chose to call a raise, then faced a big-blind shove and an early-position reshove. I called again, lost, and initially believed I could not have folded. Back at the hotel I saw the second decision differently. The difference between my first call and the new decision is what makes this hand worth reviewing.

The Stand Off rewarded surviving the final round.

Earlier that day I had won a different Portland Meadows Deepstack tournament. The evening Stand Off was separate. In my recorded explanation, the $200 event started players with 30,000 chips and stopped after twelve rounds, with those still holding chips then paid according to their stacks through an ICM-based calculation. That unusual finish mattered to how I was thinking about staying alive.

Decision 1: flat A-K or move all-in?

An early-position player raised to 15,000. Another player called in middle position. I had A-K on the button. My v9 source packet labels the original hole-card still A-clubs and K-diamonds; that specific visual has not been freshly checked against the live YouTube upload. I considered jamming, but worried the original raiser would call and the other player might also continue. My recorded plan was to call and continue strongly if an ace or king appeared on the flop.

That was the first decision. Calling could invite more preflop action from the blinds, and my shallow stack limited the room to adjust if it happened. The earlier plan to move in on an ace-high or king-high flop was a plan, not proof that I would see a flop or that such a shove would be profitable.

Decision 2: a big-blind shove and the opener's reshove.

The big blind then shoved. The original early-position raiser shoved again, the middle-position caller folded, and both all-in players covered my remaining stack according to my narration. There was no flop to respond to. I now had to decide whether to risk approximately 46,000 additional chips and my tournament life against two all-in opponents.

These arithmetic checks explain why the second call is not simply the first decision repeated. The pot and the number of opponents changed. My remaining chips had value even though 15,000 had already gone into the pot. Without exact player stacks, full payout information and reliable opponent ranges, this account cannot supply a defensible chip-EV or ICM verdict.

I called. The hands were K-K and Q-Q.

I called the additional amount. My recorded account says the big blind showed pocket kings and the original opener showed pocket queens. My A-K lost. I reported being eliminated in round 10 and receiving no cash from this Stand Off event. The earlier Deepstack trophy was a result from a different event, not an outcome of this one.

Seeing two strong holdings after the call is useful information about this one showdown, but it does not prove either opponent would always act that way with exactly those hands, or that calling A-K was always wrong.

In the car I thought I could not fold. At the hotel I reconsidered.

Immediately after busting, I said I did not see how I could fold A-K in that spot. Later, after time away from the table, I focused on a different question: if the big blind could close the action by calling only 9,000 more, why had that player chosen to shove instead?

I reasoned that some strong but nonpremium hands might prefer to call, while the reshove could signal a much stronger range. I also revisited the original raiser's decision to shove after that move. In my later narration I described the first flat as probably defensible but the additional call as a mistake under my revised assumptions.

Those are retrospective player reads, not established ranges. The big blind might take different actions with various holdings in different stacks and tournament incentives. My conclusions depended on assumptions I had not confirmed. The available packet contains no validated solver solution or ICM calculation, so the page does not label my fold conclusion as theoretically optimal.

Study question: If you were given the original opener's possible opening and reshoving ranges, the big blind's jam and flat frequencies, all effective stacks and the payout schedule, how would the tournament value of calling 46,000 more compare with folding? That is a question for a separate, explicitly configured analysis, not a solved result hidden in this narration.

My portable lesson from the episode was to treat the changed action as a new decision. Ask what I planned, what each opponent's new move may mean, and whether I would risk the remaining chips if I were deciding afresh.

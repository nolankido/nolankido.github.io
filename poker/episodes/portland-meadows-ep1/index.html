Portland Meadows: From 6.5 Big Blinds to a Trophy

Published November 2, 2026 · Nolan Kido · Public video companion

From 6.5 big blinds to a trophy.

In this Portland Meadows High Noon Deepstack episode, I return from the second break with 65,000 chips, only 6.5 big blinds, and 26 players still in. The video follows the short-stack recovery, two consequential all-ins, and an unexpected official first-place trophy after a four-way deal.

Watch the turning points.

  1. 0:00, 6.5 BB and 26 players left. At about 4:15 PM, the big blind will be 10,000 with a 10,000 big blind ante, leaving 65,000 chips and a stack of 6.5 big blinds.
  2. 4:09, the short-stack rebuild. My account says uncalled open shoves and reshoves take the stack from 65,000 to about 148,000 before the bubble.
  3. 6:08, 6-4 offsuit against A-K suited. At 13 players with 10 paid, I shove from the small blind, get called, and survive when a four comes. My read about the opponent is not a measured solver output.
  4. 9:00, pocket sevens against A-T. Four-handed at the final table, I shove over the aggressive button's raise. The caller has ace-ten offsuit and my sevens hold.
  5. 10:47, the deal and trophy. The players agree to a four-way deal, I report a $2,900 payment, and the largest stack receives the official winner's trophy.
  6. 11:40, recap and the next-event teaser. The following Survivor tournament is teased, but no second episode is being claimed as published here.

The 6-4 shove: separate the read from the result.

I describe treating the approaching money bubble as a chance to pressure tighter opponents with a short stack. The big blind's A-K suited is an understandable call, and I win the pot by catching a four. The fortunate river or board outcome does not by itself make the shove correct or incorrect.

There are details an independent review would still require, including exact stacks, pot and ante accounting, the big blind's realistic calling range, payout structure and tournament risk premium. In the video I express a belief that this opponent was tighter than a baseline range. That is my table read, not a verified solver finding or a promise that shoving any two cards is generally profitable.

The sevens hand and the final-table deal.

With four players remaining, I describe a button raise to 75,000 at 15,000/30,000/30,000 and a 585,000 effective starting stack for me. I move all-in with pocket sevens and face A-T offsuit. The sevens hold, moving me into the chip lead. These amounts are from my recorded narration, not a separately reconstructed hand history.

At the last break I report about 1.4 million chips at a 60,000 big blind, roughly 23 big blinds. The remaining four players then agree to a deal before another hand is played. My reported $2,900 share and the official trophy are different facts from the displayed $5,100 first-place prize.

For a technical evaluation of the shove, I would need the exact payout schedule, other stacks, positions, blind structure, button opening range and calling response. A single coin-flip result does not establish the best play.

The 10 Big Blind Tournament Playbook

A complete written guide to ten-big-blind tournament decisions. Check the chip counts, compare action branches, reproduce a prize-equity calculation and practice with six answered questions. No video is required.

Published November 19, 2026 · Updated November 21, 2026 · By Nolan Kido · Free study guide

The six checks before a 10 BB decision

  1. Count the chips. Separate live blinds from dead antes. Know the current pot, additional investment, final matched pot, chips behind, and the next blind level.
  2. Identify the players. Record your position, everyone still able to act, who covers whom, and the other stacks that matter to payout pressure.
  3. Name the decision. Are you first in, facing a raise, considering an all-in call, or playing a later street? State the actual legal choices.
  4. Estimate ranges. Use evidence available at the time. Treat unobserved opponent frequencies as assumptions, not facts.
  5. Compare complete choices. Value folds and calls separately for a shove; plan responses and remaining streets when chips stay behind. Compare betting with checking.
  6. Check the objective. Are you maximizing chips, ordinary prize value, satellite-seat value, or bounty returns? Use the payout and eligibility structure of the actual event.

The hand's eventual result is not proof that the earlier decision was correct. Record the original information first, and only then test the proposed adjustment.

First in: 10 BB does not automatically mean shove

One published nine-handed chip-EV example uses a 1 BB big-blind ante and permits three first-in choices from the button: fold, raise to 2 BB, or shove 10 BB. Under that restricted model, A-A raises to 2 BB, 5-5 and A8o shove, and 7-2o folds. The example is not a rule for every ante, position, stack or opponent.

If A-A opens to 2 BB and only the big blind calls, the 0.5 BB folded small blind and dead 1 BB ante remain in the pot: 5.5 BB on the flop, 8 BB behind each player. If the big blind instead reshoves to 10 BB after the small blind folds, calling costs another 8 into a final 21.5 BB pot, requiring approximately 37.21% equity. Plan the response to a shove or a call before choosing the original size.

The button action is only as useful as the model and available branches. A push/fold chart that excludes raises does not disprove a raise in a broader decision tree.

Facing an all-in: count the price, then test the range

Assume A♠J♦ on the button, no ante, and a 10 BB starting stack. You opened to 2 BB. The small blind folded its 0.5 BB and the big blind shoved to 10 BB. You owe 8 BB to make a 20.5 BB final pot. The immediate all-in threshold is 8/20.5, approximately 39.02%.

Reshoving needs both branches

A reshove can win the current pot immediately if all relevant opponents fold or realize a share of the final pot when called. For the supplied A♠K♦ exercise, 99 of 192 opening combinations fold and 93 call. Those are approximately 51.56% folds and 48.44% calls. Equity when called is approximately 52.2%. Weighting the branches produces about +2.2 BB against folding in that specified model. It does not evaluate a flat call or every alternative raise.

EV(shove vs fold) =
  P(fold) × pot already there
  + P(call) × (called equity × final matched pot − extra shove)

When a proposed read changes which hands continue, it can change both how often everyone folds and your equity against the continuing range.

After the flop: the same price can lead to different decisions

Hypothetical no-ante chip-EV hand: you have A♠J♠ on the button, and the flop is K♠7♠2♦. Your 2 BB open was called by the big blind, leaving a 4.5 BB pot and 8 BB behind for each player. The big blind shoves 8 BB. Calling costs 8 BB to contest 20.5 BB, again requiring approximately 39.02% equity.

A smaller call does not purchase the river

If a player instead bets 1.5 BB into 4.5 BB, your 1.5 BB call produces a 7.5 BB pot. The immediate price is 1.5/7.5 = 20%, and both players still have 6.5 BB behind. Further betting may occur. Do not substitute all-in runout equity for the complete value of a non-all-in call.

Follow one river branch at a time

In the written A♥Q♣ example, a button 2 BB raise and big-blind call produce 4.5 BB. On Q♠7♦2♣, the button bets 1.5 BB and is called: 7.5 BB pot, 6.5 BB behind each. Turn 3♥ checks through. On river 9♦, if the big blind shoves 6.5, the button must call 6.5 into 20.5, needing about 31.71% equity. In the separate rewind where the big blind checks, the pot is again 7.5 BB and a 2 BB value bet is a different decision. Compare the expected returns from checking, worse hands calling, and better hands check-raising rather than treating all river lines as one state.

Complete ICM calculation: why a positive chip call can lose prize value

This worked three-player calculation is a separate hypothetical hand, not a solver verdict on the reported 97o fold near a bubble. Verify the model with the offline checker rather than a timestamp.

Three players remain. Prizes are $100, $60, $40. Before posting, stacks are button 5 BB, small blind 20 BB and hero in the big blind 10 BB. There is no ante, bounty, deal or chop; the button folds and the small blind shoves covering hero. Hero must call 9 more BB to complete a 20 BB matched pot. The small blind's uncalled excess is returned. Total chips are 35 BB.

Decision or outcomeHero / SB / BTN stacksHero modeled prize value
Fold9 / 21 / 5$64.000000
Call and win20 / 10 / 5$80.761905
Call and lose0 / 30 / 5$40.000000

Under the standard Independent Chip Model, for hero's chips h, other stacks a and b, and total chips T = h + a + b:

P(first)  = h / T
P(second) = (a / T) × h / (T − a)
          + (b / T) × h / (T − b)
P(third)  = 1 − P(first) − P(second)

Prize value = $100 × P(first)
            + $60 × P(second)
            + $40 × P(third)

With a win-or-lose, no-tie assumption, the call's expected prize value as a function of win probability p is:

V(fold) = $64
V(call) = p × ($1696 / 21) + (1 − p) × $40

Prize break-even p
  = ($64 − $40) / (($1696 / 21) − $40)
  = 63 / 107 ≈ 58.8785%

Chip break-even p = 9 / 20 = 45%

Reproduce the three-player ICM threshold and change one assumption

Download the exact-fraction ICM checker (Python, standard library only) and run it offline with python ten-bb-playbook-icm-verify.py. It independently enumerates all six possible finishing orders for the two surviving three-player states, keeps the $40 third-place payout after a loss, and prints the 45%, 50%, 55% and 60% hypothetical results. It needs no account, solver, network connection or private hand data.

Why the percentage differs: The exact win probability needed to tie folding is 63/107, approximately 58.88%, compared with a 45% chip price, a difference of about 13.88 percentage points in this specific model. A 60% invented win probability crosses both thresholds and earns about $0.46 more modeled prize value than folding; 55% does not. This arithmetic does not establish an opponent range or an actual call decision.

This is not the site's different four-player, three-paid bubble example, which assumes other stacks, prizes and a different ICM threshold of about 56.40%. Do not transfer that number into the three-handed lesson or turn either model into a solver-approved range.

The important distinction: Both examples are profitable in chip EV relative to folding but lose expected prize value under the exact specified payouts and stack distribution. The loss outcome still pays third place, $40, not zero. The 58.88% requirement is not a universal ICM premium, and these win probabilities are teaching assumptions, not measured reads on an actual player. With ties, every tie outcome would need its own resulting chip and prize state.

Six final decisions: complete written answer key

Try each prompt before opening the answer. All six questions have complete written reasoning and can be studied without a video. The first four checks describe how to structure a decision, while these six questions test whether you apply the inputs correctly. They are separate from the three supporting lesson drills.

Question 1: A-A on the button: what is the complete plan?

Nine-handed, 1,000/2,000 blinds, 2,000 big-blind ante paid separately, and 20,000 of live betting capacity per active player. All fold to you with A♣A♠ on the button. The specified chip-EV chart allows fold, raise to 4,000 or shove 20,000. Choose an opening and plan for a big-blind reshove or call.

Show answer and reasoning

In this limited published model, raise to 4,000. If the small blind folds and the big blind reshoves to 20,000, call the remaining 16,000. The final pot is 43,000, and the price is 16,000/43,000, approximately 37.21%. If the big blind instead calls the open, the pot is 11,000 (5.5 BB) and both players have 16,000 (8 BB) behind. Plan the flop rather than betting automatically. This is a model-specific choice, not a universal instruction for aces.

Question 2: The big-blind ante: how many chips can you call?

You start with 10 BB before posting a 1 BB big-blind ante and a 1 BB live blind. A covering small blind shoves. What must you add, and what is the final matched pot?

Show answer and reasoning

Add 8 BB. After the dead 1 BB ante and live 1 BB blind, 8 BB remain behind, making 9 BB of live contribution. The opponent matches 9 BB. The final pot is 9 + 9 + 1 = 19 BB, including the dead ante. The immediate all-in threshold is 8/19, about 42.11%. Do not substitute the different model in which 10 BB remain available after the ante.

Question 3: A-J faces a reshove: did your earlier raise commit you?

Button holds A♠J♦. No ante or payout adjustment. You opened to 2 BB, small blind folded 0.5 BB, and big blind reshoved to 10 BB. You need 8 BB to call a final 20.5 BB pot. Against the supplied 99+, AQs+, AQo+ range, A-J has 29.62% equity.

Show answer and reasoning

Fold against this specified narrow range. The price is 8/20.5, about 39.02%; the supplied equity is only 29.62%. Call EV relative to folding is about 20.5 × 0.2962 − 8 = −1.93 BB. Folding retains the 8 BB you have behind. The earlier 2 BB is already in the pot and does not force another 8 BB investment.

Question 4: The shove wins folds and showdowns: which case is profitable?

A shove risks 9 additional BB to contest a current 3 BB pot. If called, the final pot is 20 BB; the opponent folds 40% of the time. Case A has 30% equity when called. Case B has 45% equity when called. Compare shove EV with folding.

Show answer and reasoning

Only case B is profitable in this supplied two-branch model. Case A: 0.40 × 3 + 0.60 × (20 × 0.30 − 9) = −0.6 BB. Case B: 0.40 × 3 + 0.60 × (20 × 0.45 − 9) = +1.2 BB. Fold frequency remains 40%; equity conditional on a call changes. A real opponent-range adjustment can also alter fold frequency.

Question 5: A flop call costs 20%: does it buy the river?

With A♠J♠ on K♠7♠2♦, the pot is 27,000, each player has 48,000 behind, and the big blind bets 9,000. You would pay 9,000 to make a 45,000 pot.

Show answer and reasoning

The immediate price is 9,000/45,000 = 20%. Calling leaves 39,000 behind for each player. It does not guarantee both the turn and river or establish a profitable call without a range and later-street plan. A later bet can charge again. This is distinct from an all-in runout-equity comparison.

Question 6: Can a positive chip call still lose prize value?

Three remain, payouts $100/$60/$40; initial BTN/SB/BB stacks 5/20/10 BB. No ante or bounty. BTN folds, SB shoves, and BB must add 9 to a final matched pot of 20 BB. Assume 55% to win and no ties.

Show answer and reasoning

Fold under the supplied standard ICM model. Calling earns 0.55 × 20 − 9 = +2 BB relative to folding, but expected prize value is 0.55 × $80.761905 + 0.45 × $40 ≈ $62.42, below the $64 folding value. Losing still pays $40. The 58.88% prize break-even probability is specific to these exact stacks, prizes and assumptions.

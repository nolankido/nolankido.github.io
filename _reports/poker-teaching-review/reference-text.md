<a id="ref-first-decisions-read-cards"></a>

#### [read-cards] 1. Read your two cards without overrating them

Each player receives two private cards, called hole cards. The shared cards form the board: the first three are the flop, the fourth is the turn, and the fifth is the river. A card has a rank, such as an ace or a seven, and a suit: spades, hearts, diamonds or clubs. Aces can be high, or low in an A-2-3-4-5 straight. A pocket pair means your two private cards have the same rank. Suited means they share a suit; offsuit means they do not.

Those labels describe useful features, not a decision by themselves. A pair begins with a made pair. Two nearby ranks can work together in straights. Suited cards can work together in flushes. None of that means every pair, connector or suited hand should be played in every situation.

#### Three different starting shapes

8♠8♦ A pocket pair: two eights.

J♥T♥ Suited connectors: a jack and ten of hearts.

A♠7♦ An ace with a lower, offsuit companion.

<a id="ref-first-decisions-check-suited"></a>

Checkpoint 1: Two hearts in your hand. Do you already have a flush?

No. A flush needs five cards of one suit. Two hearts are a possibility for later, not a completed five-card hand. If the flop contains only one heart, you have three hearts in total, so you still do not have a flush. Count the actual cards instead of letting the label “suited” do the thinking.

If you see AKs, the final “s” means suited; AKo means offsuit. These are groups of hands, not particular suits. In a full card code such as As, however, “s” means spades. This guide shows the suit symbols and gives each displayed card an accessible full name.

<a id="ref-first-decisions-starting-hands"></a>

#### [starting-hands] 2. Ask what makes a starting hand vulnerable

An ace is not a guarantee of a strong hand after the flop. When two players pair the same high card, their other cards can decide the winner. A hand is often called dominated when an opponent shares its higher rank and has a better accompanying card. The weaker hand can improve and still be behind.

#### A pair of aces is not the whole comparison

Your cards: A♠7♦

Other player's cards: A♣K♦

Final board: A♥9♣4♠2♦J♣

<a id="ref-first-decisions-check-kicker"></a>

Checkpoint 2: Both players paired the ace. Is this a split pot?

No. Ace-king wins. Your best five are A-A-J-9-7. The other player's are A-A-K-J-9. The king beats the jack at the first difference. The seven is not automatically compared with the king: write all five cards in order. Nor does this single ending prove how either player should have acted before the flop.

Before committing chips, identify your position, the price, the effective stack and the action before you. Position tells you who acts before and after you; acting later gives you their actions to consider. In a two-player contest, the effective stack is the smaller relevant stack: with 60 and 100 chips, those players can each contest at most 60 from that point. An unopened pot and a raise followed by a caller are different situations, even when your two cards are identical. Folding is an ordinary option, not a failure to participate. Avoid a rule such as “any ace” or “any two suited cards.”

Learn the situation before using a chart. A chart must match the game, stack depth, position and action it describes. The chart-reading guide explains those inputs when you are ready; this lesson does not prescribe a fixed range or bet size.

<a id="ref-first-decisions-legal-actions"></a>

#### [legal-actions] 3. Name the actions available right now

Your choices depend on the current wager, not just your cards. On a new postflop betting round with no bet yet, you can check or bet. Facing a wager, the usual choices are fold, call or raise, when a raise is permitted and your stack allows it. Short all-ins can create exceptions; ask the dealer when the action is unclear.

Three distinctions to say out loud

Action / What it does

Check versus fold / Checking keeps your hand in without adding chips when nothing is owed. Folding gives up your claim to the pot.

Call versus raise / Calling matches the current wager, subject to your stack. Raising increases the wager that others must respond to.

Bet versus raise / A bet opens wagering on a street. A raise increases an existing wager. The big blind is already a live preflop wager.

<a id="ref-first-decisions-check-facing-bet"></a>

Checkpoint 3: Your opponent bets 6 on the flop. Can you check to see the turn?

No. There is now a wager to respond to. With enough chips and your action open, you can fold, call 6 or make a legal raise. Checking is not a free way to stay in against a bet. If you call, another player can still raise before that round is complete.

<a id="ref-first-decisions-check-checked-to"></a>

Checkpoint 4: The flop is checked to you. Must you bet to keep your cards?

No. You can check. If everyone still able to act checks, the betting round ends and the next card is dealt. A check does not fold your hand or establish that you are weak; it only passes this action without betting.

<a id="ref-first-decisions-turn-order"></a>

#### [turn-order] 4. Find your turn, including the big blind's option

The button is a marker identifying the dealer position, even when a staff member deals. The small blind and big blind are forced wagers posted before the cards. The cutoff is the seat immediately before the button in preflop action order. At a table with three or more players, preflop action begins clockwise after the big blind. After the flop, action begins with the first remaining player clockwise after the dealer button. Skip folded players and players who are all-in with no chips left to bet. Do not act simply because the player beside you moved: follow the actual order.

The big blind has already paid a live wager. If other players only call it, the big blind still gets an option to check or raise. The small blind usually has more to add to complete a call. An ante is a separate contribution and does not reduce the amount owed to a bet.

#### An unraised six-player pot

Blinds are 1/2, with no ante or fees. The first two players fold. The cutoff calls 2, the button folds, and the small blind adds 1 to the 1 already posted. The big blind has already posted 2. All players have ample chips.

<a id="ref-first-decisions-check-big-blind"></a>

Checkpoint 5: Does the dealer deal the flop immediately?

No. The big blind must get its turn. Nothing extra is owed to call, so the big blind can check or raise. If it checks, the pot is 6: three contributions of 2. “Everyone matched the blind” is not enough if someone has not had the opportunity to act.

Two-player exception: when the entire table is heads-up, the button is the small blind and acts first preflop, last after the flop. This does not change the positions midway through a hand just because everyone else folded. The position guide develops the difference between a seat and acting after an opponent.

<a id="ref-first-decisions-chips-owed"></a>

#### [chips-owed] 5. Count the new chips, not the number announced

Keep two separate numbers: the current total wager on this street, and what you have already contributed on this street. Their difference is the additional call, unless your remaining stack is smaller. Previous-street contributions are already in the pot and do not pay for a new bet.

<a id="ref-first-decisions-check-call"></a>

Checkpoint 6: You posted the big blind of 2. A player raises to 6. What does a call add?

4 more: 6 total minus your existing 2. Adding 6 would put in too much. If you call, your total preflop contribution is 6. Announcing a raise “to” an amount describes a new total, not how many extra chips that player adds.

<a id="ref-first-decisions-check-new-street"></a>

Checkpoint 7: You paid 6 preflop. On the flop an opponent bets 4. Is your call already covered?

No. Calling costs 4 new chips. The 6 belonged to the earlier round. Your flop contribution starts at zero. Thinking of the pot as one long bill can lead to this mistake; reset the street-specific contribution count when the next betting round starts.

<a id="ref-first-decisions-check-min-raise"></a>

Checkpoint 8: With blinds 1/2, a player raises to 6. Is a reraise to 8 a full legal raise?

No, assuming enough chips and ordinary no-limit rules. The first raise increased the wager from 2 to 6, an increment of 4. The next full raise must add at least 4 to the current 6, making 10 total. It is not enough merely to add another big blind. Short all-ins and reopening action need separate treatment in the betting reference; these examples exclude those exceptions.

At a live table, wait until it is your turn and the amount is clear. State your action clearly before moving chips. Do not experiment with silent chip motions: one oversized chip facing a bet is normally a call under TDA procedures, not automatically a raise. The house or tournament rules govern the actual ruling.

<a id="ref-first-decisions-round-complete"></a>

#### [round-complete] 6. A raise can give you another decision

A betting round is not finished merely because every player has acted once. A later bet or raise can send the action back around. Players who have not folded and are not all-in must respond to the current wager; everyone entitled to act must have had that opportunity. When all but one player have folded, the hand ends without a showdown. A player who is all-in still has a claim to eligible pots and has not folded.

#### Three players on the flop

A checks, B bets 4, C calls 4, and A raises to 12. All three began the street with enough chips to continue. The action goes back to B.

<a id="ref-first-decisions-check-another-turn"></a>

Checkpoint 9: B and C already acted. Can the turn card be dealt now?

No. B faces 8 more to call: 12 minus the 4 already contributed. If B only calls, C then faces the same 8. A new raise would change the amounts and decisions again. A's earlier check did not surrender the right to raise when facing B's bet.

<a id="ref-first-decisions-best-five"></a>

#### [best-five] 7. Build five cards, even when the board does the work

In hold'em, use the best five cards from your two private cards and the five shared cards. You may use two, one or zero private cards. There is no sixth-card tiebreaker, and suits do not break equal hands. This differs from Omaha, which requires exactly two private cards.

#### One private card plays

Your cards: A♠7♦

Board: A♥K♣K♦8♠2♣

<a id="ref-first-decisions-check-one-hole"></a>

Checkpoint 10: Are your best five A-A-K-K-7?

No. They are A-A-K-K-8. The shared eight is a better fifth card than your seven. Only your ace is needed. There is no requirement to include both private cards, and holding a private card does not make it more valuable than the same rank on the board.

#### The board plays

Your cards: A♠3♦ Other player's cards: A♣2♦

Board: Q♠J♥T♦9♣8♥

<a id="ref-first-decisions-check-board"></a>

Checkpoint 11: Does your three beat the other player's two?

No. Both play Q-J-T-9-8 and split the pot. Neither private hand improves the shared queen-high straight. The three and two are outside the best five and cannot break the tie. Odd-chip allocation, if needed, follows the room's rules.

For more comparisons, open four worked showdowns. A useful habit is to say the category first, then list the five cards. “I have an ace” is not a complete description of your final hand.

<a id="ref-first-decisions-first-hand"></a>

#### [first-hand] 8. Put the questions together in one practice hand

Use this scene before looking at any answer. It is a mechanics exercise, not a recommended betting line. Four players use counters with blinds 1/2, no ante and no fees. Everyone starts with 100. The first player folds, the button raises to 6, the small blind folds its posted 1, and the big blind calls.

-

Preflop: Say the additional cost of the big blind's call, then total the pot.

-

Flop, K♠ 8♣ 3♦: The big blind checks, the button bets 4, and the big blind calls. Update the pot.

-

Turn, 7♥: Both check. Does the pot change?

-

River, 2♠: Both check. The button shows K♥ J♦ and the big blind shows K♣ T♦. Compare the best five.

<a id="ref-first-decisions-check-full-hand"></a>

Checkpoint 12: Reveal the complete accounting and showdown

The big blind adds 4 preflop. The pot is 13 = 6 + 6 + 1; the folded small blind's counter stays in. The flop adds 4 from each remaining player, so the pot becomes 21. The turn and river checks add nothing.

The button has K-K-J-8-7; the big blind has K-K-T-8-7. Both private cards play for each player. The button wins with the better kicker. Starting from 100, the button paid 10 and receives 21, finishing with 111. The big blind finishes with 90, the small blind with 99, and the first player with 100. All 400 counters are accounted for.

Winning the 21-counter pot is a gain of 11 for the button, not 21. This result tells you who won this example; it does not establish that each earlier bet or call was a good strategy.

#### [hand-features] 1. Describe a hand's strengths and weaknesses

Preflop means before the first three shared cards appear. Your two private cards have possibilities, not a guaranteed finishing hand. A range is a collection of hands someone could hold or choose to play. Comparing your hand with one imagined opponent hand is different from comparing it with an appropriate range.

Hand features to recognize, not a list of automatic plays

Feature / What to notice

A high pair, such as two kings / Already a pair, with strength against many unpaired hands. Shared cards and opponents' holdings can still produce a better hand.

Two high cards, such as ace-queen / Can make high pairs. When an opponent shares your paired rank, kickers can matter.

Connected cards, such as jack-ten / Can work together in straights. A straight possibility does not guarantee a useful flop or a winning straight.

Suited cards / Can work together in a flush. A low flush can lose to a higher one.

A small pair, such as two fives / Already a pair, but higher shared cards can complicate later decisions. A hoped-for third five is not a plan by itself.

Several features can combine: jack-ten of hearts is both suited and connected. Features can also come with a weakness: ace-seven offsuit can pair the ace yet lose to a better kicker. Use the worked domination example if that comparison is new.

<a id="ref-starting-hand-decisions-check-features"></a>

Exercise 1: Are 9♥ 8♥ and 9♥ 8♣ the same starting hand because the ranks match?

No. Both are connected, but only the first is suited. The two hearts can contribute together to a heart flush. That is a meaningful feature, not an instruction to play either hand in every situation. Neither is already a straight or flush. The rest of this lesson supplies the context a two-card label leaves out.

A helpful beginner habit is to describe both an advantage and a vulnerability before declaring a hand “good.” Do not let a suited label excuse every price, or let one memorable win turn a weak situation into a rule.

<a id="ref-starting-hand-decisions-position"></a>

#### [position] 2. Count who still has a turn

At a six-player table, the initial preflop order is first to act, the next seat, cutoff, button, small blind and big blind. The cutoff is the seat immediately before the button. Some seat labels vary between teaching materials; the number of players and actual action order are the useful facts.

For the two scenes below, everyone starts with 100 big blinds before posting, the blinds are 1/2, and there is no ante. Your cards are jack-ten of hearts in both scenes. Nothing about the cards changed.

-

Scene A: You are first to act. All five other players still have their initial decision.

-

Scene B: You are on the button. The first three players folded. Only the two blinds remain to take their initial decision.

<a id="ref-starting-hand-decisions-check-position"></a>

Exercise 2: Which scene gives you more information before your first decision?

Scene B. You have seen three folds and have two players behind, rather than five. If you reach a flop with either blind, the button acts after that blind in the initial postflop order. This makes the two situations different; it does not establish the best action or raise size for jack-ten suited. A raise can return action to someone who already acted.

Being later in the order gives you other players' actions to consider. It does not reveal their private cards. The big blind acts last in the initial preflop order but usually acts before the button after the flop. “Last preflop” and “in position after the flop” are different descriptions. Heads-up has a special order: the button posts the small blind and acts first preflop, then last postflop. See position and stacks for the longer explanation.

<a id="ref-starting-hand-decisions-earlier-action"></a>

#### [earlier-action] 3. Read what happened before you

An unopened pot has no voluntary call or raise yet: earlier players have folded, or you are first to act. Posting a blind does not count as a voluntary opening raise. A limp is a preflop call of the big blind. An open-raise enters an unopened pot with a raise. Raising after someone has limped is a different situation. A three-bet preflop is a re-raise after that first raise; it does not mean three times the previous amount.

For a concrete sequence with blinds 1/2: someone raises to 6, and another player raises to 18. That second raise is a three-bet. A later raise would be a four-bet. “To 18” means a total contribution of 18 on that street, not 18 added on top of an earlier contribution.

#### Same king-queen, different action

At a six-player table with blinds 1/2 and no ante, you are the cutoff with king-queen of spades. In scene A, the two earlier players fold. In scene B, the first player raises to 6 and the next calls 6. The button and both blinds are still behind you in either scene. All players began with 200 chips.

<a id="ref-starting-hand-decisions-check-action"></a>

Exercise 3: Can one unopened-pot chart answer both king-queen scenes?

No. Scene A is unopened. Scene B already has a raiser and a caller, and calling would cost you 6 because you have posted nothing. You also have three players behind. The earlier actions change the situation and the ranges you would need to consider. A call is not proof of a weak hand, and a raise does not identify one exact strong hand.

Always read the action heading of a reference. “Cutoff, unopened” is not the same question as “cutoff, facing a raise and a call.” Also distinguish opening a pot from defending a blind. A chart cell has meaning inside its stated situation, not everywhere those two ranks appear.

<a id="ref-starting-hand-decisions-price-and-action"></a>

#### [price-and-action] 4. Separate the current price from future costs

The cost of calling is what you must add now to match the current wager, subject to your remaining stack. It is not the total pot, not the opponent's whole stack and not a ticket to see every remaining shared card. Future betting can create new decisions even when your call ends the current round.

In these separate six-player scenes, blinds are 1/2 with no ante or fees. The cutoff raises to 6 and the button folds. The earlier players have folded, and everyone has chips left after matching 6.

Same raise, different blind decisions

You are / Already posted / Add to call / Still to act

Small blind / 1 / 5 / Big blind

Big blind, after the small blind folds / 2 / 4 / A call completes this preflop round

<a id="ref-starting-hand-decisions-check-price"></a>

Exercise 4: Does the small blind's 5-chip call guarantee a flop for that price?

No. The big blind can raise, so the small blind may face another preflop decision. In the separate big-blind scene, adding 4 closes preflop action because the only other remaining player is the original raiser. The folded small blind's 1 stays in the pot, which becomes 13. A completed preflop round still permits betting on later streets.

In that big-blind scene the pot is 9 before your call and 13 after it. The fraction 4/13 is about 30.8%, but it is not a sufficient preflop calling rule: later betting and how the hand reaches showdown matter. The clean river examples in the pot-odds workshop first remove those complications. A small-looking price and a large-looking pot are prompts to calculate, not reasons to stop thinking.

<a id="ref-starting-hand-decisions-stack-depth"></a>

#### [stack-depth] 5. Measure depth in big blinds

A stack's chip count becomes more informative when compared with the big blind. For these examples, a starting stack is measured immediately before posting this hand's blind or ante. State that convention in your notes so you do not mix starting chips with chips remaining after bets.

Starting stack in BB = starting chips / big blind. The small blind is not the denominator, and a separate ante does not change which number is the big blind. If an ante exists, record it separately because it affects the pot and the situation.

<a id="ref-starting-hand-decisions-check-depth"></a>

Exercise 5: You start with 8,000 chips. Is that always a 40-BB stack?

No. At blinds 100/200, 8,000 / 200 = 40 BB. At 200/400, 8,000 / 400 = 20 BB. These are separate snapshots before posting. A tournament blind increase can reduce the stack measured in big blinds without any chips disappearing. A reference for 40 BB does not automatically answer the 20-BB scene.

Shallower stacks leave less room for later bets relative to the blinds. Deeper stacks create more possible future exposure, not an obligation to commit it. Neither depth guarantees safety or profit. Tournament payouts and remaining players can also affect decisions; a cash-game chart should not silently stand in for a tournament decision near a payout boundary.

<a id="ref-starting-hand-decisions-effective-stacks"></a>

#### [effective-stacks] 6. Compare the relevant stacks, not just yours

Between two players, the smaller starting stack limits how many starting chips each can contest against the other in this hand. That is their effective starting stack. When several players remain, record the separate comparisons. A single number can hide an important deeper opponent.

<a id="ref-starting-hand-decisions-check-effective"></a>

Exercise 6: You start with 80 BB, one opponent with 20 BB and another with 120 BB. Is the whole situation “20 BB effective”?

That label is incomplete. You are 20 BB effective against the short stack and 80 BB effective against the deeper stack, using stacks before posting. The short stack cannot win every chip in a side pot formed by the deeper players. Keep both comparisons in your record, then use the side-pot lesson if the accounting is unfamiliar.

“I cover them” means you have at least as many relevant chips, not that those chips are already yours. If you instead report stacks remaining at a later decision, label the street and contributions already made. Do not compare your remaining stack with an opponent's starting stack as though the numbers describe the same moment.

<a id="ref-starting-hand-decisions-future-winnings"></a>

#### [future-winnings] 7. Do not spend imagined future winnings

A small pair may improve to three of a kind, and suited cards may improve to a flush. The possibility of receiving later bets is often discussed as implied odds. The missing word is “if”: if you improve, if your hand is best, and if an opponent contributes more. A deep stack makes more chips available; it does not promise to pay them.

#### Two ceilings on a possible payout

You are the button with two fives and 200 chips before posting. The cutoff raises to 6 at blinds 1/2 with no ante. Compare two separate scenes: the cutoff began with 14 and now has 8 behind, or began with 200 and now has 194 behind. Both blinds still have a turn.

<a id="ref-starting-hand-decisions-check-future"></a>

Exercise 7: If you improve to three fives, can you assume the raiser will pay you another 100?

No in either scene. The short raiser has only 8 left; the deep raiser has 194 but may fold, contribute less, or hold a better eventual hand. The blinds can also change the action. This comparison identifies different ceilings on later contributions, not a profitable call. A third five is an improvement, not a guaranteed pot.

Later costs can move the other way, too. You might make a pair or a low flush, continue paying and lose to a better hand. This is why “I will win a big pot when I hit” needs more than a hopeful ending. Do not substitute a fixed stack multiplier for a full decision without understanding the assumptions behind that shortcut.

<a id="ref-starting-hand-decisions-study-record"></a>

#### [study-record] 8. Build a complete decision before looking for an answer

Use this final fictional scene. Six players are in a fictional no-limit hold'em cash game with blinds 1/2, no ante and no fees. You are the button with ace-jack of spades. Starting stacks before posting: you 150, cutoff 60, small blind 200, big blind 400. The first two players fold. The cutoff raises to 6. It is your turn.

Before opening the answer, write the current pot, your additional call, the players behind, your starting stack in BB and your effective starting stack against each remaining opponent. Then name what you still do not know. You are reconstructing a study problem; the example has no prescribed best action.

<a id="ref-starting-hand-decisions-check-record"></a>

Exercise 8: Reveal the complete preflop study record

- Pot now: 9 chips: the cutoff's 6, small blind's 1 and big blind's 2.

- Your call now: 6. You have posted nothing. If you call, the pot becomes 15 before the blinds respond.

- Players behind: small blind and big blind. A call does not close the action.

- Your starting depth: 150 / 2 = 75 BB.

- Effective starting depths: 30 BB against the cutoff; 75 BB against each blind.

- Known action: cutoff open-raised to 3 BB after two folds. You are on the button with ace-jack suited.

- Unknown: opponents' private cards, their actual ranges, how the blinds will respond and future shared cards. No prior behavioral evidence was supplied.

A useful strategy reference must address this game and action, including the short opener and deeper blinds. If its setup uses equal stacks, a different raise size, fees or an ante, note the mismatch. One attractive ace-jack cell is not enough evidence to transfer its answer here.

The progress is being able to explain why this is a particular decision, instead of asking whether ace-jack is always a good hand. To study choices next, use how to read a preflop chart. To understand what earlier action can and cannot tell you, continue with ranges and reasons for betting.

<a id="ref-starting-hand-decisions-study-template"></a>

#### [study-template] A short template for your next practice hand

Copy these prompts onto paper or into your own private notes. Use invented player labels, and record only information you are entitled to use. This page has no form and stores no answers.

-

Setting: game, cash or tournament, number of players, blinds, ante and known fees.

-

Cards and seat: exact private cards or hand class, your position and players still behind.

The flop gives you three new cards and a new decision. Start by naming what you have, what could change, whose turn it is and what continuing would cost. These eight lessons turn those questions into a repeatable way to study a hand.

For beginners to standard 52-card no-limit Texas hold'em. Every scene is fictional, not Nolan's recorded play. Practice with paper or a deck; no wager, account or purchase is needed. Begin with the basic actions and starting-hand context if those are still unfamiliar.

#### Build the habit

Name the hand and the draw separately. Then ask what the other player could have and what the action actually tells you.

Read your first flop

#### Watch the hand change

A card can improve both players. Compare the best five again instead of carrying the flop's verdict to the river.

Try the changing-winner examples

#### Put it together

<a id="ref-first-flop-decisions-made-and-drawing"></a>

#### [made-and-drawing] 1. You can have a made hand and a draw

A made hand describes what the available cards make now. A draw describes a possible improvement that still needs another card or cards. These labels can both apply. You do not have to choose one box and ignore the other.

When your flop has several opponents, continue with limped and multiway pots. The action order and each opponent’s range become part of the decision.

#### One hand, two useful descriptions

Your cards: A♥T♥

Flop: T♠7♥2♥

<a id="ref-first-flop-decisions-check-pair-and-draw"></a>

Exercise 1: Is this a pair, a flush draw, or both?

Both. You have a pair of tens with an ace kicker, plus four hearts among the five available cards. One more heart would complete a flush. Your made five-card hand now is T-T-A-7-2. Because ten is the highest board rank, this is called top pair. Because your ace is the highest possible accompanying rank, the pair has top kicker.

You also hold the ace of hearts, so a completed heart flush would be ace-high. That does not make you unbeatable: a full house, four of a kind or a straight flush can outrank a flush when the cards permit one. Nor is this already a flush. A useful description names both the current pair and the possible improvement.

“Top pair” compares your pair with the board's ranks. It does not mean the best possible hand. An opponent could already have two pair or three of a kind. See the hand-ranking reference when the categories are unfamiliar.

<a id="ref-first-flop-decisions-whose-turn"></a>

#### [whose-turn] 2. Read the action before selecting a button

In a heads-up pot between the big blind and the button, the big blind acts first after the flop. Imagine you are on the button, you raised before the flop, and the big blind now checks. There is no wager to match on this street.

<a id="ref-first-flop-decisions-check-action"></a>

Exercise 2: Must the preflop raiser bet after the big blind checks?

No. You can check behind or bet. Checking behind completes this heads-up flop round and lets the turn be dealt without another flop payment. Betting gives the other player another decision. You do not “call” when nothing is owed. Having raised preflop does not create a rule requiring a flop bet.

If you bet, the action is called a continuation bet, or c-bet. That name describes the sequence; it does not tell us whether your bet is for value or a bluff. The big blind's check is also not a revealed hand. Strong hands, draws and weak hands can all legally check.

Checking is an active choice. It may preserve a hand's chance to win at showdown while avoiding a new bet, but it also lets another shared card arrive. Whether that tradeoff is useful depends on the situation. A bet can be raised, and a check by the first player does not necessarily end the round when other players still have a turn.

Before studying strategy, say the available actions correctly. Facing a bet, checking is no longer an option; your choices are fold, call or make a permitted raise. Rules for short all-ins can complicate raising rights. The betting guide handles those mechanics.

<a id="ref-first-flop-decisions-bet-purpose"></a>

#### [bet-purpose] 3. Give a bet a purpose beyond “I have a pair”

A value-betting explanation asks which worse hands might call. A bluffing explanation asks which better hands might fold. Before the river, a bet can also make a hand with chances to improve give up those chances. These purposes can overlap. They are questions to investigate, not proof that any chosen size works.

Return to ace-ten of hearts on T♠ 7♥ 2♥. Consider two hypothetical opponent holdings separately: T♣ 9♦ and 7♠ 7♦. These are possible comparisons, not a measured range or a claim that the opponent is equally likely to have each one.

<a id="ref-first-flop-decisions-check-purpose"></a>

Exercise 3: Which hypothetical call supports a value-betting explanation?

A call from ten-nine is the helpful example. Its pair of tens currently trails your pair with the ace kicker. The pocket sevens instead make three sevens, which beat your pair. A value-betting case needs enough calls from worse hands; simply observing that some hand could call is not enough.

We have not established that ten-nine calls a particular size, that sevens always raise, or how often either holding reaches this flop. If all the worse hands fold and only better hands call, “I have top pair” has not justified betting for value. Checking remains an option when no bet is faced.

A draw can be used as a semi-bluff: it may win immediately if opponents fold, or improve if called. That label is not a license to bet every draw. An opponent can continue or raise, the draw can miss, and improvement can still lose. The ranges and betting guide develops these ideas after the basic hand reading feels comfortable.

<a id="ref-first-flop-decisions-straight-completions"></a>

#### [straight-completions] 4. Name the cards a draw actually needs

Instead of saying “I am close to a straight,” point to the missing ranks. In these separate scenes, you hold eight-seven and only your two cards plus the three flop cards are known. Treat the remaining card locations as unknown.

#### Scene A: either end can fill

Your cards: 8♠7♦

Flop: 6♣5♥K♠

Your 5-6-7-8 sequence can become 4-5-6-7-8 or 5-6-7-8-9. This is an open-ended straight draw.

#### Scene B: the missing rank is inside

Keep the same private cards, but replace the six of clubs with the four of clubs. The flop is 4♣5♥K♠

Now 4-5-7-8 needs a six to become 4-5-6-7-8. This is a gutshot, or inside straight draw.

<a id="ref-first-flop-decisions-check-straight"></a>

Exercise 4: Are both scenes eight-card straight draws?

No. Scene A has eight next-card completions: the four fours and four nines. Scene B has four: the four sixes. None of those target cards is among the five known cards in its scene. A nine in scene B leaves a gap, so it does not complete a straight.

These are counts of cards that complete a straight on the next card. They do not guarantee winning at showdown. Other holdings and later shared cards still matter. Count each physical card once; a card that also completes another draw does not become two cards.

The draws and overlap examples show how to combine straight and flush possibilities. Do not assume that two hands described as “draws” have the same chances or should face the same price.

<a id="ref-first-flop-decisions-shared-improvement"></a>

#### [shared-improvement] 5. A helpful card can help the other player more

For this comparison, turn both private hands face up. You hold queen-jack of hearts; the other player holds two eights. This is an open-card teaching scene. In an ordinary hand, you do not get to see the opponent's private cards while deciding.

#### The heart that also pairs the board

Your cards: Q♥J♥ Opponent's cards: 8♠8♦

Flop: K♥8♥3♣

Turn: 3♥

<a id="ref-first-flop-decisions-check-shared"></a>

Exercise 5: The heart arrived. Are you now ahead?

No. Your flush loses to a full house. Your best five are the hearts K-Q-J-8-3. The other player's best five are 8-8-8-3-3: three eights and the board's two threes. The turn completed your flush and simultaneously improved the opponent's three eights to a full house.

This is why “nine cards make my flush” differs from “nine cards make me win.” A card counted as a useful improvement under one assumption may fail against another holding. Re-read the entire board and compare five-card categories before celebrating the draw's label.

Notice that the three of hearts is a single shared card doing two jobs. It supplies your fifth heart and pairs the board for both players. Looking only at its suit would miss half the information. This example compares current hands after the turn; it does not calculate river equity or prescribe a call.

<a id="ref-first-flop-decisions-counterfeited"></a>

#### [counterfeited] 6. Rebuild two pair when the board pairs

Your private pairings do not reserve places in the final five. Shared cards can create a higher pair for everyone and replace a lower pair you were relying on. Players call this kind of loss of usefulness counterfeiting.

#### Two pair before and after, but a different comparison

Your cards: 9♠4♦ Opponent's cards: Q♥Q♦

Flop: 9♥4♣K♠

Turn and river: K♦2♥

<a id="ref-first-flop-decisions-check-counterfeit"></a>

Exercise 6: Do your flopped nines and fours still beat the queens?

No. On the flop, your two pair beats one pair of queens. On the final board your best five are K-K-9-9-4. The other player's best five are K-K-Q-Q-9. Both now have two pair, but queens beat nines as the lower pair, so the opponent wins.

Your pair of fours no longer fits alongside the higher kings and nines. The four remains only a kicker in your best five. The king on the turn already changed the lead; the river two does not restore your earlier advantage. Always rebuild the hand instead of protecting its old name.

A paired board does not automatically mean you have lost. This result follows from the two shown holdings. With hidden cards, you would need a plausible account of the opponent's range and actions. A possible stronger hand is not proof that the opponent has it.

<a id="ref-first-flop-decisions-paying-again"></a>

#### [paying-again] 7. A flop call can lead to another bill

Use this separate fictional heads-up cash-game snapshot, with no fees. There are 18 chips in the pot and each player has 50 behind at the start of the flop. The opponent bets 6. You call 6. The pot becomes 30, and both players have 44 remaining.

The turn arrives. The opponent bets 15. Earlier contributions are already in the pot; they do not count as credit toward this new street's wager.

<a id="ref-first-flop-decisions-check-second-payment"></a>

Exercise 7: Did the first call already pay for the river?

No. Calling again costs another 15. The pot is 45 when the turn decision reaches you and would become 60 after a call. Both players would then have 29 remaining. You added 6 on the flop and would add another 15 on the turn, for 21 across these two decisions.

You are allowed to fold when facing this new bet. Having paid the earlier 6 is not an obligation to pay 15 now. This ledger shows costs, not the best action: no private cards or opponent range were supplied. A river bet could create yet another decision.

A probability of improving by the river assumes access to both remaining cards. It cannot quietly replace a next-card probability when another payment may be needed on the turn. Completing a draw also differs from winning the pot. Use the card-probability table for that distinction and the pot-odds workshop for the conditional calculations.

Earlier chips are already committed. Review the new price and your current information. Do not continue simply to make a previous call feel worthwhile, and do not treat a lost hand as money that must be recovered in the next one.

<a id="ref-first-flop-decisions-decision-snapshot"></a>

#### [decision-snapshot] 8. Put the hand, action and price together

Return to ace-ten of hearts on T♠ 7♥ 2♥. You are on the button against the big blind in a fictional cash-game hand with no fees. The pot is 13 before flop action, and each player has 94 behind. The big blind checks, you bet 4, and the big blind raises to 12. It is your turn.

Before revealing the answer, record: your made hand and draw; the current pot; the extra amount a call costs; whether checking is available; the pot and remaining stacks after calling; and what the raise does not tell you.

<a id="ref-first-flop-decisions-check-snapshot"></a>

Exercise 8: Reveal the complete decision snapshot

- Hand: pair of tens, ace kicker, plus a heart-flush draw. No completed flush.

- Pot now: 13 + 4 + 12 = 29. The opponent has put in 12 total on this street, not 16.

- Your additional call: 12 - 4 = 8.

- Available actions: fold, call 8 or make a legal raise. Checking is unavailable because you face an unmatched wager.

- If you call: the pot becomes 37. Each player has put in 12 on the flop and has 82 remaining. This heads-up call closes the flop round.

- Still unknown: the opponent's private cards, actual raising range, future cards and later betting. A check-raise is an action sequence, not proof of a set.

The fraction 8 / 37 is about 21.6%. It is a price calculation, not a verdict that the call is correct. Applying it as a simple equity threshold requires no further betting or fees; this hand can face future bets. The scene deliberately gives no universally correct action or validated solver answer.

A useful study note ends with a question you can investigate: “Which hands might raise here, how would they respond if I raise again, and how can later cards change this decision?” Keep observed facts separate from guessed frequencies. If a later showdown reveals a bluff or a strong hand, it adds evidence about that one hand; it does not retroactively make the hidden cards knowable.

The turn leaves one card to come. The river leaves none. That small change transforms the questions worth asking: what can still improve, what already wins at showdown, and what a bet or call could actually accomplish.

For beginners to standard 52-card no-limit Texas hold'em. Every scene is fictional, not Nolan's recorded play. Practice with a deck or a private note; no wager, account or purchase is needed. This lesson follows your first decisions after the flop. It teaches interpretation, not a recommended betting strategy.

Start with the turn

#### Remember shared pots

#### [turn-reset] 1. Re-read the hand when the turn arrives

The turn is the fourth community card. There is one more shared card to come if the hand continues. Before thinking about another bet, separate three observations: your best hand now, the improvement you are still drawing to, and the hands the new board makes possible for someone else.

#### The king changes the description

Your cards: Q♥J♥

Flop: J♠7♥2♥

Turn: K♣

On the flop you had a pair of jacks with a queen kicker, plus a heart-flush draw. Jack was the highest board rank, so the pair was top pair. Now say what is different without guessing the opponent's cards.

<a id="ref-turn-and-river-decisions-check-turn"></a>

Exercise 1: Did the king erase your pair or complete your flush?

Neither. You still have a pair of jacks. Your best five on the turn are J-J-K-Q-7. The king is now the highest board rank, so your jacks are second pair rather than top pair. There are still only four hearts among your cards and the board; the flush is unfinished.

The king makes a pair of kings possible for an opponent holding a king. It does not reveal that the opponent has one. Your draw still has a chance to complete on the river, but the new board, the action and the price all matter. “An overcard arrived” describes a change, not an automatic instruction to fold.

An overcard here means a card ranked above the jacks you paired. Your pair's category did not disappear when its position on the board changed. Conversely, retaining a pair does not guarantee you remain ahead. Naming the actual five cards helps avoid both mistakes.

<a id="ref-turn-and-river-decisions-river-reset"></a>

#### [river-reset] 2. A missed draw can leave a made hand

#### Continue the same runout

River: 8♦

The complete board is J♠ 7♥ 2♥ K♣ 8♦. Keep your Q♥ J♥.

The river is the fifth and final community card. Your heart draw did not arrive. There is no sixth shared card waiting after the next call. Start the river description with the hand you actually have, rather than only the draw you wanted.

<a id="ref-turn-and-river-decisions-check-river"></a>

Exercise 2: Does missing the flush mean you have nothing?

No. You still have a pair of jacks. Your best five are J-J-K-Q-8. The river eight replaces the seven among your five best cards. You have no completed flush, and no remaining opportunity to draw to one in this hand.

Compare two hypothetical opponents separately. Against A♠ Q♣, which makes A-K-Q-J-8, your pair wins. Against K♠ T♦, which makes K-K-J-T-8, your pair loses. These comparisons show possibilities, not a claim that either holding is likely or that calling a bet is justified.

Showdown value means a hand has some ability to win or share the pot at showdown without needing the opponent to fold. The missed heart draw and the surviving pair describe different features. Do not discard the second just because the first failed.

Even on the river, the outcome may be unknown to you because the opponent's private cards are hidden. That uncertainty is about the existing hands, not a chance of being dealt a helpful sixth card. More betting can still happen; more community cards cannot in this ordinary single-board hand.

<a id="ref-turn-and-river-decisions-checking-behind"></a>

#### [checking-behind] 3. Checking behind on the river does not give a free card

Use the same final board and your pair of jacks. You are on the button, only the big blind remains against you, both have chips, and the big blind checks on the river. You act last. You can check behind or bet.

On an earlier street, checking behind can allow the next shared card to arrive without another payment on that street. On the river, no later shared card remains. The reason “I must bet to stop the flush draw getting there” no longer fits.

<a id="ref-turn-and-river-decisions-check-check"></a>

Exercise 3: If you check behind, can the big blind bet again on this river?

No, in this heads-up sequence. The big blind checked and you checked behind. The final betting round is complete, so the hand reaches showdown. Checking keeps your hand eligible to win; it does not fold your pair. It also does not guarantee that your pair is best.

If you bet instead, the big blind gets a response and may fold, call or make a legal raise. Betting is not simply checking plus extra winnings. It creates another decision for the opponent and can create one for you. That distinction matters even when you act last initially.

A value-betting explanation would name worse hands that might call, such as a weaker pair, and justify why enough of them continue. A bluffing explanation would name better hands that might fold. The card comparison alone establishes neither response. You do not have to turn every hand with uncertain strength into a bet.

Order matters: if you check first with another eligible player still to act, your check does not close the river. That player can bet, bringing the decision back to you. “Checking reaches showdown” is true only when the required action is complete. Follow the dealer's procedures for showing cards; these examples do not replace house rules.

<a id="ref-turn-and-river-decisions-ace-high"></a>

#### [ace-high] 4. Even an unpaired missed draw can win

Start a separate open-card comparison. The final board is already dealt, and both players check the river. The two private hands are exposed only to explain the showdown. They are not information either player possessed while choosing an action.

#### Two missed heart draws, different high cards

Your cards: A♥K♥

Opponent's cards: J♥T♥

Final board: Q♥7♥2♣8♦3♠

<a id="ref-turn-and-river-decisions-check-ace-high"></a>

Exercise 4: With no pair or flush, must you lose or split?

Neither. Your ace-high hand wins this comparison. Your best five are A-K-Q-8-7. The opponent's are Q-J-T-8-7. Both hands are in the high-card category, and the ace wins at the first comparison. The opponent does not have a straight: Q-J-T-8-7 has a missing nine.

Both heart draws missed. That does not make the hands equally strong or remove all showdown value. It also does not mean ace-high beats an arbitrary river betting range. Here we know the exact opposing cards and that the betting ended with checks; those are teaching conditions, not a model of every opponent.

“I missed, so I have to bluff” skips the comparison with checking. A bet could make a better hand fold, but a hand that could win by checking may also get called only by stronger hands. Naming a possible bluff is not evidence that bluffing this size against this opponent would improve your result.

On the river, the draw cannot improve after a call. A bet intended to make better hands fold is a bluff, not a semi-bluff that may later hit its draw. A hand can still have some showdown value while being used as a bluff. The label describes the bet's intended effect; it does not certify the decision.

<a id="ref-turn-and-river-decisions-catching-a-bluff"></a>

#### [catching-a-bluff] 5. Calling a bluff is not making a bluff

Return to Q♥ J♥ on J♠ 7♥ 2♥ K♣ 8♦. Change the river action: in this fictional heads-up cash-game snapshot there are 24 chips in the pot, and each player has 60 behind before river betting. The big blind bets 12. You are on the button and must respond.

The current pot is 36. A call adds 12, making a final pot of 48. Each player would have 48 left behind. A call closes this heads-up river action. There are no fees, side pots or tournament-prize effects in this example.

<a id="ref-turn-and-river-decisions-check-call"></a>

Exercise 5: Can you call 12 as a bluff to make the bettor fold?

No. A call does not ask the bettor to fold. It matches the wager and takes this hand to showdown. You can win if your existing five-card hand beats the opponent's, or receive a share if the hands tie. You cannot improve by receiving another community card.

A bluff catcher is a hand whose role, against a specified betting range, is to beat its bluffs while losing to its value hands. Calling with it attempts to catch a bluff. It is not itself a bluff. Your pair is not automatically a bluff catcher against every possible range: a weaker made hand might also bet.

The break-even equity for calling versus folding in this closed-action, no-fee chip model is 12 / 48 = 25%. That is the required average share of the final pot, not an estimate of your actual share against the opponent. We have supplied no credible frequencies for their hands.

You may fold, call or make a legal raise with the stated stacks. The arithmetic above compares calling with folding only; it does not show that raising is bad. A raise creates a new wager and asks the opponent to respond, which a call does not. Checking is unavailable when facing this bet.

A complete study answer names the price and the missing evidence: “Calling costs 12; I need a credible account of which hands bet and how often.” Do not replace that evidence with “they could be bluffing.” Finding one possible bluff proves possibility, not frequency. The river Decision Lab develops that distinction with explicit range weights.

<a id="ref-turn-and-river-decisions-shared-pot"></a>

#### [shared-pot] 6. No outright wins can still mean a share of the pot

Use a final, separate heads-up river example. Your cards are two of clubs and three of diamonds. The board has the highest possible straight, with no suit appearing more than twice. Both players are still eligible for the same pot.

#### The board supplies all five cards

Your cards: 2♣3♦

Final board: A♥K♦Q♣J♠T♥

There are 24 chips before the opponent bets 12. Both players had 60 behind at the start of the river. Suppose your call closes the action. No fees are taken, and there are no other players or side pots. Compare calling with folding using chips only.

<a id="ref-turn-and-river-decisions-check-share"></a>

Exercise 6: With zero chance to win outright, is calling worth zero?

No. You are guaranteed half of this final pot under the stated conditions. Every legal opposing hand uses the same A-K-Q-J-T straight. No higher straight exists. Two private cards cannot make a flush on this board or combine with its five distinct ranks to make a full house or four of a kind. Pairs and three of a kind rank below the straight. Neither player's private kicker can improve or break the tie.

Calling adds 12 and makes 48. Your half is 24, including the 12 you just paid. Relative to folding now, the call therefore gains 24 - 12 = 12 chips. Your equity is 50% even though your probability of winning the whole pot alone is zero.

This is not the full hand's profit: earlier contributions are outside the snapshot. Actual deductions can change a split pot's accounting. The example illustrates equity as pot share, not a universal instruction to call any bet on any board that looks coordinated.

For a heads-up river model with wins, losses and equal splits, equity is the chance of winning outright plus half the chance of tying. On this board the first term is zero and the second is half of 100%. Other pot structures require their own accounting. The pot-odds workshop explains the general comparison.

## ranges-for-beginners

“They might have aces” names one possibility. It does not explain what else the opponent could hold, which hands your cards beat, or how often each hand takes this action. A range is a way to make those missing parts visible.

<a id="ref-ranges-for-beginners-known-and-possible"></a>

#### [known-and-possible] 1. A range is an account of possibilities

A range describes a collection of possible private hands. In a useful decision model it also describes their relative likelihoods. You can begin with a short list in words; you do not need a colored chart or software before you understand the idea.

Keep three layers separate. Known cards are your private cards and the shared board, plus any other genuinely exposed cards. Possible holdings are hands that do not reuse those cards. A proposed betting range is a model of which possible holdings reach this decision and choose this action. That last layer needs assumptions or evidence.

#### One river snapshot for the lesson

Your cards: K♥Q♦

Final board: K♠9♣5♥3♦2♣

Only one opponent remains. Your best five are K-K-Q-9-5: a pair of kings with a queen kicker. No further community card will arrive.

Imagine the opponent checks. That action is something you observed. “The opponent has a weak hand” is an interpretation. A player can legally check a strong hand, a medium hand or a hand that is unlikely to win. The rules do not turn a check into an exposed card.

<a id="ref-ranges-for-beginners-check-check"></a>

Exercise 1: Does the check prove that your pair of kings is ahead?

No. The opponent could hold ace of clubs and ace of diamonds, making a pair of aces that beats your kings. They could instead hold queen of hearts and jack of hearts, making a high-card hand that loses to your pair. Both are physically possible on this board, and both can check.

This does not claim the two hands are equally likely. It shows why a check alone cannot prove that you lead. A useful review says which hands you consider and why they might check, rather than translating every check into “weak.” When their cards are hidden, your explanation should preserve that uncertainty.

A range is not a special ability to see hidden cards. It is a model you can examine and revise. Even a carefully reasoned range can be incomplete or wrong. Naming the assumption makes an explanation more useful than presenting a confident guess as a fact.

<a id="ref-ranges-for-beginners-remove-known-cards"></a>

#### [remove-known-cards] 2. Remove impossible cards before judging plausibility

Return to your king of hearts and queen of diamonds on K♠ 9♣ 5♥ 3♦ 2♣. The king of spades is already on the board, and the king of hearts is in your hand. The opponent cannot hold either card. Those are physical exclusions, independent of how that opponent tends to play.

Consider four candidate holdings separately: K♠ A♠, K♥ J♦, A♣ A♦ and Q♥ J♥. A card code names an exact rank and suit: K♠ is the king of spades, not any king. In written study notes, the same card might be written Ks.

<a id="ref-ranges-for-beginners-check-remove"></a>

Exercise 2: Which two candidate holdings must be crossed out?

K♠ A♠ and K♥ J♦. The first reuses the board's king of spades. The second reuses your king of hearts. Neither is a legal opposing holding in this snapshot. A♣ A♦ and Q♥ J♥ reuse none of the known cards, so both remain physically possible.

Do not go further and call the remaining hands equally likely. Removing an impossible card is certain; deciding how often someone arrives with aces or bets queen-jack is a different question. Nor should you remove a card because you suspect a folded player had it. A guess about an unseen folded hand is not an additional known card.

Your known cards are often called blockers because they remove combinations from other players' possibilities. The label does not automatically favor your decision: a card can remove a strong opposing hand or remove a hand you hoped the opponent was bluffing with. First state what is removed; investigate the strategic consequence separately.

<a id="ref-ranges-for-beginners-four-holdings"></a>

#### [four-holdings] 3. Compare four exact holdings, not four broad labels

Now use only the four holdings below as a deliberately small teaching model. Each row names one exact two-card combination. These are alternatives for the same opponent, not eight cards that opponent holds at once. Cards may appear in more than one alternative in a general range, but no individual holding may duplicate a known card.

#### A: one exact pair of aces

A♣A♦

#### B: one exact ace-king

K♣A♥

#### C: one exact pair of nines

9♥9♦

#### D: one exact queen-jack

Q♥J♥

Keep your K♥ Q♦ and the same complete board beside the four rows. Write the best five cards for each alternative before opening the answer. Saying “I have top pair” is not enough to compare it with all four.

<a id="ref-ranges-for-beginners-check-compare"></a>

Exercise 3: Which of the four exact holdings does your hand beat?

Only D, queen-jack of hearts. Your K-K-Q-9-5 loses to A's A-A-K-9-5, B's K-K-A-9-5 and C's 9-9-9-K-5. It beats D's K-Q-J-9-5. There are no ties in these four comparisons.

Aces are a higher pair than kings. Ace-king shares your pair of kings and wins on the first different kicker, the ace. The nines make three of a kind. Queen-jack has only a high-card hand on this board. The number of listed hands you beat is one; the probability of facing that hand has not yet been supplied.

Do not confuse these four exact holdings with the labels AA, AK, 99 and QJ. A label can represent several physical combinations. Before any cards are removed, a pocket-pair label contains six combinations; an unpaired label containing both suited and offsuit versions contains sixteen. This exercise intentionally starts smaller. The combination workshop teaches group counts after the distinction feels comfortable.

<a id="ref-ranges-for-beginners-equal-weights"></a>

#### [equal-weights] 4. Equal chances are an explicit assumption

Change the action to a bet. In a fictional heads-up cash-game river decision there are 40 chips before the opponent bets 20. Both players began the river with 100 behind. You face 20 with enough chips to call. A call closes betting. Assume no fees, side pots or tournament-prize effects.

The current pot is 60. Calling adds 20 and makes 80; both players then have 80 remaining. The simple break-even share is 20 / 80 = 25%. This prices calling relative to folding now. It does not estimate your hand's equity or compare calling with a legal raise.

For this calculation only, suppose our opponent has exactly A, B, C or D at this betting decision, each with the same chance. No other holding is included. The equal likelihood applies after the observed action; we are not measuring how a real player arrives here. In this model your hand loses to A, B and C and wins against D.

<a id="ref-ranges-for-beginners-check-equal"></a>

Exercise 4: Is calling profitable if the four alternatives are equally likely?

It is exactly break-even relative to folding under these assumptions. You win one time out of four and never tie, giving 25% equity against this invented betting range. Receiving the final 80 one quarter of the time and paying 20 each time gives 0.25 × 80 - 20 = 0.

Equivalently, a win gains the current 60 and a loss costs the additional 20: 0.25 × 60 - 0.75 × 20 = 0. Earlier contributions are already in the pot. This says nothing about full-hand profit. Most importantly, listing four hands did not justify assigning equal chances to them. That was a supplied teaching assumption, not a conclusion about an opponent.

Here the three stronger alternatives can be treated as the model's value candidates, and D as its bluff candidate. Those labels describe this restricted betting model. A weaker made hand could bet in another model, and a real opponent can have hands we left out. Your kings are not automatically a pure bluff catcher against every possible betting range.

<a id="ref-ranges-for-beginners-unequal-weights"></a>

#### [unequal-weights] 5. A possible bluff can appear less often

This optional step introduces a weight: a relative amount of probability assigned to a hand. Keep the same cards, same bet and same price. Assign weight 1 to each of A, B and C, but weight 0.5 to D. Those values describe the relative likelihood of the four alternatives at this betting decision, including the action history.

The weights total 3.5: 1 + 1 + 1 + 0.5. The share belonging to D is therefore 0.5 / 3.5 = 1 / 7, about 14.3%. A half-weight hand is not half of a physical card. It is a way to say that this alternative appears half as often as each full-weight alternative in the constructed model.

<a id="ref-ranges-for-beginners-check-weight"></a>

Exercise 5: Do you still have 25% equity because the same four hands remain on the list?

No. You now have 1 / 7 equity, about 14.3%, with no ties. D is still the only hand you beat, but it has less weight. The 25% call price did not change. In this revised chip model, the value of calling relative to folding is (1 / 7) × 80 - 20 = -60 / 7, about -8.57 chips.

This does not establish the opponent's actual bluff rate or the best action among every legal choice. It demonstrates sensitivity to one assumption. If a weight already includes reaching the river and betting, do not multiply the same action history into it again. For a more detailed construction, continue to the range-combination workshop.

Do not quietly turn “could be a bluff” into “is as common as every value hand.” Conversely, removing every bluff without evidence is also an assumption. When you cannot justify frequencies, preserve that limitation. A precise calculation conditional on uncertain inputs remains uncertain as a description of the real decision.

<a id="ref-ranges-for-beginners-one-reveal"></a>

#### [one-reveal] 6. One revealed hand does not validate the whole range

Imagine the opponent eventually shows D, queen-jack of hearts. That reveals what happened in this one fictional hand. It does not reveal all the hands the opponent could have arrived with, how often they bet them, or which alternative model best described your earlier information.

<a id="ref-ranges-for-beginners-check-reveal"></a>

Exercise 6: Does seeing queen-jack prove the equal-weight model was correct?

No. Both the equal-weight and half-weight models allow queen-jack. A single revealed result does not distinguish their frequencies. It also does not prove that your earlier call was good merely because it won, or that every future bet should be called.

Record the actual board, action and shown cards without adding a story you did not observe. Then ask a narrower study question: which other hands could take this line, and what evidence would change their relative weights? Keep other players' identifying information out of public notes. A useful review preserves what was unknown at the time instead of rewriting the decision around its ending.

#### [next-step] Leave with a range question you can explain

Close the answers. Explain, in order: the known cards; the holdings those cards exclude; which remaining hands beat or lose to yours; the price of continuing; and the assumptions about how often each alternative appears. You can use the four exact holdings again without pretending they are a realistic complete range.

Keep a private study note with two columns, “Observed” and “Assumed.” Put the board, bet and amount to call in the first. Put the candidate range and frequencies in the second. Add one sentence about what would change your conclusion. There is no answer form here and no practice response is stored.

- If the five-card comparison was difficult, revisit kickers and ties.

- If the 20-chip call was unclear, revisit river calls.

- If exact holdings and labels became mixed together, use the combination workshop.

“I have a good hand” describes your cards. “I want a worse hand to call this amount” explains a reason to bet. Learning the difference makes both your own study notes and a commentator's explanation easier to question.

For beginners to standard 52-card no-limit Texas hold'em. All scenes and opponent responses here are fictional, not Nolan's recorded play or measured player tendencies. Use a deck, counters or paper; no wager, account or purchase is needed. If comparing several possible hands is unfamiliar, read your first poker range first.

#### Start with the purpose

Ask what you want an opponent to do. Checking is an option to compare, not a failure to make a decision.

#### Try the small model

#### Explain it without numbers

<a id="ref-betting-with-a-purpose-purpose"></a>

#### [purpose] 1. Name the response you want

A value bet aims to get called by a worse hand. A bluff aims to make a better hand fold. These describe an intention relative to the opponent's possible hands, not just the strength of your own two cards. The same pair can be a reasonable value candidate in one assumed situation and an unhelpful bet in another.

When no bet is facing you, checking keeps you in the hand without adding chips. Acting last on the river after the opponent checks, your check completes betting and takes the hand to showdown. Checking earlier in the order does not prevent another player from betting. All river comparisons below put you last to act, heads-up, after a check.

<a id="ref-betting-with-a-purpose-check-purpose"></a>

Exercise 1: “I want to see where I am.” What is missing from that reason to bet?

Which hands will respond, and why that response helps. A call does not reveal one exact hand; a raise does not prove that you are beaten. Paying for a reaction can grow the pot without answering your original question. Rewrite the reason as “I think these worse hands can call” or “I think these better hands can fold,” and state what supports that belief.

A bet may produce information, but information alone does not establish that betting is better than checking. If you cannot name a useful response, preserve the uncertainty and compare the check. This is a review prompt, not a rule that you must always check when unsure.

<a id="ref-betting-with-a-purpose-value"></a>

#### [value] 2. A worse hand must actually call

#### One board, four alternative opponents

You hold queen of hearts and jack of diamonds:

Q♥J♦

The complete board is queen of spades, eight of clubs, five of hearts, three of diamonds and two of clubs:

Q♠8♣5♥3♦2♣

Your best five are Q-Q-J-8-5. The pot is 40 before your decision. Both players have 100 behind, with no fees, side pots or tournament payouts. The opponent has checked. A check by you ends betting. For a bet, the teaching model allows only a fold or call, with no raises.

The four rows below are alternative exact holdings for one opponent, not eight cards held together. For this exercise only, give each alternative equal probability at this decision. That is a supplied assumption, not something established by listing four hands. In real play you do not see these hidden cards.

Fictional responses to your river bet of 10

Exact holding / Compared with yours / Assumed response

Q♣ T♦: queen of clubs, ten of diamonds / Worse pair of queens / Call

9♥ 9♦: nine of hearts, nine of diamonds / Worse pair of nines / Call

8♥ 8♦: eight of hearts, eight of diamonds / Better three of a kind / Call

A♣ K♦: ace of clubs, king of diamonds / Worse ace-high hand / Fold

<a id="ref-betting-with-a-purpose-check-targets"></a>

Exercise 2: Which two holdings are the targets of this value bet?

Q♣ T♦ and 9♥ 9♦. Your Q-Q-J-8-5 beats queen-ten's Q-Q-T-8-5 and the nines' 9-9-Q-8-5. The model says these worse hands call. The eights make 8-8-8-Q-5 and beat you. Ace-king makes A-K-Q-8-5 and loses, but its assumed fold adds no opposing chips.

You win a showdown against three of the four alternatives, yet only two supply a worse call. Counting every hand you beat as a paying customer would confuse hand strength with betting value. The stronger hand calling is a cost you must include, not evidence by itself that the value-bet intention was mistaken.

<a id="ref-betting-with-a-purpose-check-compare"></a>

Exercise 3, optional arithmetic: Is betting 10 better than checking in this restricted model?

Yes, by 2.5 chips on average under the supplied responses. Measure chips received minus new chips paid from this decision onward. Earlier contributions are already in the 40-chip pot. Checking wins 40 against three alternatives and receives 0 against the eights: (40 + 40 + 0 + 40) / 4 = 30.

With a bet of 10, a winning call returns a 60-chip pot after you paid 10, giving 50. A losing call costs 10. A fold returns the 50-chip pot including your own 10, giving 40, not 50. The average is (50 + 50 - 10 + 40) / 4 = 32.5. The difference from checking is 32.5 - 30 = 2.5.

These are decision-point values, not full-hand profits. The comparison covers a check and this one bet in this intentionally restricted model. It does not prove 10 is an optimal size or that an actual opponent supplies these responses.

Now change only one assumption: both worse pairs fold to the same bet of 10. The eights still call and ace-king still folds. The cards, pot and equal starting weights stay the same.

<a id="ref-betting-with-a-purpose-check-folds"></a>

Exercise 4: You still beat three hands. Does that preserve the benefit of betting?

No. Checking now has the higher value in this model. Three folds each return the existing 40 net of your own returned bet, while the only call loses 10: (40 + 40 - 10 + 40) / 4 = 27.5. Checking still averages 30. Betting is 2.5 chips worse than checking, even though it wins the pot in three of the four alternatives.

The useful lesson without arithmetic is that a bet which drives out all the worse hands and receives only a better call can damage your result. “I usually have the best hand” is not a complete value-betting argument. Which hands continue, and how often, can reverse the comparison.

<a id="ref-betting-with-a-purpose-size"></a>

#### [size] 3. A larger bet can lose its customers

Return to the first response model. Consider a bet of 20 into the same 40-chip pot. For this new size, suppose both worse pairs fold, the eights call and ace-king folds. We changed the opponent's responses with the price; we did not prove they must behave this way.

<a id="ref-betting-with-a-purpose-check-size"></a>

Exercise 5: Does twice the bet automatically mean twice the value?

No. In this supplied 20-chip model, three folds return 40 each and the losing call costs 20. The average is (40 + 40 - 20 + 40) / 4 = 25. That is less than the check's 30 and the first model's 10-chip bet value of 32.5.

This does not establish that small bets are always better. If worse hands continued to call the larger size, the comparison would change again. State the bet size and the assumed responses together. A useful study note asks which hands keep calling as the price increases, instead of scaling the expected reward while pretending every other input stays fixed.

Raising a pot after a fold can look successful on video. Success at making someone fold is different from getting the most value from a hand. Likewise, losing after a value bet does not automatically invalidate its earlier reasoning. Review the information and assumptions available before the cards were exposed.

<a id="ref-betting-with-a-purpose-bluff"></a>

#### [bluff] 4. A bluff needs better hands to fold

#### A different fictional river

You have J♥ T♥, jack-ten of hearts, on K♠ 9♣ 5♥ 3♦ 2♣. The opponent's alternative in this model is Q♥ Q♣. Your K-J-T-9-5 loses to their Q-Q-K-9-5. There are no ties or cards still to come.

The pot is 30, both players have 100 behind and the opponent checks to you last to act. The model excludes fees, side pots and raises. Checking loses at showdown. Suppose a bet of 10 makes this better hand fold one quarter of the time and call three quarters of the time. That frequency is supplied for teaching, not measured.

<a id="ref-betting-with-a-purpose-check-bluff"></a>

Exercise 6: What does the bluff gain on a fold, and what does it risk on a call?

A fold gains the existing 30; a losing call costs your new 10. Your own bet is returned inside the pot when the opponent folds, so the gain is not 40. At the assumed frequencies, the average is (1 / 4) × 30 - (3 / 4) × 10 = 0. Checking also gives 0 from this decision because your hand always loses this restricted showdown.

For this zero-showdown-value bluff, break-even folds are 10 / (30 + 10) = 25%. This is the required fold frequency, not your hand's equity, the opponent's call price or the frequency with which you should bluff. Less folding makes this bet worse than checking; more makes it better within the model.

Do not carry the 25% to a different size. A 30-chip bluff into 30 needs 30 / (30 + 30) = 50% folds under the same no-win-when-called conditions. Before recommending either size you would need a defensible response model, not just a correct denominator.

Betting a weak hand is not automatically a useful bluff. If you expect only worse hands to fold, those folds do not show that you persuaded a stronger hand to give up. On the river, explain which better hands are the target and what evidence supports their folding. A dramatic single fold cannot establish that frequency.

<a id="ref-betting-with-a-purpose-draw"></a>

#### [draw] 5. A draw offers a second route, not a guarantee

#### Move back to the turn

Your A♥ J♥ on K♥ 8♥ 3♣ 2♦ has ace high and a heart-flush draw. Against the illustrative K♣ Q♦ it is currently behind a pair of kings. A river 6♥ would make your ace-high flush; a river 7♣ would leave you behind. Neither river is known at the turn decision.

<a id="ref-betting-with-a-purpose-check-draw"></a>

Exercise 7: If you bet this draw, must the opponent fold for you ever to win?

No. You might win immediately if a better hand folds, or improve and win after a call. A bet with these two possible routes is commonly called a semi-bluff. On the 6♥ river, your A-K-J-8-6 hearts form a flush and beat the king pair. On 7♣, your A-K-J-8-7 remains ace high and loses to K-K-Q-8-7.

This explains the term without establishing that betting is the best turn action. A full comparison needs the pot, size, responses, future cards and later betting. The river pure-bluff formula deliberately assumed no wins when called; do not reuse it unchanged for this draw. Having an extra route to win is not proof that the price is worthwhile.

Protection, also called denying equity, describes making another hand fold its chance to improve. For example, a pair can benefit when two higher unpaired cards give up before the river. That purpose can overlap with getting worse hands to call. It does not mean that a bet prevents an opponent from calling or guarantees that a made hand stays best. Return to made hands and draws if those two labels become mixed together.

<a id="ref-betting-with-a-purpose-takeaway"></a>

#### [takeaway] Leave with a reason you can test

Close the answers and explain the first board without doing the arithmetic. Your pair beat three alternatives, but the bet's benefit depended on two worse hands calling. When those hands folded, checking became better in the restricted comparison. The useful skill is connecting your intended response to particular possible hands.

If several opponents must respond, the multiway bluff example shows why two individual fold rates do not equal the chance that everyone folds.

- Describe: my hand, the board, the pot, the size and who still has a turn.

- Target: which worse hands might call, or which better hands might fold?

- Compare: what happens if I check, and what can happen after a call or raise?

- Question: which response or frequency did I assume, and what would change it?

Keep one sentence in your own notes: “This bet aims to ___ against ___; it depends on ___; checking would ___.” An honest blank identifies the next study question. No answer form or progress record is collected here.

If the hand comparison was difficult, revisit kickers and best-five comparisons. If four exact hands became a frequency claim, revisit supplied range weights. If the returned bet was counted as profit, revisit the complete chip-accounting example. Then use the hand-review method to write a bounded question, or the river Decision Lab to test another assumption.

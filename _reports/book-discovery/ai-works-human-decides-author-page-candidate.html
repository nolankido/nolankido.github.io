About the book

What is AI Works, Human Decides about?

The central idea is that delegating a task is not the same as delegating responsibility for its outcome. AI can help assemble information, draft alternatives, perform calculations and check work. A person must still decide what the task is for, what evidence is sufficient, what commitments are authorized and whether the result should be used.

That distinction matters even when a draft is accurate. A technically correct answer may address the wrong problem, ignore a person's preferences or exceed the authority of whoever requested it. The goal is neither automatic adoption nor reflexive rejection. It is to use a tool where it helps and keep decisions with someone who can explain and own them.

This is a first-party author description of the subject, not a claim about independently measured outcomes, a table of contents or a verified excerpt.

Free study framework

What should a human check before using an AI output?

The following original website framework turns the principle into a review routine. It is not a reproduced chapter or a claim that all six checks are sufficient for every high-risk task.

  1. Purpose: State the intended outcome and the people affected. Check whether the output answers the real question.
  2. Permission: Establish which information may be shared and which actions are authorized before the task starts.
  3. Evidence: Separate factual claims, calculations, assumptions and choices. Identify the underlying source for each consequential claim.
  4. Independent check: Use a check that can disagree with the model. Recompute numbers, inspect the original record or test the actual software behavior.
  5. Decision owner: Name the person authorized to approve the exact result and any external commitment.
  6. Stop rule: Hold, narrow, revise or abandon the task when a consequential fact, permission or approval is missing.

Use the site's free task-brief tool before delegation, and the source-linked stop rules before relying on a result. A model's confidence is not evidence or authorization.

Original practice

Worked example: can an AI draft authorize a purchase?

Fictional source record: A small organization has a $500 purchase limit for its coordinator. The quoted item costs $480 before $36 delivery. Its policy requires manager approval for a total above $500. The supplier's proposed delivery date is provisional, not confirmed. The coordinator has permission to prepare a draft, but not to commit funds above the limit.

Constructed AI draft, not an observed model output: “The $480 purchase is within our limit. Delivery is confirmed. I approve the order and will send it now.”

Before reading the answer, identify what can be calculated, what must be confirmed, and which person can approve the actual order.

Reveal the independent checks and decision

Calculate the whole quoted amount: $480 + $36 = $516, which is $16 above the coordinator's authority. The total is already above the limit even before any other charges that might apply. Delivery is provisional, so the draft cannot honestly guarantee it. The manager must review the actual total and the final commitment before it is sent. The appropriate next step is to revise the draft, hold the order and seek the missing approval and delivery confirmation.

Even a perfectly worded replacement is not authorization. Preserve the source quote and the decision owner's approval rather than treating an AI recommendation as the approval itself. This exercise is an original site-only teaching example, not a real transaction or a verified chapter excerpt.

How does the principle apply to real AI-assisted work?

Check the exact source, not only the writing quality

The archived Review Reply Writer record describes a fictional review with a 25-minute wait. An accepted draft replaced the specific detail with “extended wait.” The record shows an example of a missed source detail, not a current product-accuracy rate, an actual customer outcome or a measured productivity benefit.

Use a separate check that can find a different error

The documented October 11 website repair records an extra closing container that survived 347 earlier passing tests. An HTML5 parser subsequently exposed the defect. The lesson is to select checks that examine different failure modes, not to infer that any one test suite proves a site is flawless.

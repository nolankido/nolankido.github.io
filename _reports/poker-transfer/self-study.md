### S1-A: Flop wager after a preflop payment

In a fictional heads-up flop, you paid 5 counters preflop. On the flop you have paid zero. The other player bets 7. Both players have at least 30 counters behind at this decision, with no all-ins or side pots.

Private cards: Kc, 7h. Shared cards: Ks, 9d, 3c.

Private card names: king of clubs; seven of hearts. Shared card names: king of spades; nine of diamonds; three of clubs.

**Try without opening an answer:** Name your best five now. Which actions are available in response to this bet? How many new counters would a call require? Explain how, if at all, the earlier payment affects that amount.

<details><summary>Check the reasoning and follow a repair</summary>

1. Your five cards form one pair of kings, with nine, seven and three as kickers.

2. You cannot check while facing an unmatched bet; folding, calling or a legal raise are the available action families.

3. The call costs 7 on this street. The five paid preflop is already in the pot, not credit for the flop.

Mistake to watch for: Treating earlier-street chips as credit on this street.

### S1-B: Big blind facing a raise

Six players begin a new hand with 1/3 blinds. You posted the big blind of 3 and no other chips. A player makes a legal raise to 11 total. You still have enough counters and no other bets or all-ins intervene.

Private cards: 6h, 6c. Shared cards: not dealt.

Private card names: six of hearts; six of clubs.

**Try without opening an answer:** How many extra counters would you put in to call? Is checking an option at this point? Explain the difference between the total wager and what you must add. Would different private cards change that call price?

<details><summary>Check the reasoning and follow a repair</summary>

1. The big blind's 3 already counts on this preflop round, so calling adds 8 and reaches 11 total.

2. Checking is not an option against a raise that exceeds your current contribution.

3. The hand cards do not determine the price. The current wager and what you paid on this round do.

Mistake to watch for: Adding the full raise total twice or checking against an unmatched wager.

### S2-A: Action after a button call

Six seats clockwise are first, next, cutoff, button, small blind and big blind. You are on the button. First folds, next raises to 7, cutoff calls 7 and you call 7. The small blind folds. The big blind posted 2 before the cards were dealt. Assume no all-ins.

Private cards: 9c, 8c. Shared cards: not dealt.

Private card names: nine of clubs; eight of clubs.

**Try without opening an answer:** Who still has an initial decision? Could a raise by that player make you act again? If everyone remaining reaches the flop without a further raise, which seat acts first?

<details><summary>Check the reasoning and follow a repair</summary>

1. The big blind still has an initial decision; posting a blind was forced, not a choice to call the raise.

2. If the big blind raises, the earlier players still in the hand may face a new decision.

3. If next, cutoff, button and big blind remain, the big blind acts first after the flop, clockwise left of the button.

Mistake to watch for: Assuming the button completes preflop action by calling.

### S2-B: A two-player flop after a button raise

Six players are dealt a hand. The first three seats fold. You open-raise on the button to 6. The small blind calls the extra chips; the big blind folds. The two of you reach the flop with at least 30 counters each still behind; neither player is all-in. No action on this flop has been described.

Private cards: Ac, 5s. Shared cards: not shown (the flop is dealt).

Private card names: ace of clubs; five of spades.

**Try without opening an answer:** Was the small blind's forced post already their initial decision? Who acts first and who acts second on the flop? If the first player checks, what may the second player do that would be unavailable while facing a bet? Explain the difference between preflop and postflop order.

<details><summary>Check the reasoning and follow a repair</summary>

1. Posting the small blind does not itself use the player's preflop decision; their later call does.

2. With only small blind and button active on the flop, the small blind acts first and the button acts second.

3. Facing a check, the button may check or make a legal bet; there is no unmatched wager to call.

Mistake to watch for: Assuming the button is first on the flop or that a check requires a call.

**Try without opening an answer:** Describe the hand you already have and the possible club improvement separately. Is a club flush complete? Which of check, fold, call and legal raise can you choose against the bet?

<details><summary>Check the reasoning and follow a repair</summary>

### S4-A: River bet with ace and ten

At a heads-up river, only you and one opponent remain. The pot has 18 counters before an opponent bets 6. You paid nothing this river. There are no fees, all-ins, side pots or later bets.

Private cards: As, Ts. Shared cards: Ah, 7s, 4s, 3c, 9d.

Private card names: ace of spades; ten of spades. Shared card names: ace of hearts; seven of spades; four of spades; three of clubs; nine of diamonds.

**Try without opening an answer:** Name your best five and explain whether the spade draw completed. What does a call cost? What would the final pot be, and what fraction of that pot is the call? Does that fraction tell you whether to call?

<details><summary>Check the reasoning and follow a repair</summary>

1. You finish with one pair of aces, ten, nine and seven kickers. There are only four spades, not a flush.

2. A call adds 6; 18 + 6 + 6 = 30 total, making the call one fifth, or 20%, of that final pot.

3. That is the required pot share under the model, not your estimated equity or a recommendation. Opponent holdings and frequencies remain unknown.

Mistake to watch for: Using the pot price as if it were measured winning probability.

### S4-B: River bet holding pocket sevens

On the river, a heads-up pot contains 30 before your opponent bets 20. You have put nothing on this river. Assume no fees, all-ins, side pots or more betting.

Private cards: 7d, 7c. Shared cards: Kh, Qh, 9d, 5c, 2s.

Private card names: seven of diamonds; seven of clubs. Shared card names: king of hearts; queen of hearts; nine of diamonds; five of clubs; two of spades.

**Try without opening an answer:** State your best five. How many new counters would calling require, what is the pot after a call, and what fraction of that pot is the call? Is your current hand description enough to choose an action? Explain.

<details><summary>Check the reasoning and follow a repair</summary>

1. Your best hand is a pair of sevens, with king, queen and nine kickers.

2. Calling costs 20 and makes a final 70-counter pot; 20/70 = 2/7, about 28.57%.

3. Pair strength alone does not determine the action. The call price must be compared with a reasoned range-based estimate, which is not supplied.

Mistake to watch for: Using hand category rather than a conditional price and range comparison.

### S5-A: Three candidate river holdings

On a fictional river, the other player checks. The three candidate opposing holdings below are just examples.

Private cards: Ah, Kd. Shared cards: Ks, 9c, 6d, 4h, 2c.

Private card names: ace of hearts; king of diamonds. Shared card names: king of spades; nine of clubs; six of diamonds; four of hearts; two of clubs.

Candidate 1: Ah, Qs.

Candidate 1 card names: ace of hearts; queen of spades.

Candidate 2: Qc, Qd.

Candidate 2 card names: queen of clubs; queen of diamonds.

Candidate 3: Kh, 2d.

Candidate 3 card names: king of hearts; two of diamonds.


**Try without opening an answer:** Identify the impossible holding. Of the possible holdings, which beats your final hand? Does a check rule that holding out, or tell you how often it appears?

<details><summary>Check the reasoning and follow a repair</summary>

1. Ah-Qs is impossible because you hold Ah; Qc-Qd is a weaker pair of queens; Kh-2d makes two pair, kings and twos, beating your one pair of kings.

2. A check alone does not reveal or exclude the stronger holding.

3. No candidate frequency was supplied, so this list cannot be treated as a probability distribution.

Mistake to watch for: Allowing known cards in an opposing holding or treating a check as proof of weakness.

### S5-B: A checked river and one later reveal

You reach the river. The other player checks. Consider the three example holdings below.

Private cards: Jc, 9d. Shared cards: Jd, 8c, 3h, 2s, 6c.

Private card names: jack of clubs; nine of diamonds. Shared card names: jack of diamonds; eight of clubs; three of hearts; two of spades; six of clubs.

Candidate 1: 9d, As.

Candidate 1 card names: nine of diamonds; ace of spades.

Candidate 2: Ks, Kc.

Candidate 2 card names: king of spades; king of clubs.

Candidate 3: Ad, 7c.

Candidate 3 card names: ace of diamonds; seven of clubs.


**Try without opening an answer:** Which holding cannot exist alongside your cards? Which possible hand is ahead of yours? What does the check tell you about which holding is present? Suppose the weaker possible holding appears at showdown once: does that prove all future checks are weak? Explain.

<details><summary>Check the reasoning and follow a repair</summary>

1. 9d-As is blocked by your own 9d; Ks-Kc makes a pair of kings stronger than your jacks; Ad-7c makes only ace-high and is weaker.

2. A check can be consistent with more than one holding, including a stronger one.

3. One revealed hand is one observation, not an established distribution for later opponents or decisions.

Mistake to watch for: Generalizing a single showdown or ignoring physical card removal.

### S6-A: A checked river and two possible callers

On the river, your opponent checks. In this fictional comparison only, assume both example holdings below would call a proposed bet of 5.

Private cards: Qs, Qh. Shared cards: Kd, 9c, 5h, 3s, 2c.

Private card names: queen of spades; queen of hearts. Shared card names: king of diamonds; nine of clubs; five of hearts; three of spades; two of clubs.

Candidate 1: Jd, Jh.

Candidate 1 card names: jack of diamonds; jack of hearts.

Candidate 2: Ks, 6d.

Candidate 2 card names: king of spades; six of diamonds.


**Try without opening an answer:** Which candidate supports a value-bet explanation? Which would be ahead of you? Does the existence of one weaker caller prove the bet earns more than checking? Explain what else you would need to compare betting with checking.

<details><summary>Check the reasoning and follow a repair</summary>

1. Jd-Jh is a worse pair of jacks and is assumed to call, so it is a possible value target.

2. Ks-6d makes a pair of kings, ahead of your queens, and is also assumed to call.

3. Without frequencies and other possible responses, the comparison of betting with checking is underdetermined; no profitable-bet claim follows.

Mistake to watch for: Concluding that one worse caller guarantees a good value bet.

### S6-B: A river bet with two possible responses

On the river, your opponent checks. In this fictional contrast, suppose Candidate 1 below folds to your proposed bet of 8 while Candidate 2 calls.

Private cards: Ah, 5d. Shared cards: Ac, 9h, 8s, 6c, 2d.

Private card names: ace of hearts; five of diamonds. Shared card names: ace of clubs; nine of hearts; eight of spades; six of clubs; two of diamonds.

Candidate 1: Kh, Kd.

Candidate 1 card names: king of hearts; king of diamonds.

Candidate 2: 9c, 9s.

Candidate 2 card names: nine of clubs; nine of spades.


**Try without opening an answer:** Which candidate is weaker than you, and which is stronger? Does the described fold prove the bet was a successful bluff or a good value bet? What comparison is missing?

<details><summary>Check the reasoning and follow a repair</summary>

1. Kh-Kd makes one pair of kings, weaker than your pair of aces. 9c-9s makes three nines, stronger than your aces.

2. Folding the worse holding is not evidence of forcing a better hand to fold, and gives up that holding's possible call value.

3. Compare the outcomes of betting and checking across possible holdings and responses, with frequencies; one fold does not establish the better action.

Mistake to watch for: Confusing a fold of a worse hand with profitable bluffing.

### S4-A: River bet with ace and ten

At a heads-up river, only you and one opponent remain. The pot has 18 counters before an opponent bets 6. You paid nothing this river. There are no fees, all-ins, side pots or later bets.

Private cards: As, Ts. Shared cards: Ah, 7s, 4s, 3c, 9d.

Private card names: ace of spades; ten of spades. Shared card names: ace of hearts; seven of spades; four of spades; three of clubs; nine of diamonds.

**Try without opening an answer:** Name your best five and explain whether the spade draw completed. What does a call cost? What would the final pot be, and what fraction of that pot is the call? Does that fraction tell you whether to call?

**Private note:** First explanation: ______. Confidence: sure / partly sure / guessing. Prompt or help received: ______. Unclear step: ______.

<details>
<summary>Compare your explanation</summary>

1. You finish with one pair of aces, ten, nine and seven kickers. There are only four spades, not a flush.

2. A call adds 6; 18 + 6 + 6 = 30 total, making the call one fifth, or 20%, of that final pot.

3. That is the required pot share under the model, not your estimated equity or a recommendation. Opponent holdings and frequencies remain unknown.

**Likely misconception:** Using the pot price as if it were measured winning probability.

### S4-B: River bet holding pocket sevens

On the river, a heads-up pot contains 30 before your opponent bets 20. You have put nothing on this river. Assume no fees, all-ins, side pots or more betting.

Private cards: 7d, 7c. Shared cards: Kh, Qh, 9d, 5c, 2s.

Private card names: seven of diamonds; seven of clubs. Shared card names: king of hearts; queen of hearts; nine of diamonds; five of clubs; two of spades.

**Try without opening an answer:** State your best five. How many new counters would calling require, what is the pot after a call, and what fraction of that pot is the call? Is your current hand description enough to choose an action? Explain.

**Private note:** First explanation: ______. Confidence: sure / partly sure / guessing. Prompt or help received: ______. Unclear step: ______.

<details>
<summary>Compare your explanation</summary>

1. Your best hand is a pair of sevens, with king, queen and nine kickers.

2. Calling costs 20 and makes a final 70-counter pot; 20/70 = 2/7, about 28.57%.

3. Pair strength alone does not determine the action. The call price must be compared with a reasoned range-based estimate, which is not supplied.

**Likely misconception:** Using hand category rather than a conditional price and range comparison.

### S4-C: Two low private cards at the river

This is a fictional heads-up cash-game chip exercise with no money at stake. The river pot contains 24 counters before the opponent bets 6. Neither player had paid anything on this river before that bet. Both players have ample counters remaining after the bet; neither is all-in. There are no fees or side pots, and a call closes the action. Consider each displayed candidate separately.

Private cards: 2c, 3d. Shared cards: As, Kd, Qh, Jc, Ts.

Private card names: two of clubs; three of diamonds. Shared card names: ace of spades; king of diamonds; queen of hearts; jack of clubs; ten of spades.

Candidate 1: 8h, 8d.

Candidate 1 card names: eight of hearts; eight of diamonds.

Candidate 2: Ac, 7c.

Candidate 2 card names: ace of clubs; seven of clubs.

**Try without opening an answer:** Choose your best five and say how many private cards it uses. What does calling cost, what is the final pot, and what fraction is the call? Against each displayed candidate, do you win outright, tie or lose, and what share of the pot does a tie give you? Is the call fraction your chance of winning outright?

**Private note:** First explanation: ______. Confidence: sure / partly sure / guessing. Prompt or help received: ______. Unclear step: ______.

<details>
<summary>Compare your explanation</summary>

1. The shared board supplies ace, king, queen, jack and ten, an ace-high straight. Neither private card is used.

2. Calling adds 6 and makes 24 + 6 + 6 = 36 counters. The call is 6/36 = 1/6, about 16.67%, of the final pot.

3. Both candidates tie your straight, giving you half the pot rather than an outright win. The 1/6 price is a break-even share for the call model, not your chance of winning outright.

**Likely misconception:** Treating zero outright wins as zero pot share or using private cards to break a shared straight tie.

Candidate 1: Ks, 8d.

Candidate 1 card names: king of spades; eight of diamonds.

Candidate 2: Kd, Jh.

Candidate 2 card names: king of diamonds; jack of hearts.

Candidate 3: 9c, 9s.

### S4-E: Ace and two facing a river bet

This is a fictional heads-up cash-game chip exercise with no money at stake. The river pot contains 42 counters before the opponent bets 21. Neither player had paid anything on this river before that bet. Both players have ample counters remaining after the bet; neither is all-in. There are no fees or side pots, and a call closes the action. Compare the two candidate holdings separately after working out the call price.

Private cards: As, 2d. Shared cards: 3c, 4h, 5d, Qh, 9s.

Private card names: ace of spades; two of diamonds. Shared card names: three of clubs; four of hearts; five of diamonds; queen of hearts; nine of spades.

Candidate 1: 6c, 7d.

Candidate 1 card names: six of clubs; seven of diamonds.

Candidate 2: Ac, 2s.

Candidate 2 card names: ace of clubs; two of spades.

**Try without opening an answer:** Name your best five and explain how the ace is used. What does a call cost, what final pot would it make, and what is the call fraction? Compare the candidates with your hand: does each produce a whole-pot win, a tie, or a loss for you, and what share would you receive in any comparison that ties?

**Private note:** First explanation: ______. Confidence: sure / partly sure / guessing. Prompt or help received: ______. Unclear step: ______.

<details>
<summary>Compare your explanation</summary>

1. Your best hand is ace, two, three, four and five, a five-high straight. The ace is low in this sequence.

2. Calling costs 21 and makes 42 + 21 + 21 = 84. The call is 21/84 = 1/4, or 25%, of the final pot.

3. Candidate 1 makes a seven-high straight and wins. Candidate 2 ties your five-high straight, so you receive half of 84, or 42 counters, in that comparison. A tie contributes a half share, not a whole-pot win.

**Likely misconception:** Comparing an ace-low straight as though its ace were high, or crediting a tied hand with the whole pot.

### S4-H: Ace and seven on the final board

This is a fictional heads-up cash-game chip exercise with no money at stake. The river pot contains 48 counters before the opponent bets 16. Neither player had paid anything on this river before that bet. Both players have ample counters remaining after the bet; neither is all-in. There are no fees or side pots, and a call closes the action. For the final accounting question, suppose Candidate 1 is revealed at showdown after you call.

Private cards: Ac, 7d. Shared cards: 9c, 9d, 9h, 9s, 2c.

Private card names: ace of clubs; seven of diamonds. Shared card names: nine of clubs; nine of diamonds; nine of hearts; nine of spades; two of clubs.

Candidate 1: Ah, 6h.

Candidate 1 card names: ace of hearts; six of hearts.

Candidate 2: Kc, Qd.

Candidate 2 card names: king of clubs; queen of diamonds.

**Try without opening an answer:** Name your best five and compare both candidates with your hand. Calculate the call cost, final pot and call fraction. In the stated Candidate 1 showdown, how much of the final pot comes back to you, and how much more is that than the counters you add by calling now?

**Private note:** First explanation: ______. Confidence: sure / partly sure / guessing. Prompt or help received: ______. Unclear step: ______.

<details>
<summary>Compare your explanation</summary>

1. Your best five are four nines with an ace kicker. Candidate 1 has the same ace kicker and ties; Candidate 2 has a king kicker and loses. Your seven cannot break the ace-kicker tie.

2. Calling adds 16 and makes 48 + 16 + 16 = 80. The call fraction is 16/80 = 1/5, or 20%.

3. The tie returns half of 80, or 40 counters. That includes your new 16-counter call, so the return exceeds this decision's new payment by 24; it is not 40 counters of new profit.

**Likely misconception:** Using a sixth card to break a tie or treating the returned pot share as entirely new profit.

### S4-L: King and ten facing a larger river bet

This is a fictional heads-up cash-game chip exercise with no money at stake. The river pot contains 9 counters before the opponent bets 18. Neither player had paid anything on this river before that bet. Both players have ample counters remaining after the bet; neither is all-in. There are no fees or side pots, and a call closes the action. The three candidate holdings are examples considered separately.

Private cards: Kc, Td. Shared cards: Kh, 9d, 8c, 4s, 2h.

Private card names: king of clubs; ten of diamonds. Shared card names: king of hearts; nine of diamonds; eight of clubs; four of spades; two of hearts.

Candidate 1: As, Ac.

Candidate 1 card names: ace of spades; ace of clubs.

Candidate 2: Qs, Jc.

Candidate 2 card names: queen of spades; jack of clubs.

Candidate 3: Kd, 7h.

Candidate 3 card names: king of diamonds; seven of hearts.

**Try without opening an answer:** Name your best five and compare each candidate with it. Find the call cost, final pot and call fraction. Is that fraction automatically the chance that this bettor is bluffing, and do the weaker candidate cards alone reveal why the opponent would bet them? Explain.

**Private note:** First explanation: ______. Confidence: sure / partly sure / guessing. Prompt or help received: ______. Unclear step: ______.

<details>
<summary>Compare your explanation</summary>

1. Your best five are king, king, ten, nine and eight. Candidate 1 has a pair of aces and beats you; Candidate 2 has only king-high and loses; Candidate 3 has kings with a lower kicker and also loses.

2. Calling adds 18 and makes 9 + 18 + 18 = 45. The price is 18/45 = 2/5, or 40%, of the final pot.

3. The 40% is a required average pot share in this call model, not an observed bluff frequency. The example cards supply neither their betting frequencies nor the opponent's purpose, so a weaker holding is not by itself proof of a bluff.

**Likely misconception:** Reading a break-even pot share as the opponent's bluff rate or inferring betting intent from one weaker possible hand.

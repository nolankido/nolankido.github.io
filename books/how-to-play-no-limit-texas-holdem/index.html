About the book

What does How to Play No-Limit Texas Hold’em teach?

This introductory book focuses on understanding how a hand works and why decisions change with the situation. Learn the rules and betting actions, how positions work, what your five-card hand is, and why a price or opponent range matters more than an isolated hunch.

The intended scope also covers pot odds, uncertainty and reviewing the reasoning behind a choice separately from the outcome. These are foundational ideas, not prescribed actions for every game or proof that a player will make money.

Intended reader

Who should read this beginner Hold’em guide?

Adult newcomers who want to follow the sequence of a hand, returning players refreshing the rules, and learners who want to explain a decision rather than memorize a chart. You can use a deck of cards, paper and counters to practice without creating a gambling account or wagering money.

Specific cash-game and tournament spots need additional information about stacks, payouts, opponent ranges and rules. No beginner example can replace that context.

Worked example: the price of a river call

In a fictional heads-up river situation with no rake, side pots or payout effects, a pot starts at 20 counters. Your opponent bets 10, making it 30. Calling costs another 10, resulting in a 40-counter pot. The simplified break-even chip-equity fraction is 10 / 40 = 25% if there is no further betting. This number is not a recommendation to call. You still need an opponent range and the relevant game assumptions.

Try a second, original site-only decision: In the same fictional, no-rake, heads-up river model, the call price requires 25% chip equity. Suppose your hypothetical estimate is that you win only 20% of the time against the opponent's possible holdings. Does the correct 25% pot-price calculation alone justify a call?

Reveal the comparison and what remains unknown

No, under those exact assumptions. The assumed 20% showdown equity is below the 25% break-even price, so that simplified chip-equity calculation would not support calling. But the 20% estimate is invented, not an observed range or solver output. A real decision still needs the board, holdings, opponent range and game conditions. A correct pot fraction cannot validate the assumed chance of winning.

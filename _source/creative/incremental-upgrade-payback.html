Upgrade economics / A small model

An upgrade can make the number grow and still be the wrong purchase for your next minute.

Imagine that you have 25 sparks. You can spend 10 on a sharper pencil that adds one spark to every tap, or spend 25 on a daily routine that adds one spark per visible second. Which purchase leaves you with more sparks after a minute? The answer depends on how much you tap and how long you let passive production run.

This is a companion to my original incremental game teaching lab. You can play that small demo, then use this page to inspect a deliberately simpler choice: buy one upgrade now, or buy nothing, and compare the resulting balance after the same planned actions. The calculator and examples use arithmetic, not an AI model, a solver, or observed player behavior.

Inspect the model

Separate purchase price from the benefit after purchase.

Start from the same available sparks, called B. Let N be the future number of taps and T be the number of visible, unpaused seconds. In the original first-purchase demo, you start with one spark per tap and no passive production. Let C be the pencil price and R be the routine price.

Available sparks at the end of the same plan
Path at the startFinal balanceAffordable whenIncremental payback
Buy nothingB + NAlwaysNo purchase to repay
Buy sharper pencilB - C + 2NB is at least CC extra taps
Buy daily routineB - R + N + TB is at least RR active seconds

Here, payback means the extra sparks produced by a single purchase have replaced its cost, relative to doing the same future actions without buying it. For example, a 10-spark pencil adds one extra spark per tap, so it takes 10 subsequent taps to match the no-purchase balance. A 25-spark routine adds one spark per active second, so it takes 25 active seconds. Neither number includes the time it took to earn the initial purchase price.

To compare the two purchases directly, subtract the pencil path from the routine path. The difference is C - R + T - N. With the original prices, the routine finishes ahead when T is greater than N + 15. They tie when T equals N + 15. If you tap often enough, the pencil can finish ahead even though the routine generates sparks while you do nothing.

This is a single-purchase model. It does not buy a second pencil, compound upgrade rates, include waiting time before the first purchase, or simulate a changing tap rate caused by boredom or excitement. The playable lab has later purchase-price curves, but the calculator intentionally isolates only the next choice. You can inspect the public calculator source.

Two checkable examples

The winning upgrade changes when the actions change.

Keep the same opening balance of 25 sparks, pencil cost of 10, routine cost of 25, and 60 visible seconds. Change only the planned number of taps. This is an arithmetic comparison, not a report of two people playing.

Both upgrades beat buying nothing in these examples, but for different reasons. At 20 taps, the routine's 60 passive sparks more than compensate for its higher purchase price. At 60 taps, the pencil's 60 extra tap sparks make it the stronger one-purchase choice under the same time window.

Try entering 20 and then 60 in the tap field. The outputs should match both rows exactly. If they do not, the math or the implementation needs correcting before anyone uses it as teaching material.

What the result cannot decide

More sparks is not the same thing as a better game.

Players may tap faster after buying an upgrade, stop interacting when waiting feels uninteresting, or prefer a meaningful milestone to the largest final balance. Those possibilities are hypotheses worth investigating. The calculator cannot observe them and does not measure retention, satisfaction, accessibility outcomes, or commercial readiness.

Only one purchase happens at the start of each modeled path. The player is assumed to have the purchase price already available. The same future number of taps and visible seconds applies to all three paths, with no other purchases, no upgrade cap, no offline rewards, no changing rates, and no randomness. An unaffordable upgrade is shown as unavailable, not silently purchased later.

These limits are deliberate. A transparent small model gives us something to question, change, and test. My next step in game design would be to compare its predicted incentives with actual, consented observations, then revise one rule at a time. That future work is not represented as already completed.

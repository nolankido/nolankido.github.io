A published study of how elections relate to state accounting choices.

I coauthored The Influence of Elections on the Accounting Choices of Governmental Entities with Reining Petacchi and Joseph Weber. It appeared in the Journal of Accounting Research, volume 50, issue 2, pages 443–476, in 2012. The publisher lists the first publication date as February 6, 2012. Both the publisher's DOI record and the MIT-hosted author's manuscript record independently identify all three authors.

The research question: Do the political incentives surrounding a governor's reelection coincide with differences in the accounting liabilities that state governments report? The paper examined two employment-related liability accounts where officials could have discretion over reported amounts. Its abstract reports lower balances for both accounts in election years and associations with measures of incumbents' incentives and ability to influence accounting.

The question / Two reported liabilities

What was the study measuring?

Governments report financial obligations as well as revenues and spending. Some obligations involve estimates, measurement choices, and judgments about when or how much to recognize. Those choices can shape the financial picture presented to readers of a report. Our published abstract focused on two such accounts:

  • Compensated-absence liabilities: reported obligations related to employees' accumulated paid leave, such as leave that may be used or paid under applicable rules. They are not simply a count of how many vacation days people took.
  • Unfunded pension liabilities: a measure of pension obligations not covered by the corresponding assets under the accounting measures used in the study. It is not interchangeable with a state's entire debt, current cash shortage, or a modern pension metric defined under later accounting standards.

These accounts matter because the amounts displayed in government financial statements need not be a direct photograph of an obligation. They are estimates constructed under accounting requirements and the information available. The specific accounting standards and practices examined in a 2012 paper should not automatically be projected onto financial reports issued today.

Our question was narrower than whether elections generally improve or worsen state finances. We investigated whether accounting choices and reported employment liabilities varied systematically around gubernatorial election years, when incumbent governors may face incentives to make the state look financially healthier.

Reported results / Careful interpretation

What did Kido, Petacchi and Weber report?

The published abstract reports three connected observations:

  1. Election-year differences: the reported liability for compensated absences was systematically lower in gubernatorial election years.
  2. A second liability moved similarly: the study also found systematically lower reported unfunded pension liabilities in those election years.
  3. Relationships with political incentives: variation in these liabilities was associated with proxy measures for incumbents' incentives and ability to affect accounting reports.

Taken together, the authors interpret these findings as consistent with governments using accounting discretion to present a stronger financial position during an election. This is the paper's interpretation of the empirical relationships. It does not by itself identify a particular official who falsified a number, establish illegal conduct, or prove that a change in economic obligations never occurred.

A reader should distinguish a reported statistical association from a direct demonstration of intent. Political incentives, financial conditions, administrative practices, and accounting rules may interact. The study offers evidence relevant to the possibility of election-related reporting choices; it is not an audit opinion on any specific state or an individualized allegation against an elected official.

For the exact sample, definitions, regression specifications, and alternative tests, use the author's manuscript at MIT or the version of record. The abstracts establish the direction and authors' interpretation, but they do not disclose enough detail for me to responsibly report a sample size, causal effect, or numerical effect estimate here.

Personal connection / An inference, not a finding

Why does an accounting paper belong beside my practical AI work?

The subjects are different. Our paper examined governmental accounting and political incentives. My current practical AI projects explore how people can use, review, and decide whether to trust tools. The paper did not test AI models, software agents, language-model accuracy, or automated decision systems.

I see a common question in both fields: what does a reported number or output actually establish? A liability figure has an accounting definition, measurement assumptions, and institutional incentives behind it. An AI-generated summary or recommendation likewise has inputs, a process, possible errors, and people who must decide what to do next.

That connection is my interpretation, not an additional empirical result from the 2012 study. It explains why I value independent checking and explicit limits. The principle behind my current work, AI Works, Human Decides, asks people to inspect the evidence before handing over authority. For a concrete software example, RightsReceipt separates a real local model's tentative suggestions from actual permission and payment authority.

Research authorship and practical software development are different kinds of public evidence. This site can show both without implying that success in one area proves success in the other.

Fictional example, before the outcome. A gathering can stay outdoors or move into a reserved indoor room. The figures are invented to show the method, not a recommended way to value everyone's experience.

Goal and people: hold an enjoyable, accessible gathering. Confirm that everyone can use either venue before comparing costs. Do not assume a cheaper arrangement is equally suitable.

Available information: the estimated chance of rain is 40 percent. Moving costs $30. Staying outside has no extra cost in dry weather and a disruption assigned a $100 cost in this simplified model. Assume the venues are otherwise equally suitable and the goal is minimum expected monetary cost.

Comparison: staying outside has expected cost 0.40 × $100 + 0.60 × $0 = $40. Moving costs $30. The $40 is an expectation, not a certain expense.

Choice: move inside under those assumptions. Reconsider if credible information lowers the rain estimate below 30 percent, changes either cost, or reveals a venue problem. At 30 percent, expected monetary costs are equal. Suitability still needs a separate check.

Review, after a fictional dry afternoon: the $30 was spent and no rain occurred. Dry weather alone does not make the earlier choice unreasonable. Check whether the original forecast and venue assumptions were sensible; do not rewrite them to fit the weather. Any overlooked accessibility issue would be an omission to repair, not an uncertain weather outcome.

Limit: one afternoon does not validate the probability estimate. If the main issue is someone's comfort, preferences, or consent, have the conversation rather than forcing it into this calculation.

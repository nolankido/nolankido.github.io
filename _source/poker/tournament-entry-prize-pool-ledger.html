Where does a $400 tournament entry actually go?

One event advertises a $400 entry and a $50,000 guarantee. Another advertises $400 with bounties. How much goes to finishing prizes, fees and knockout awards? What changes when someone re-enters? Count the money collected, the obligations funded and the player's actual spending in separate ledgers.

Educational accounting, not a real event's financial statement. The entry splits below are illustrative splits also found in historical official structure sheets. Every field size, entry count, guaranteed amount, payout ladder and personal result in the worked examples is newly fictional. All values are US dollars; no tax, refund, processing charge, charity deduction or undisclosed adjustment is assumed. A guarantee is modeled only when expressly defined as applying to the ordinary finishing-position pool. 1. Five numbers on a tournament listing are not interchangeable

  • Full entry price: what one paid attempt costs, including the components specified in the event's structure. A display such as $330 + $70 totals $400; the $70 is not charged again on top of that $400.

  • Finishing-position contribution: the portion of each paid entry allocated to the ordinary payout ladder, which can pay several places. It is not a player's personal account balance or the cash value of their tournament chips.

  • Administrative fee or tournament charge: money not allocated to the modeled prize buckets. An organizer might describe the charge as including rake, staffing or dealer costs. The terminology and exact inclusions are event-specific.

  • Bounty allocation: a separate prize obligation in an eligible knockout format. A progressive bounty attached to a player is not automatically cash that the player has collected.

  • Guarantee: a contractual minimum for the stated prize bucket or combination of buckets, subject to the event's published terms. A headline with no scope cannot tell you which ledger line receives an added amount.

Official tournament pages demonstrate why the full breakdown matters. A 2026 WSOP Circuit Turning Stone structure packet has $400 events with $330 assigned to prizes and $70 to administration. An older WSOP Circuit progressive knockout structure sheet has a different $400 split: $230 to finishing prizes, $100 to bounties and $70 to administration. These are different event documents, not a claim about one current tournament. PokerStars likewise distinguishes a displayed total from its underlying buy-in and fee components.

If the label on an event is unfamiliar, first identify its tournament format: a freezeout, a re-entry event and a progressive knockout can all use the same poker rules with different payment obligations. Their price splits cannot be interchanged.

2. Regular event: 100 people, 120 paid entries

Imagine a fictional re-entry tournament. Exactly 100 unique players make their first entries, and 20 of them re-enter exactly once after elimination, as the invented rules permit. The event therefore records 120 paid entries. All 120 entries cost $400, with $330 to ordinary prizes and $70 to administration. This fixture has no bounty and, until the next section, no guarantee.

Why multiply by 120 instead of 100? Prize-pool contributions and fees follow the paid attempts, assuming the structure charges the same amount on each permitted re-entry. Unique players, current survivors, total entries and payout places are four different counts. Twenty paid re-entries do not mean twenty extra people are still in the tournament.

The $70 charge is 17.5% of the $400 total entry ($70 ÷ $400), or approximately 21.21% of the $330 ordinary prize contribution ($70 ÷ $330). The percentages use different denominators. They are not contradictory, but they must never be compared without naming the base. This event has no claimed table rake per hand; its fee is taken at registration under the stated fixture.

3. A genuine $50,000 finishing-pool guarantee changes the funding

Now change one assumption in the regular event: its fictional contract guarantees at least $50,000 in ordinary finishing-position prizes, explicitly excluding fees and bounties. The 120 entrants still contribute only $39,600. Under that expressly stated guarantee, the organizer must fund the shortfall from outside these entrants' contributions.

Overlay, under the hypothetical contract

Guaranteed ordinary pool $50,000 − collected ordinary contributions $39,600 = $10,400 overlay. Source of ordinary prize funds: $39,600 from paid entries plus $10,400 additional organizer funding. The $8,400 in entry fees remains a separate ledger line, not a deduction secretly made again from the guaranteed $50,000. Total modeled sources are $48,000 paid by entrants plus $10,400 from the organizer = $58,400; total uses are $50,000 ordinary prizes plus $8,400 fees = $58,400.

If an event's actual terms define the guarantee before an allowed deduction, or combine a finishing pool with bounties, a different calculation is required. Do not assert that every advertised $50,000 guarantee means $50,000 of ordinary cash payouts. Check the event's effective structure, addenda and final payout posting. An organizer's announced guarantee is not an individual's promised cash prize.

The event-selection guide shows which other structure facts to compare with that headline: registration timing, level duration, starting depth and continuation conditions. Accounting for the guarantee is one part of the decision to attend, not a ranking system for events.

The exact fictional break-even entry count is also checkable: 151 entries × $330 = $49,830, leaving $170 below the $50,000 ordinary-prize guarantee; 152 entries × $330 = $50,160, $160 above it. The first whole number of paid entries whose modeled contributions meet this particular ordinary-pool guarantee is therefore 152. This is a funding threshold, not a forecast of attendance or who will cash.

Do not calculate a regular-prize pool as 80 × $330 in this event. Its $330 non-fee component is split between two prize obligations. Also do not add another $8,000 to ordinary prizes merely because bounties are awarded during play. Under a 50/50 PKO rule, transferring part of a target's already funded $100 bounty to the eliminator's own head changes the claimant and timing, not the money originally collected. Whether a player receives cash now, a head marker grows or leftover markers are paid at the end depends on the event-specific rules. The cited historical knockout sheet even specifies rounding and a combined-pools guarantee; do not treat it as a generic exact-halves promise.

6. Two entries cost the individual twice, regardless of field size

Back in the fictional regular $400 event, one player pays for an initial attempt, is eliminated while a re-entry is permitted, and elects to buy one more. Both entries carry the original $330 + $70 allocation. This player's total entry payments are $800, with $660 contributing to the event's ordinary pool and $140 in fees. Those contributions do not become a personal payout or credit.

If the player later receives a hypothetical $950 gross finishing prize and separately paid $60 for travel and food, their simple pre-tax cash result is $950 − $800 − $60 = +$90. With zero prize receipts it would instead be −$860. No unused reserve is a loss; no outcome from the first entry changes the second entry's price. Other costs and the value of time are excluded from this deliberately narrow example.

A hypothetical person who used only one $400 entry, paid the same $60 trip costs and received the same $950 would have a different result: $950 − $400 − $60 = +$490. That is why a published gross cash, a prize pool, a remaining stack and an individual's net result are different measurements. For a detailed expense plan, use the complete tournament trip budget and the re-entry limit guide. Never infer Nolan's or another player's private finances from an event result listing.

7. Eight fast checks before trusting a tournament headline

An event lists $330 for prizes plus $70 in fees. Is the full entry $400 or $470?

$400. The fee is one of the two components already included in that amount. Check for any explicitly additional mandatory charges in the particular event's terms.

One hundred people enter, and twenty eliminated players each buy one re-entry. Which count funds the pool?

120 paid entries. With $330 per paid entry to prizes, 120 × $330 = $39,600. There are still 100 distinct people in this invented fixture, not 120.

What percentage is the $70 administrative fee of a $400 full payment?

17.5%. The denominator is $400. If instead you divide by only $330 of ordinary prize contribution, the ratio is approximately 21.21%. State which denominator you mean.

The fictional ordinary pool collects $39,600 but guarantees $50,000 of ordinary prizes. Who supplies the difference?

The hypothetical organizer supplies a $10,400 overlay. The event receives $48,000 from entrants and $10,400 in added funding; this does not make the $8,400 in fees part of the $50,000 guaranteed prize bucket.

Does a $50,000 headline guarantee always mean at least $50,000 in ordinary placements?

No. It does here only because the fictional contract says so explicitly. An actual guarantee might apply to combined prize buckets or allow specified deductions. Verify its exact scope before using the arithmetic.

Eighty knockout entries pay $230 to ordinary prizes, $100 to bounties and $70 to fees. How much enters each bucket?

$18,400 ordinary prizes, $8,000 initial bounty funding and $5,600 fees. The three total $32,000 in collected entry payments. Bounty transfers later do not create a second $8,000.

Is the ordinary $39,600 payout ladder automatically the same if a $50,000 ordinary guarantee is added?

No. In that alternate fixture there is $10,400 more to allocate. You need the new actual final payout ladder, not the one written for the $39,600 base pool.

A player receives $950 after paying two $400 entries and $60 in trip costs. Is the cash profit $550?

No. The modeled pre-tax result is +$90: $950 − $800 − $60. Subtract both paid attempts, not just the last one. The result changes if other costs or awards exist.

8. Reconcile the actual structure before you pay

  1. Identify the event: organizer, date, event number, flight, source URL and the applicable structure revision. Photograph or save the actual fee and payout terms for private reference.

  2. Record one attempt: full entry price = ordinary prize allocation + bounty allocation + administrative component + any separately stated compulsory components. If the advertised price excludes a required payment, record that separately.

  3. Count paid entries: distinguish unique players and re-entries. Multiply each applicable amount by the paid-entry count. Check the event's refund and void rules before assuming every registration contributed.

  4. Write the exact guarantee scope: ordinary prizes only, bounties only, combined pool, or something else? Compute an overlay only on the eligible base, after reading all permitted adjustments.

  5. Verify the final ladder: sum ordinary placement payouts and keep actual knockout cash or outstanding bounty obligations separately. Explain any differences before claiming a reconciliation.

  6. Keep your own ledger: paid entries, other charges and trip expenses, actual cash prizes, bounty receipts, credits or noncash tickets and unused reserves all get separate lines. Do not publish private statements or imply that a cash is a net profit.

The satellite bubble: why a good chip call can lose a seat

Three players remain. Two identical tournament tickets are awarded. You have ten big blinds, another player has ten, and a third player has just one. The other big stack shoves into you. You estimate that you win at showdown more often than you lose. Should you call? Winning chips is not the same objective as winning one of two tickets. This lesson reconstructs both answers from the same fully specified hand.

For after-play study only. All stacks, prizes, equities, ranges and actions below are fictional. The arithmetic is an independently constructed three-player Independent Chip Model (ICM) teaching exercise, not a solver result, a real satellite, an estimated opponent range or advice to play a particular hand. The toy event has exactly three remaining players, two identical seats, 0.5/1 BB blinds, no ante, no tie or split-pot outcomes, no fee, no bounty and no other payout. Stacks are before posting. The satellite stops as soon as only two players remain, awarding each survivor one identical seat. Actual event terms and the house's rules always govern.

1. Define exactly what qualifies as a win

A standard equal-seat satellite pays the same qualifying seat to each survivor at the cutoff. For this exercise there are two seats for the last two players; a player eliminated in third receives no seat. Once two remain, their relative chip counts do not affect how many tickets they receive. Compare the guide to tournament formats before assuming a ticket event follows these terms.

This particular model assigns each ticket one unit of seat value. An 80% modeled chance to qualify is 0.8 seat units, not a cash payment, not an 80% chance of winning the target tournament, and not an 80% guarantee. Some organizers instead award a single winner, chip-target or milestone seats, multiple ticket denominations, cash alongside tickets, or restricted entries. Different structures require different objectives and a different calculation.

Three stacks, two identical seats

Player A is the small blind with 10 BB before posting. Player B is you, the big blind with 10 BB before posting. Player C is the button with 1 BB before the hand and folds. There is no ante. A shoves all 10 BB in total; you have already posted 1 BB live and may fold or add 9 BB to call. Every player has enough chips to pay any assigned blind; no player is all-in from a short forced post.

There are 21 BB in the tournament. No chips appear or disappear during this one hand. To isolate this decision, the model excludes rake, deals, future blind changes, tie outcomes and transfers to other tables.

The one-blind player is an important part of the calculation even though they folded. If A and B play an all-in to completion, one of them is eliminated and C wins the second seat without gaining a chip. An ordinary cash-game pot-odds answer cannot price that effect.

You are being asked to add 9 BB, not 10, because the live big blind is already yours in the pot. The called pot is 11 + 9 = 20 BB. With no tie or other betting, the direct chip call price is 9 / 20 = 45%. That is the winning-probability threshold for chip expectation against folding now, not the threshold for one of the two tickets.

If you fold, you retain 9 BB. If you call, you finish with 20 BB when you win and zero when you lose. The extra 1 BB C holds never enters this pot. It remains relevant to the tournament payout, which is why the full tournament contains 21 BB while the called pot contains 20 BB.

3. Calculate the ticket chance after folding, from first principles

We use the conventional independent chip model. For three players with positive stacks and no ties, a player's modeled probability of finishing first is their chip share. To finish second, another player must first win; the target player then wins the conditional two-player race by chip share of the remaining stacks. We add the first-place and second-place probabilities because either result awards the same ticket.

After you fold, the stacks are A = 11, B = 9, C = 1. The total is 21. B is the player whose seat chance we calculate:

  1. B finishes first: 9 / 21 = 3 / 7, about 42.86%.

  2. A finishes first, then B second: (11 / 21) × (9 / 10) = 33 / 70, about 47.14%.

  3. C finishes first, then B second: (1 / 21) × (9 / 20) = 3 / 140, about 2.14%.

  4. B gets either seat: 3 / 7 + 33 / 70 + 3 / 140 = 129 / 140 ≈ 92.14%.

The fractions are deliberately left visible so the calculation can be audited. The two possible paths to second place are separate: if A finishes first, B competes with C's one chip; if C finishes first, B competes with A's eleven chips. Adding B's own first-place probability gives a model-based chance of taking either seat.

For a longer, four-player worked calculation with ordinary $50/$30/$20/$0 prizes, compare the separate 20 BB ICM call. Keep its payouts and pot out of this two-seat fixture.

This 92.14% is the model's evaluation of the post-fold stack snapshot. It does not simulate the next blind rotation or prove that folding now guarantees survival. ICM does not know future opponents' skill, ranges, positions, future all-ins or tournament-specific timing. Your actual fold choice is one hand in a changing event, not a promise that the stacks remain 11/9/1 forever.

4. A chip-profitable call can have negative seat equity

Let q be B's probability of winning the called all-in, conditional on A shoving, with no ties. It is an unknown parameter, not a claim about any specific two cards. If B wins, A is eliminated and B and C immediately receive the two tickets: B's seat value is one. If B loses, B is eliminated in third: B's seat value is zero. Therefore the modeled seat value of calling is exactly q × 1 + (1 − q) × 0 = q.

Folding leaves B with seat value 129 / 140. Calling breaks even with that snapshot only when q = 129 / 140 ≈ 92.14%. The 45% direct chip threshold and 92.14% equal-seat threshold answer different questions. Neither figure is a universal preflop calling range.

This is the article's central inversion: the invented 60% equity assumption makes the call better by three tournament chips than folding, yet worse by approximately 32.14 percentage points of expected ticket probability. The win assumption has not been measured, extracted from a chart or solved. A change to A's actual shove range, the presence of ties, the number of seats or any other player's stack requires new work.

Why do players sometimes shove much wider than they can call in satellite endgames? A shove can sometimes win when everyone folds; a call against an already committed player cannot create that same fold outcome. This particular table evaluates B's call only; it does not solve A's original shove. Read the separate 6–10 BB fold-equity example to see how an opener's outcome branches must be specified.

A single winner-only seat has no multi-seat bubble in this example, so the ICM threshold collapses to the 45% chip-share comparison. The ordinary 70/30 payout lands between that and the equal-seat case. This is why neither “satellite” nor “bubble” by itself specifies the correct threshold. The actual distribution of prizes is an input, not a footnote.

If a chart seems to disagree with this example, first check the chart's payout and action-tree assumptions. An ordinary chip-EV chart, a winner-only ticket model and a two-equal-seat ICM model are not interchangeable even with matching blind depth.

6. The one-blind player can benefit without seeing another card

If B folds, the full modeled chance of a seat is 33 / 35 for A, 129 / 140 for B and 19 / 140 for C. Those three fractions add to two complete seat units, which is exactly the number of tickets available. C's chance is about 13.57% after the fold, even though C has just one chip.

If A and B instead play the all-in to a no-tie finish, C becomes one of the last two players regardless of who wins. C's seat chance becomes 100%. That is an enormous modeled gain without an increase in chips. It also explains why a strong hand may be worth declining when two much larger stacks collide and a very short player remains.

But do not invert this into “the shortest stack should always fold” or “big stacks should never shove.” The short player must survive future forced bets and may need to risk chips. Coverage, positions, payout rules, opponent responses, and who can win a ticket when the hand ends all matter. A button shove that may win folds is not the same problem as a big-blind call that closes the action. 7. Eight questions you can answer before checking

Two identical seats, three remaining players. Does the chip leader get a larger ticket because they have more chips?

No. In this stated equal-seat format, each of the final two receives one identical ticket. Chip stacks influence estimated qualification probability before that cutoff, not the number of seats afterward.

A starts with 10 BB in the small blind and shoves to 10. B starts with 10 BB in the big blind. With no ante and C folded, how much more must B call?

9 BB. B's 1 BB live blind counts. A has 10 BB committed, B has 1 BB committed, so B faces an 11 BB pot and would make a 20 BB called pot.

B folds. What are the exact remaining stacks for A, B and C, and why?

A 11, B 9, C 1. A receives its unmatched 9 BB shove back and wins B's 1 BB blind; B loses only the blind already posted. The three stacks still sum to 21.

Under three-player conventional ICM, how can B with nine of 21 chips have more than a 90% chance of either of two identical tickets?

Because both first and second qualify. B's first-place probability is 3/7; second-place paths sum to 69/140. The total is 129/140 ≈ 92.14%, conditional on the post-fold state and the model.

With a stipulated 60% win chance in the all-in, what are B's expected chips from calling, and how does that compare with folding?

Calling yields 0.60 × 20 = 12 BB in expected chips; folding retains 9 BB. Thus calling gains +3 BB in chip expectation, with no ties in this exercise.

Does that same 60% call improve B's chance of taking either identical ticket?

No. Calling gives B 60% modeled seat value, because only a win qualifies. Folding leaves 129/140 ≈ 92.14%; the call loses about 32.14 percentage points of modeled qualification chance despite its chip gain.

Keep the action identical but change the prizes to $70 first, $30 second, $0 third. Is the break-even call threshold still 92.14%?

No. The standard ICM fold value is $627/14, the call-win value is $1430/21, and the no-tie break-even q is 1881/2860 ≈ 65.77%. A different prize ladder creates a different objective.

Why is this not a solved preflop calling range for your next satellite?

The hand, ranges, position, payout, stack distribution, future blind schedule, tie probability and event terms would need to match. The example supplies conditional ICM values and a free variable q, not the actual probability against a real shoving range or the strategy of any real event.

8. A practical worksheet for a real satellite review

  1. Identify the award: number of seats left, exact qualifying rule, whether tickets have the same value, any cash for nonqualifiers, milestone or winner-only exceptions and transfer or expiration rules.

  2. Rebuild the tournament state: all remaining player stacks, who covers whom, exact blind and ante payments, tables and seats, all-in and folded players, and the number of eliminations until tickets are awarded.

  3. Count the pot: distinguish the chips already posted from the incremental call, any uncalled wager returned and all pots open to each player. Write the no-tie chip threshold separately.

  4. Map the branches: fold state, call and win, call and lose, ties if possible, and, for a shove, each opponent-fold and opponent-call branch. Recalculate prize eligibility after each end state.

  5. State the model: exact ICM or other evaluator version, payout inputs, unknown opposing ranges, assumed fold frequencies and which future events are not represented.

  6. Save an honest conclusion: write what the model actually calculated, whether the comparison is to the post-fold snapshot, and what unknowns could reverse it. Do all software-aided study outside live play and follow the room's device rules.

Poker / Strategy & math

The first flop decision in a three-bet pot

The preflop pot is 21.5 big blinds and both players have 90 behind. The cutoff checks. Does the button check back, bet 5 BB, or bet 14 BB? Each action changes the price of continuing and the next street's stack-to-pot ratio. Work through two contrasting flops, then distinguish the arithmetic from the ranges you would need to choose a strategy.

Educational review away from live play. Every action, hand and board here is fictional. The model is six-handed no-limit hold'em cash with 0.5/1 BB blinds, 100 BB starting stacks, no ante, straddle, rake or fee. Sizes are examples for accounting, not solver prescriptions, observed opponent tendencies or recommended betting frequencies. No wager or purchase is necessary.

1. Reconstruct the pot before touching the flop

Fictional hand: cutoff versus button, 100 BB effective

Six players start with 100 BB each. Small blind posts 0.5 BB; big blind posts 1 BB. Under the gun and hijack fold. Cutoff opens to 2.5 BB total. Button three-bets to 10 BB total, adding 10 BB from a seat without a blind. Both blinds fold. Cutoff calls another 7.5 BB, making their total 10 BB. The result is one heads-up pot and no other contributors.

The flop pot is cutoff 10 + button 10 + folded small blind 0.5 + folded big blind 1 = 21.5 BB. Each continuing player has 90 BB behind. Effective stack-to-pot ratio, or SPR, is 90 / 21.5 ≈ 4.19. These are the same assumptions as the 10 BB branch of the preflop sizing lesson. If a blind calls, a player four-bets, or fees are taken, the following ledger no longer describes that hand.

Cutoff acts first on every postflop street, because the button has position. The cutoff can check or lead out with a bet. If the cutoff checks, the button can check behind or make a continuation bet, commonly shortened to c-bet. Raising preflop does not force a flop bet. A cutoff lead, sometimes called a donk bet, creates a different decision tree for the button and is not quietly included in the check-first examples below.

When the button checks back after the cutoff checks, the flop round ends: no player has wagered on the flop, so there is nothing to call. The pot stays 21.5 BB, both players retain 90 BB, and the cutoff acts first again on the turn. Checking can protect some hands from facing a raise immediately, but it also allows the next community card to arrive without charging the other player. Which consequence matters depends on both ranges.

2. The same preflop pot can land on two very different flops

The following flops are separate branches of the same fictional preflop setup, not successive streets. Read board texture first if dry, connected, rainbow or two-tone are unfamiliar.

Board A: A♣ 7♦ 2♠

Flop A

A♣7♦2♠

Unpaired, rainbow and disconnected. There are three different suits and few immediate straight possibilities. A player can hold a set or two pair, but no five-card flush is possible on the flop. This texture gives the cutoff and button fewer immediate draw types to consider than the connected example.

The button's illustrative A♥ K♥ has a pair of aces with a king kicker here. That describes one holding, not the strength of every hand in the button's three-betting range.

Board B: 9♠ 8♠ 7♦

Flop B

9♠8♠7♦

Unpaired, connected and two-tone. Two spades create immediate flush draws for a player holding two more spades; nearby ranks support straights, two pairs, sets and straight draws. For example, T♣ 6♣ makes a ten-high straight using 6-7-8-9-T. Whether a particular player actually reaches this flop with T6 is a separate preflop range question.

The same button holding A♥ K♥ is now only ace-high with no immediate flush draw. It has not acquired a pair merely because it was a strong preflop hand.

Your own hand and your range answer different questions. A♥ K♥ is a top-pair hand on Board A and an unpaired hand on Board B. Yet a player can hold an individually weak hand on a board where their whole range performs relatively well, or hold a strong hand where the opponent's range also has substantial equity. Do not translate “the three-bettor is strong preflop” into “bet every flop.”

3. If cutoff checks, count the check and two illustrative c-bets

Assume cutoff checks. For a clean comparison, allow the button to check, bet 5 BB (about 23.3% of the 21.5 BB pot), or bet 14 BB (about 65.1%). These are legal illustrative amounts, not evidence that either is an appropriate size on either board. The same ledger applies to both boards because the starting pot and stacks are identical.

Independent flop branches after cutoff checks, with no rake

Check back21.5 BB; no wager21.5 BB; no call needed90 BB90 / 21.5 ≈ 4.19
Bet 5 BB26.5 BB31.5 BB85 BB85 / 31.5 ≈ 2.70
Bet 14 BB35.5 BB49.5 BB76 BB76 / 49.5 ≈ 1.54

For the small bet, 21.5 + 5 = 26.5 BB is in the middle before cutoff decides. A call costs the cutoff 5 BB, creating 26.5 + 5 = 31.5 BB. Each player has invested 10 preflop and 5 on the flop, so each now has 100 − 15 = 85 BB remaining. For the larger bet, the analogous calculation is 21.5 + 14 + 14 = 49.5 BB; each has 100 − 10 − 14 = 76 BB behind.

A smaller bet changes the price differently from a larger bet. If cutoff calls 5, the pot grows by 10 on that street; if cutoff calls 14, it grows by 28. The larger size lowers the next street's SPR far more. It might extract more from some calls, lose more when raised or called by better hands, deny more equity to hands that fold, or drive away hands that could have called a smaller bet. Without response ranges, we cannot know which effect dominates.

The check branch is not a “zero-value” branch. It can preserve hands that would dislike a check-raise and allow the button to decide again on a later street. The bet branches are not a guaranteed “equity-denial” profit. A continuation bet is a descriptive action label, not proof that the cutoff will fold. See value, bluff and equity-denial purposes for the questions behind each bet.

4. What is the immediate price when cutoff faces the bet?

The cutoff sees the pot after the button's bet, but a call must also enter the final pot. In the artificial model where the hand proceeds directly to showdown, no more betting occurs, there is no rake, and ties are ignored, a call needs enough chance of winning to recover its price. This is the basic break-even price for a direct showdown, call / (pot after bet + call).

Break-even share under the direct-showdown model only
Button betPot facing cutoffCutoff callsFinal potCall / final pot
5 BB26.5 BB5 BB31.5 BB5 / 31.5 ≈ 15.87%
14 BB35.5 BB14 BB49.5 BB14 / 49.5 ≈ 28.28%

These percentages are not instructions to call with any draw. There are still two community cards to come, and future bets may make a hand pay again or force it to fold before seeing the river. Position and the opponent's possible bets influence whether equity can be realized. The numbers also assume no rake or ties. See the pot-odds workshop for the distinction between immediate price and a complete strategy.

5. A check-raise creates a third price, not a prediction of strength

Return only to the button bets 5 BB branch. Instead of calling, cutoff check-raises to 20 BB total on the flop. Cutoff checked first, the button bet 5, and now cutoff adds 20 on this street. Since the previous bet was 5, an ordinary minimum full raise is to 10 BB; a raise to 20 is legal with these stacks. This is a fictional action, not a suggested check-raise size or a forecast that the cutoff has one type of hand.

The check-raise creates a current pot of 21.5 + button 5 + cutoff 20 = 46.5 BB. Button already put in 5 BB and would owe 20 − 5 = 15 BB to call. If the button calls, the flop pot becomes 46.5 + 15 = 61.5 BB, and each player has 70 BB behind. Turn SPR would then be 70 / 61.5 ≈ 1.14. If the button instead folds, cutoff wins the existing pot and no call is added. A permitted further raise is a distinct branch requiring fresh accounting.

The button cannot check when facing the raise. Fold, call and a legal re-raise are the possibilities, constrained by stacks and applicable betting rules. A check-raise might represent a made hand, a strong draw, or a bluff in different models. Its name does not reveal cutoff's cards. Compare the independent first-flop check-raise exercise to practice action order without combining two different example pots.

6. Which board invites which strategic questions?

Range advantage describes how one player's whole possible hand distribution compares with another's over a board, often measured by overall equity. Nut advantage asks which range more often contains the strongest possible hands. They are not interchangeable. On a high-card disconnected flop, a three-bettor could plausibly hold many strong high-card combinations. On a lower connected flop, an opener's calling range could retain suited connectors, sets or two-pair combinations that interact closely with the board. Both statements depend on which hands were actually included preflop.

To avoid a false shortcut, do not assert that the button has a fixed numerical range advantage on A♣ 7♦ 2♠ or that the cutoff owns the nuts on 9♠ 8♠ 7♦ without defining both players' preflop distributions. For example, a cutoff call range excluding T6 cannot suddenly contain a T6 straight. On the other hand, the possibility of a hand being in a range is not proof that any opponent held it. Combination counting and the chart-reading checklist help make those assumptions visible.

7. Five self-checks with counted answers

After button three-bets to 10 and cutoff calls, why is the flop pot 21.5 BB and not 20 BB?

The folded blinds contribute 1.5 BB. Both surviving players have invested 10 BB, and the small blind's 0.5 plus big blind's 1 remain in the pot: 10 + 10 + 0.5 + 1 = 21.5 BB. No rake or ante was included.

Cutoff checks, button checks. Who acts first on the turn, and how big is the pot?

Cutoff acts first; the pot stays 21.5 BB. Both checked the flop, so no new chips were added. Each has 90 BB behind.

Button bets 14 into 21.5 and cutoff calls. What is the turn pot, and how much remains?

49.5 BB in the pot, 76 BB behind each. Add 21.5 + 14 + 14; subtract the 10 BB preflop and 14 BB flop contribution from each player's 100 BB starting stack. The turn SPR is 76 / 49.5 ≈ 1.54.

Cutoff check-raises a 5 BB c-bet to 20 BB total. How much more would button pay to call, and what would the pot become?

Button calls another 15 BB; pot becomes 61.5 BB. The raise made cutoff's flop contribution 20 BB, not 25. Button already contributed 5 BB on the flop, so the price is 20 − 5 = 15. The final pot after calling is 21.5 + 20 + 20 = 61.5 BB.

Can the 15.87% direct-showdown threshold prove that cutoff should call 5 BB with an unspecified hand?

No. It is the ratio 5 / 31.5 in a simplified no-rake, no-tie model with no further betting. A real flop call faces two later streets, future possible payments, position and uncertain opposing ranges. No particular hand equity or full strategy has been provided.

8. Capture the decision for a useful review later

  1. Fix the preflop scene: players, starting effective stacks, posted blinds and antes, raises stated as totals, who folded, and the exact flop pot.

  2. Record the flop: card ranks and suits, first actor, each check or bet, and whose turn it is. Identify which range assumptions remain guesses.

  3. Count every branch separately: pot before a bet, pot facing a response, additional amount owed, pot after a call, and each remaining stack.

  4. Revisit the plan: what changes on representative turn cards, what can check-raise, and how future actions affect an apparent pot-odds threshold.

  5. Audit your source: match any chart or solver reference to game type, positions, stack depth, rake, ranges and bet sizes. Do not import a tournament conclusion into a 100 BB cash model without qualification.

Make the review after play, subject to room and device policies. If the hand is emotionally charged, a recorded explanation can wait. The Decision Lab separates known action from hypotheses; safer-play resources offer support without a gambling account.

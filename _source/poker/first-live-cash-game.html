Before taking a seat, know the game, the permitted buy-in, the charges and your own stopping point. You do not need a tournament entry or a software subscription to understand a cash-game session. You can also decide not to play.

This guide is for: someone who knows basic hold'em but has not joined a live cash table. New to the betting sequence? Start with one complete hand. The preparation steps below are a suggested checklist, not a room's official rules.

Decide what you are prepared to spend.

Set a total gambling-spend limit and a leaving time before you enter the room. Keep transport, food and essential expenses outside that amount. A posted minimum buy-in is an entry condition, not advice about what you can afford. When the available game exceeds your limit, skip it rather than increasing the limit to fit the game.

Use the room's official information to check its hours, entry requirements and game listings. Ask what identification and membership procedures apply. A listing on a third-party directory is a research lead, not proof that a seat exists or that you are eligible to play. The player-protection guide explains how to distinguish operator claims from the applicable authority.

Ask the desk for the actual game, not just the blinds.

A useful opening question is: “I am new to this room. What hold'em cash games are running, what is the buy-in range, and how are the games charged?” Confirm the betting structure, number of seats, any ante or straddle, and whether the displayed range includes a match-the-stack option. Do not assume another room's conventions apply here.

Ask how the waiting list works, how your seat will be called and where to obtain chips. Get the assigned table and seat from staff rather than occupying an apparently empty chair. Before buying in, read the cash-game cost guide: a percentage, a cap, a fixed collection and a time charge are different inputs.

A fictional desk conversation

The board says “1/3 NLH.” You learn that this invented room allows 100 to 300 in chips and takes a charge from qualifying pots. That tells you more than “1/3,” but you still need the charge amount and trigger. A possible straddle also changes the betting context. None of these invented amounts are a recommendation or a quote for a real room.

Let the dealer settle the entry procedure.

Confirm your buy-in, check the chip denominations and ask when you may receive a hand. Posting requirements, waiting for the big blind, missed blinds and table changes depend on house rules. Do not move chips or take cards based on a rule remembered from a tournament.

One published example is PokerStars Live's cash-game rules. Its table-stakes rule ordinarily limits a pot to money in play at the start, with specified house-approved exceptions. It also sets its own buy-in, entry, seat-change and return-to-game procedures. These illustrate what to ask about, not a universal rulebook for your room.

Ask procedural questions without asking for strategy.

Wait for your turn and make your intended action clear. Before an unclear action, ask the dealer to clarify the amount facing you or the house procedure for announcing a raise. Leave chips countable, protect your cards, and let the dealer finish settling a pot before moving anything. For a disputed rule or an uncomfortable interaction, ask staff to assist instead of arguing with other players.

Keep other players' live hands and your strategic questions out of table discussion. PokerStars Live's published rules, for example, prohibit outside consultation during a hand. A phone policy is not permission to use an equity calculator or ask someone else how to act. Use this site away from play; the table-procedure guide offers more context.

Leaving does not require getting even.

Finish any hand you are involved in, then tell the dealer you are leaving and ask where to cash out. Do not remove part of a stack while continuing unless the house expressly permits the particular transaction. Rejoining or changing tables may carry buy-in obligations, so ask rather than treating a brief departure as a way to reset a stack.

You do not owe the table another orbit or a chance to win your losses back. Treat reaching your planned limit, being tired or feeling pressure as reasons to stop. Ask staff for help with a safe departure when needed. Gambling-support resources are available without completing this learning path.

Keep the money record separate from the hand review.

After leaving, record each buy-in, each top-up and the final cash-out. A fictional buy-in of 200 plus a top-up of 100 followed by a cash-out of 260 produces 260 - 200 - 100 = -40, not a 60 profit. Charges or tips already paid from the stack are already reflected in that difference; separately paid costs need their own line.

Then choose one completed hand worth understanding. Use positions instead of identifying other players, label uncertain amounts and record only what you actually know. The five-session study route can use a fictional hand instead of requiring another gambling session.

Does an advertised 100-BB game tell me the exact buy-in and all the rules?

No. It does not establish the room's limits, charges, straddle rules, entry procedure or your personal budget. Confirm those separately. There is no obligation to join.

Defending the blinds: price the call, then plan for the players

The button raises to 2.5 big blinds. The small blind and big blind see the same raise but owe different amounts to call. One may still face a squeeze; the other may close the betting round. The chips, the action order and the ranges matter more than a rule to “always defend your blind.”

1. Defend the decision, not the blind already posted

A blind is a forced contribution, and it stays committed when you fold. When someone raises, the decision is about the additional chips required to continue, not recovering the chips you already posted. In ordinary poker language, defending a blind means continuing against an attempted steal or open-raise, usually by calling or three-betting. Folding may still be the best response with some hands and against some ranges.

The small blind (SB) posts half a big blind in these examples; the big blind (BB) posts one. The button normally acts after the blinds on later betting streets. Therefore a blind caller usually plays out of position against the button after the flop, even though the big blind acted last in the initial preflop order. Read the position and stack guide if those terms are unfamiliar.

2. Button opens: the blinds do not face the same call

Fictional six-handed cash hand A

Blinds 0.5/1 BB; no ante or rake in the arithmetic. Everyone starts with 100 BB before posting, and each has enough chips for the raises shown. The first three seats fold. The button opens to 2.5 BB total. The small blind holds K♣ Q♣; the big blind holds 9♠ 8♠. These shown cards are teaching fixtures, not proposed calls or three-bets. Neither opponent's opening or continuing range is specified.

Before either blind responds, the pot is 4 BB: button 2.5 + small blind 0.5 + big blind 1. The small blind acts first. A button open is the first raise, so a new raise from either blind is a three-bet, not a four-bet.

The small blind's call is 2.5 − 0.5 = 2 BB, whereas the big blind's call after a small-blind fold is 2.5 − 1 = 1.5 BB. In the small-blind call branch, the big blind still has a turn and could raise. In the big-blind call branch, the small blind has already folded and no one can re-raise over the BB's call preflop. That is a meaningful difference even before considering who is in position after the flop.

For a matched re-raise comparison, take the two three-bet branches separately. If the SB three-bets to 9, the BB folds and the button calls another 6.5, the pot becomes 19 BB: 9 from each continuing player plus the folded big blind's 1. If the SB folds, the BB three-bets to 9 and the button calls another 6.5, the pot becomes 18.5 BB: 9 + 9 + the folded small blind's 0.5. This is accounting for two different histories, not a claim that either blind should use 9 BB.

To see how a different three-bet total changes the call price and flop SPR, compare the 8, 10 and 12 BB sizing examples with a fixed opener and stacks.

In the second branch, the big blind must act before the button after the flop. If the button instead four-bets, there is a new preflop decision. For that continuation and the minimum raise rule, see the counted three-bet and four-bet lesson.

3. What could change the defense choice?

Compare the range the button might open, the hand class you hold, the likely responses to a three-bet, the effective stacks, the fee structure and how play continues on later streets. A button may open differently against different blinds. Do not use “buttons always steal” as a substitute for observed behavior or a clearly stated model assumption.

4. Everyone folds to the small blind

In a separate six-handed 0.5/1 BB cash hand with 100 BB starting stacks and no ante or rake, everyone before the small blind folds. The pot contains 1.5 BB. The small blind can fold, complete the big blind or open-raise. The big blind has not voluntarily acted and remains entitled to a decision.

If the small blind raises to 3 BB and the big blind calls another 2 BB, the pot is 6 BB and each player has 97 BB behind, for SPR = 97 / 6 ≈ 16.17. Both players started with 100 BB; their existing blind posts count toward their 3 BB total contributions. If the small blind merely completes, the big blind may check to see a flop or raise, so completing does not guarantee a cheap flop.

When exactly two players are dealt in, the button also posts the small blind and acts first preflop, then last after the flop. At a six-handed table that folds to the blinds, the remaining two act in the same relative order for this hand, but the button is still the original button. It does not move to the small blind mid-hand. Check the heads-up seat distinction before studying a heads-up chart.

5. An open plus a caller changes the big blind's opportunity

Fictional six-handed cash hand B: a squeeze spot

Blinds 0.5/1 BB, no ante or rake, 100 BB starting stacks for all players. The first two players fold. The cutoff raises to 2.5 BB. The button cold-calls 2.5 BB. The small blind folds, leaving 0.5 BB in the pot. You are the big blind, with K♠ Q♠ and 1 BB already posted. Neither opponent's range or reaction to a three-bet is supplied.

The pot you are facing is 6.5 BB: cutoff 2.5 + button 2.5 + folded small blind 0.5 + your live big blind 1. Calling costs another 1.5 BB and creates an 8 BB three-way flop pot if no other action remains. Raising to 11 BB total would be a squeeze three-bet, since someone already opened and another player called. Eleven is an illustrative legal total, not a prescribed squeeze size.

Check the ledger: after your squeeze the pot is 11 + 2.5 + 2.5 + 0.5 = 16.5 BB. Each opponent who chooses to call owes 11 − 2.5 = 8.5 BB more. If both call, the flop pot is 3 × 11 + 0.5 = 33.5 BB and each continuing player has 89 BB behind. If only one calls and the other folds, the pot is 11 + 11 + 2.5 + 0.5 = 25 BB. These branches assume nobody four-bets.

The cutoff responds before the button after your squeeze. The button may still fold, call or re-raise, and a four-bet would reopen decisions for other eligible players. By contrast, if you just call the original open and both opponents remain, the preflop round closes at 8 BB, but you have two opponents and act first after the flop.

If both opponents fold, you take in the 16.5 BB pot, but the 11 BB you contributed is not a new profit. Your net gain is 2.5 + 2.5 + 0.5 = 5.5 BB before any deductions. This distinction prevents the common mistake of calling the whole final pot a return on the risk of squeezing.

A squeeze creates a new price for two ranges, not guaranteed fold equity. An opponent who cold-calls with a strong hand, a short opener who might shove, or a caller likely to continue can change the available lines. 6. Tournament big-blind antes change the pot, not the live call credit

Keep the button-open example but switch to a six-handed tournament where the big blind posts a 1 BB big-blind ante as well as the 1 BB live blind. Suppose the button opens to 2.5 BB, the small blind folds, and the big blind has posted both forced contributions in full. The pot before the big blind's decision is 5 BB: button 2.5 + small blind 0.5 + big blind 1 + big-blind ante 1. Calling still costs 1.5 BB, because the ante is not credit toward the live wager. If BB calls and the action closes, the pot becomes 6.5 BB.

If the big blind began with 25 BB before posting, the 1 BB live blind and 1 BB ante leave 23 BB behind before responding. Calling another 1.5 leaves 21.5 BB behind. A no-ante 25 BB cash hand has different pot and stack accounting. The actual event may have short-stack ante priority rules, so do not use this fully funded illustration to rule on a player who cannot pay both contributions.

Tournament decisions also depend on effective stacks, who covers whom, payouts and bounty structure. A chip-EV range does not automatically transfer to a bubble or satellite. Continue with short-stack tournament decisions and tournament equity. 7. Four blind-defense checks

Button opens to 2.5 BB. Why does SB call 2 BB while BB calls 1.5 BB after SB folds?

Because each live blind already counts. SB posted 0.5 BB, so 2.5 − 0.5 = 2. BB posted 1 BB, so 2.5 − 1 = 1.5. The SB call still leaves the BB to act; the BB call after SB folds completes preflop action.

Everyone folds to SB, who completes. Can BB be forced to check?

No. In the ordinary 0.5/1 BB example, SB adds 0.5 to complete and the pot becomes 2. BB may check or raise. A completion is a call of the live blind, not an agreement to see the flop without further betting.

In the squeeze hand, one opponent calls your raise to 11 BB and the other folds. Is the flop pot 22 or 25 BB?

25 BB. The two continuing players each have 11 in front of them; the folded cold-caller or opener left 2.5, and the small blind left 0.5. The total is 11 + 11 + 2.5 + 0.5 = 25 BB.

With a 1 BB big-blind ante, does BB receive 2 BB of credit toward a button open to 2.5?

No. Only the 1 BB live blind offsets the 2.5 BB total to call. The extra 1 BB ante is in the pot but is not a live wager; BB still owes 1.5 BB to call. See the event's rules for short-posting priority and unusual all-ins.

8. Copy a complete blind-defense record after play

  1. Set the table: seats dealt in, button, format, blinds, antes, known rake or time charge, and every player's stack before forced bets.

  2. Write the action: every fold, limp, open, cold-call and raise to a total. Do not skip a caller because you are focused on the opener.

  3. Separate live and dead chips: blind credit, antes, folded contributions, extra chips to call and the exact current pot.

  4. List players still able to act: can someone overcall, squeeze, four-bet or reopen action? Who acts first after the flop?

  5. State a range hypothesis and its evidence: what is observed and what is guessed? Compare how conclusions shift if the opener's or caller's range is different.

  6. Recheck the model: same seat configuration, stack depth, rake, ante, allowed sizes and tournament objective before taking a chart result as relevant.

Study one branch at a time rather than remembering only whether a blind defense won. If you want a broader decision framework, start with responding to preflop raises.

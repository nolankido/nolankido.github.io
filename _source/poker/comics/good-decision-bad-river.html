A result is not a verdict on the decision

A good decision can lose, and a poor decision can win. Review what you knew when you acted: the pot, the price of calling, the possible hands your opponent could hold and the action so far. The river adds information you did not have on the turn.

The comic describes the opponent’s win as a “4% longshot,” but the artwork does not establish a complete, unambiguous hand or bet sizes. Treat that percentage and the claim that the call was correct as premises of this fictional story, not a verified calculation. Whether a real call is sound depends on the actual situation.

Do not dismiss every loss as bad luck. A useful review can uncover a mistake, support your original reasoning or leave a question unresolved. Taking a break does not require proving that you played perfectly.

The comic in words

  1. The turn decision

    The player faces a big decision and says, “Call.” The story presents the call as correct based on the math and the information available at that moment.

  2. The river hurts

    A nine arrives on the river. The opponent announces a straight with eight-seven. The story describes this as an unlikely outcome that beats the caller.

  3. Two competing thoughts

    One thought says, “I lost, so I played badly!” Another asks, “What did I know when I decided?” The player begins separating the outcome from the reasoning behind the call.

  4. A break instead of chasing

    The player leaves the table with a sigh. Rather than trying to win the money back immediately, he gives himself space to settle down.

  5. Review the play

    Sitting on a bench, he takes a breath and revisits the turn decision. In the story, he concludes that the call was sound despite the bad river.

  6. End the session thoughtfully

    The closing lesson: a winning result does not prove a good decision, and a losing result does not prove a mistake. The player decides to go home and rest.

Review the hand before judging yourself

Write down the action and your reasoning before focusing on the river. What price were you offered? What hands could your opponent reasonably hold? What assumption would change your decision? If you cannot reconstruct those details, record the uncertainty instead of forcing a verdict.

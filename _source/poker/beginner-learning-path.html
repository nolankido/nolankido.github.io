Build the basics in four connected lessons.

Before the five study sessions below, use this route to connect the rules to a decision. It is for standard 52-card no-limit Texas hold'em. Start with the first unfamiliar skill, read its lesson, then return for the quick check. Say why your answer works before revealing the explanation. The checks identify what to revisit; they are not a score or a certificate.

Use a deck, counters or paper. Every new scene here is fictional, and no wager, account or purchase is needed. You can pause anywhere. Already comfortable with the basics? Go to the five study sessions.

  1. Cards, actions and the pot

    Learn: read your two cards, distinguish checking from calling, find the player whose turn it is and compare the best five cards. The lesson finishes with a complete hand whose chips reconcile.

    Before moving on: explain what you owe now, rather than repeating the last number announced. Use only your contribution on this betting round when counting a call.

    Quick check: You posted a big blind of 2 and face a raise to 7. How much more is a call?

    5 more: 7 - 2 = 5. Assume you have enough chips, there is no ante and you made no other preflop contribution. Your posted 2 is already part of this street's wager. Calling brings your preflop total to 7; it does not add a new 7. On a later street, those earlier chips would not pay for a fresh bet.

    If you counted 7 or carried a previous street's payment forward, revisit counting the new chips, then explain the difference again without the answer open.

  2. Your starting hand in context

    Learn: describe position, earlier action, effective stacks and the price before asking whether two cards are worth playing. A hand's label leaves out most of the decision.

    Before moving on: distinguish information you observed from an assumption about hidden cards. Name who still has a turn, including a player who may raise after your call.

    Quick check: At a six-player table, three players fold before you on the button. Are you the last player to act preflop?

    No. The small blind and big blind still have their initial decisions. You have seen three folds, which gives you more information than acting first. It does not reveal either blind's cards or prevent a raise. If you reach a flop against a blind, the button acts after that blind in the initial postflop order. Being later preflop and having position after the flop are related, but not identical.

    If the order was unclear, revisit counting who still has a turn. Draw six seats and move a marker through the action.

  3. Your first decisions after the flop

    Learn: name the made hand and the draw separately, read checks and raises without inventing the opponent's cards, and notice when a helpful shared card improves the other hand too.

    Before moving on: explain both what your cards make now and what they still need. A possibility to improve is different from a completed hand or a guaranteed win.

    Quick check: You hold ace-nine of hearts on a flop of nine of spades, six of hearts and two of hearts. Pair, flush, or draw?

    A pair of nines with an ace kicker, plus a heart-flush draw. Your current best five are 9-9-A-6-2. There are four hearts among your five available cards, so one more heart would complete a flush. You do not already have one. An opponent could have a stronger made hand, and completing your draw does not guarantee that you win.

    If you chose only one label, revisit made hands and draws. Lay out the five cards and count the hearts once each.

  4. The turn, river and showdown

    Learn: rebuild the hand when a new card arrives, recognize that a missed draw can still have showdown value, and distinguish calling a bluff from making one. On the river there is no further community card to rescue a draw.

    Before moving on: separate the price of a call from evidence that your hand meets it. Say when the action is complete and whether you are counting outright wins or a share of the pot.

    Quick check: A heads-up river pot is 30 before a bet of 10. Does a 20% call price prove you should call?

    No. The arithmetic gives the price, not your hand's equity. Assume a fictional cash-game hand, no fees or side pots, enough chips to call and a call that ends betting. The bet makes the current pot 40. Your additional 10 makes 50, so the break-even share is 10 / 50 = 20%. You still need a justified model of the opponent's hands and your expected share, including ties. This does not compare calling with every possible raise.

    If you divided by 40 or treated the price as a measured win chance, revisit the river-call explanation.

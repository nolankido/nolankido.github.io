1. Four hole cards do not let you use any five cards

In standard four-card Omaha high, each player makes the best possible five-card high hand with exactly two of their four private cards and exactly three community cards. On the flop there are six hole-card pairs to test: 4 choose 2. On a five-card river board there are ten choices of three board cards: 5 choose 3. That is 60 legal five-card candidates per player at showdown, before comparing their ranks. The official PokerStars Omaha rules and WSOP Online Omaha rules explain the construction and pot-limit framework.

Do not borrow four board spades

Hole cards: A♠ K♦ Q♣ J♥. Board: 2♠ 7♠ 9♠ J♠ 3♦. Hold'em intuition may suggest an ace-high flush using A♠ and four spades on the board. In Omaha high, that would use one hole card and four board cards, which is illegal. This hand has only one spade among its four hole cards, so it cannot make a spade flush. The legal best five must still select two hole cards.

To check a PLO draw, begin by identifying one legal pair of hole cards and three compatible board ranks. Do not silently fill a gap with a third hole card. A draw that needs three of your private ranks to make its five-card result is not an Omaha straight draw. For the high/low split game, use the separate Omaha hi-lo workshop; it includes low qualifications and quartered pots that do not apply here. The game comparison explains other format differences.

2. A fictional wrap: J♥ T♣ 4♦ with Q♠ 9♠ 8♥ 7♥

Four private cards: Q♠ 9♠ 8♥ 7♥. Flop: J♥ T♣ 4♦. No made straight is possible yet. The flop is rainbow, and neither a flush nor straight is currently complete. We ask only one narrow question: which physical turn cards let the hero make any straight using exactly two hole cards and three board cards?

The 4♦ is not used in any listed straight, but it is still a known card removed from the deck. Notice also that a seven completes a lower straight than a king. 3. What changes when an opponent's hand is visible?

For a different, fully exposed version of this same flop, suppose an opponent holds K♦ Q♦ 5♣ 5♠. Those four cards are unavailable as turn cards, including K♦ and Q♦ from the original straight-completion list. The other two exposed fives were not in that list. Now 52 − 4 hero − 3 flop − 4 opponent = 41 unknown turn cards remain.

Blockers change both sides of the fraction

Original information set: 16 / 45 = 35.56% to complete a straight on the turn. With all four opponent cards exposed: (16 − 2) / (45 − 4) = 14 / 41 = 34.15% rounded. The removed straight-completing cards are specifically K♦ and Q♦. This is conditional card-removal arithmetic, not the probability of winning against K♦ Q♦ 5♣ 5♠.

When an opponent's hole cards are not exposed, you cannot remove a particular hidden king or queen as though you saw it. Strategic blockers inform a range hypothesis, but that requires additional evidence and conditioning. Distinguish known dead cards from a guess about a rival's range. For counting other relevant combinations, see the range-combination guide and probability under uncertainty.

4. Your straight arrives. Nine clubs and three queens threaten it, but one card overlaps

Reset the opponent's cards. Keep the hero Q♠ 9♠ 8♥ 7♥ and flop J♥ T♣ 4♦, then deal the 8♣ turn. The board is now J♥ T♣ 4♦ 8♣. The hero has a queen-high straight, using Q♠ 9♠ with J♥ T♣ 8♣. The 8♥ in the hero's hand is not a third hole card used in the straight.

At this closed-action, face-up snapshot, suppose the opponent's actual four cards are A♣ K♣ 9♦ 2♦. The opponent does not yet have a flush. They may use A♣ K♣ with three board clubs if a club arrives. They can also make Broadway on any remaining queen, using A♣ K♣ with Q-J-T on the board. All eight private cards and four board cards are specified; there are 52 − 12 = 40 unknown rivers.

An exhaustive check of the 40 legal rivers gives 11 opponent wins, 29 hero wins and zero ties. With both players' hands known and no more action, that means 11 / 40 = 27.5% for the opponent, 29 / 40 = 72.5% for the hero. That probability applies only to these two complete exposed holdings after the turn; it is not a pre-turn equity, a range estimate, or a statement that calling a bet is profitable.

Why not 12 opposing outs? Q♣ is simultaneously a club and a queen, so adding the two lists would count one physical river twice. Also, the same river card can change the hero's legal best five, which is why the correct check evaluates both players' best five-card constructions on every river. 5. An exact card count is not a complete pot-limit decision

  1. Choose the event you are calculating. “Makes any straight on the turn,” “beats a specific revealed hand by the river,” and “wins against a range after future action” are three different questions. Label the event and street.

  2. Use exactly two hole cards and three board cards. For a river showdown, compare all 60 legal choices for each player. On the flop or turn, use the actual available board combinations rather than a hold'em five-of-seven shortcut.

  3. Name known card removal. Hero's four cards and the visible board are known. Remove exposed rival cards only when the hypothetical information set actually includes them. Do not assume an unseen card is dead.

  4. Deduplicate overlapping threats. If the same queen of clubs creates a straight and a flush, it is still one river card. An opponent's higher straight or full house can turn a “made my hand” card into a losing outcome.

  5. Then address price and action. PLO has a pot-size maximum raise calculation; a 120-chip pot facing a 90-chip bet allows a maximum total raise to 390 when no chips have yet been put into this street by the raiser: call 90, creating 300, then raise a further 300. This is betting-size arithmetic, not a strategic recommendation or a procedural instruction for physically making the raise. See the pot-limit math reference, price-of-a-call workshop and multiway pot lesson for the additional decision inputs.

Even with every opposing hand exposed, a turn-to-river showdown percentage does not account for future bets if action remains open. And if it is a tournament, chips and prize-equity value can diverge. See tournament equity before transferring a simple chip-price calculation to a bubble or payout decision.

6. Test the rule before revealing the count

With A♠ K♦ Q♣ J♥ and four board spades, do you have a spade flush?

No. A♠ is the only spade in your hole cards. Omaha high requires two hole cards, and four board cards cannot be used in the same five-card hand.

On J♥ T♣ 4♦ with Q♠ 9♠ 8♥ 7♥, which five turn ranks complete a straight?

K, Q, 9, 8 and 7. The exact remaining physical counts are 4, 3, 3, 3 and 3. One legal example for each appears in the table above.

Why are there 45 possible turns when only your four hole cards and the flop are visible?

There are 52 − 4 − 3 = 45 unknown physical cards. Unknown opponents' cards remain part of this information set's unseen-card distribution rather than being removed by name.

The rival exposes K♦ Q♦ 5♣ 5♠. Why is it 14 of 41 instead of 14 of 45?

Four more cards are known, reducing the unseen set to 41. Two of them, K♦ and Q♦, were straight-completing cards, reducing that numerator to 14.

The turn is 8♣. Which two hole cards actually make the queen-high straight?

Q♠ 9♠ plus board J♥ T♣ 8♣. The other private cards, 8♥ and 7♥, cannot be added as extra straight cards.

Against A♣ K♣ 9♦ 2♦ on that turn, does Q♣ count as one winning river or two?

One. Q♣ completes the opponent's club flush and Broadway, but only one physical card can fall on the river.

What are the exact face-up river outcomes, with both four-card holdings known?

11 opponent wins, 29 hero wins, zero ties from 40 legal rivers. The opponent's winning rivers are nine surviving clubs or three surviving queens, with Q♣ subtracted once for the overlap.

If 16 turn cards can make a straight, is my chance to win the pot 16/45?

Not necessarily. It is the probability of a legal straight being completed on the next card in that specific hero-and-flop information set. Winning needs an opposing hand or range, legal comparisons, remaining streets and any applicable future action.

Another caller adds money to the pot and another hand you must beat. Those changes happen together. A cheaper-looking call is not proof that the decision has become more profitable.

Reconstruct the money before estimating equity.

Fictional river: 90 chips are in the pot. A bets 30, B calls 30, and C closes the action by calling 30. With no rake or side pots, the final pot is 180. C's additional call is 30, so its break-even expected pot share is 30 / 180 = 16.67%. If B had folded instead, the final pot after C's call would be 150 and the threshold 20%.

The lower threshold does not answer whether C's hand reaches it. B's call is new information about a competing range. Compare the hand against both ranges and the actual distribution of possible awards, including ties. Counting the money is exact; assigning those ranges is an assumption.

A player behind you changes the question.

If C has not acted and you are deciding in B's seat, the final pot is not known yet. C could fold, call or raise. Treating C's future call as guaranteed understates what can happen next. Write the current action order alongside the price rather than only recording an attractive fraction.

On an earlier street, opponents may bet again even after everyone calls now. An equity calculator that assumes a free showdown does not automatically value that future decision tree. GTO Wizard's original multiway discussion is useful context for closing action and shared defense, but its heuristics are not mandatory frequencies for an unknown table.

You cannot win a side pot you did not enter.

Fictional all-ins: A contributes 50, B contributes 200 and C contributes 200. There are no other contributions or deductions. The main pot is 3 × 50 = 150. The side pot is 2 × 150 = 300. A can win the 150 main pot; only B and C can win the side pot. The two awards total 450.

A's chance of having the best hand against everyone is not the only calculation B needs. B could lose the main pot to A and still beat C for the larger side pot. Evaluate each eligible pot against the players contesting it, then add expected awards. The side-pot lab shows refunds, eligibility and chip conservation in more detail.

A dry side pot starts with nothing extra to win.

When one player is all-in and the remaining players have not put additional unmatched-by-the-all-in-player chips into a separate pot, that side pot is dry. It contains zero. A bet may make another live player fold, but the all-in player remains eligible for the main pot and cannot be bluffed out of it.

That observation is not a universal instruction to check. A bet can have value, protection or other effects. It does mean that “I can win everything by making the other player fold” is wrong while the all-in player still contests the main pot. Keep the pot you are trying to win explicit.

Do not give every opponent the same heads-up quota.

A pure bluff into two opponents must get the required combination of folds. The relevant event is both players folding, not either player's individual fold rate. In a deliberately artificial independent model, two 50% fold rates yield 0.50 × 0.50 = 25% joint folds, not 50%.

In actual sequential action, use conditional probabilities: P(A folds) multiplied by P(B folds given A folded). The second player's response can change after seeing the first action, and card removal links their possible hands. Assuming independence without checking it creates false precision. A heads-up minimum-defense number is not a quota to impose on every player in a multiway pot.

Finish with a record that supports a comparison.

Write the action order, relevant stacks, contributions, pot eligibility and possible further actions. Separate a made hand from its ability to improve to the best hand; a draw can complete and still lose. State which range changes would reverse the conclusion. Use the study calculation desk only for its stated accounting tasks, not as a multiway solver.

For study, change one input at a time. Move the caller to a seat that closes action, remove a side pot or alter a stated range. Explain which conclusion changes and why. This creates a checkable lesson without pretending one fictional example gives a universal strategy.

Check your understanding.

Does another caller automatically make your call better?

No. It changes both the price and the competing range. The lower required pot share may still exceed your hand's actual expected share.

With contributions 50, 200 and 200, can the short stack win 450?

No. Without other contributions, the short stack is eligible for the 150 main pot, not the 300 side pot.

Are two players' fold rates always independent?

No. Use the joint event and conditional responses. The 25% example explicitly assumed independence.

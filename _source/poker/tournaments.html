The stack is not a cash balance

In an ordinary tournament, the entry purchases a starting stack and the payout structure determines the cash awards. The chips are units for playing the event, not dollars that can be cashed out at their printed value. Do not read a 100,000-chip win as $100,000.

Fictional result: a cash is not always a profit

Two entries at a total price of $300 each cost $600. A $450 payout leaves $450 − $600 = −$150 before travel and other expenses. A video saying “I cashed” describes a payout, not necessarily a profitable trip.

The same stack can become shallower

A level sets the blinds and usually an ante arrangement for a period of play. With a 24,000 stack and a 1,000 big blind, the stack is 24 BB. If the big blind becomes 1,500 and the stack has not changed, it is now 16 BB. No chips needed to disappear for the BB number to fall.

An ante is separate from the live blind. Under a stated 500/1,000 structure with a full 1,000 big-blind ante, the initial pot is 2,500. The big blind's 1,000 blind counts toward a call; its additional 1,000 ante does not. Other events may use individual antes, reduced antes, or none. Always read the actual structure.

Average stack is a comparison, not a payout or an instruction. If 1,200,000 chips remain among 40 players, the average is 30,000. That tells you neither the median stack nor how much the opponents at this table have. Their effective stacks matter for a particular hand.

The bubble changes what is at stake

In a fictional standard event paying 20 places, 21 remaining players means one more elimination reaches the paid positions, subject to the event's handling of simultaneous eliminations. “In the money” means qualifying for a payout. A pay jump is the difference between two finishing-position awards.

On or near a payout threshold, a tournament may use hand-for-hand play: tables coordinate hands and wait for the others to finish. A single hand can take much longer to resolve across the room than in the final video edit.

Chip counts alone cannot specify an exact cash value. In a fictional $100 prize pool paying $50/$30/$20, collecting every chip produces the $50 first prize, not ownership of the entire $100. Commentary about ICM, the Independent Chip Model, concerns modeling tournament value rather than simply counting chips. This guide does not supply ICM calculations or a blanket rule to fold.

Listen for the event format

Common format labels, not the rules of any particular event
LabelWhat changes
FreezeoutOne entry; elimination ends that attempt with no re-entry.
Re-entryA new paid entry may be allowed after elimination during a specified window.
Rebuy / add-onAn event may permit buying more chips under its own timing and eligibility conditions.
SatellitePrizes are often seats or tickets to another event rather than an ordinary cash ladder.
BountyEliminations can earn separate awards. Eligibility, coverage, and the prize mechanism depend on the format.
Flight / baggingA flight is one starting session; bagging means carrying chips to a later stage, not necessarily reaching the money.

“Entries” may count repeated attempts by the same person. “Players remaining” counts active stacks at that point. “Day 2,” “final table,” and “in the money” describe different milestones; never assume they happen together.

A stack stays at 24,000 when the big blind rises from 1,000 to 1,500. What changes?

It falls from 24 BB to 16 BB, even though the chip count is unchanged. Both the stack and the current blind level are needed to interpret the situation.

Does winning a seat in a satellite mean first place must collect every chip?

No. In a typical equal-seat satellite, play can end when the remaining players all qualify for seats. The published prize structure determines the stopping condition.

Does bagging chips guarantee a payout?

No. It means advancing with chips to a later stage. Whether that stage is already in the money depends on the event structure.

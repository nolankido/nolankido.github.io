1. You face a raise to eight

You posted the big blind of 2. On this same preflop round, another player raises to a total of 8. You have enough chips, made no other contribution, and there are no antes. Which explanation describes the extra chips required to call?

  1. Add 8 because that is the amount announced.
  2. Add 6 because your posted 2 already counts toward the 8.
  3. Add 4 because a call always costs two big blinds.
Reveal answer 1 and compare the three explanations

B: add 6. Calling brings your contribution on this betting round to 8: 8 - 2 = 6. The posted big blind already belongs to this round. A adds a fresh 8 and would bring your total to 10. C imports a fixed-size rule that was never supplied; a call depends on the actual wager and your contribution.

If your reason differed: revisit counting the new chips. Draw two boxes, “already paid this round” and “still to add.” Do not move old preflop chips into the next street's box.

Changed situation: You paid 8 preflop. On the flop, the opponent makes the first bet of 5, and you have contributed nothing on this new round. How much is a call now? Explain why the earlier 8 does or does not reduce it.

Reveal retry 1

Add 5. Your earlier 8 is already in the pot from preflop. It does not pay for a new flop bet. Your contribution on the current round is zero, so the amount still owed is 5 - 0 = 5. The rule to carry forward is about the current betting round, not the total you have spent in the hand.

2. You call from the button

At a six-player table, the first two players fold. The cutoff, the seat immediately before the button, raises to 6. You are on the button and call. The small blind and big blind have not made their initial decisions. Which explanation fits?

  1. Your call completes preflop because the button is always last.
  2. Your call completes preflop because you matched the raiser's total.
  3. The blinds still have decisions; either could change the action.
Reveal answer 2 and compare the three explanations

C: the blinds still have decisions. Calling matches the current wager for you. It does not act on behalf of the small blind or big blind, and a later raise could give earlier callers another decision. A confuses the button's initial postflop position with the initial preflop order. B counts only your chips and ignores the other players whose turns are pending.

If the order was unclear: revisit position and players still to act. Draw six seats and move a marker through their turns. Say which players remain instead of relying on “I am late.”

Changed situation: Only you on the button and the big blind reach the flop. The big blind checks, then you check. Does the big blind receive another turn merely because you acted after them?

Reveal retry 2

No. This flop betting round is complete. Both remaining players checked without a wager. Your check did not create a new decision for the big blind. This differs from the earlier preflop scene, where the blinds had not yet taken their initial turns. The street and the remaining action matter together.

3. Four spades are visible to you

You hold king of spades and queen of spades. The flop is queen of clubs, eight of spades and three of spades.

Your cards: K♠Q♠

Flop: Q♣8♠3♠

Which description separates what you have now from what you could make?

  1. A pair of queens with a king kicker, plus a spade-flush draw.
  2. A completed flush because both private cards are spades.
  3. A flush draw, so there is no made hand to name.
Reveal answer 3 and compare the three explanations

A: a pair and a draw. Your current best five are Q-Q-K-8-3. Four of your five available cards are spades. A flush requires five cards of the same suit, so B counts too few. C overlooks the pair of queens that is already present. A made hand and a draw can describe the same holding.

If you named only one: revisit made hands and draws. Put the current five-card hand beside the card you still need. Completing a draw and winning the pot are separate questions.

Changed situation: The turn is the five of diamonds. Name your made hand and the spade draw again. Did receiving a fourth community card itself complete the flush?

Reveal retry 3

You still have a pair of queens and a spade-flush draw. There are still only four spades among your six available cards. Your best five are now Q-Q-K-8-5; the five replaces the three as the lowest kicker. A new community card can change the best five without completing the draw you hoped for. One river card remains.

4. The river pot was twenty-four

A fictional heads-up cash-game river pot contains 24 before the opponent bets 8. You have enough chips to call, and a call ends betting. Assume no fees, side pots or tournament payout effects. Which statement is justified?

  1. 8 / 32 = 25%, which proves a call is good.
  2. 8 / 40 = 20% is the break-even pot share; your hand's share still needs a model.
  3. The opponent bet one third of the pot, so you win one third of the time.
Reveal answer 4 and compare the three explanations

B. The opponent's bet makes the current pot 32. Your additional call of 8 makes the final pot 40, so 8 / 40 = 20%. A leaves your call out of the final prize and then turns a price into a recommendation. C turns a relative bet size into a win probability without any information about hidden hands.

If the denominator or conclusion differed: revisit pricing a river call. Keep “What does a call cost?” and “What share can this hand expect?” on separate lines. Include ties when estimating a share.

Changed situation: The pot is 30 before a river bet of 15 under the same assumptions. What is the new break-even share? What information is still missing even after the arithmetic?

Reveal retry 4

15 / 60 = 25%. The current pot is 45, and your call makes 60. The threshold changed because the bet is now half the original pot rather than one third. You still need a justified model of the opponent's hands and your expected pot share. Neither 20% nor 25% here is a measured chance that your hand wins.

5. A check and two possible aces

You hold A♥ Q♦, ace of hearts and queen of diamonds, on Q♠ 9♣ 6♥ 4♦ 2♣. These codes name exact ranks and suits: spades ♠, hearts ♥, diamonds ♦ and clubs ♣. The opponent checks. Which explanation fits the information you have?

  1. A♥ K♥ remains possible because ace-king is a common hand name.
  2. The check proves the opponent has no hand stronger than your queens.
  3. A♣ K♦ is physically possible, but the check does not establish its frequency.
Reveal answer 5 and compare the three explanations

C. The opponent's A♣ K♦ would reuse none of your known cards. That permits the exact holding; it does not say how often the opponent reaches this point or checks with it. A reuses your ace of hearts, so that exact holding is impossible. B reads hidden strength from a check. For example, the legal Q♥ Q♣ would make three queens and beat your pair, while still being able to check.

If a possible hand became a fact: revisit known cards and possible hands. Make two headings, “Observed” and “Assumed.” Put the actual cards and check in the first; put candidate hands and frequencies in the second.

Changed situation: At showdown, the opponent reveals A♣ K♦ once. Does this prove that an earlier equal-weight range model was correct, or establish how often the opponent checks ace-king?

Reveal retry 5

Neither. You now know the opponent's holding in this one fictional hand. Models assigning different probabilities to ace-king can both allow that result. One reveal does not determine the probabilities across other hands. Keep the observed result, and keep the earlier frequency assumption visibly separate.

6. You have a pair and can bet

You hold J♣ T♦ on T♠ 8♥ 5♣ 3♦ 2♠. The opponent checks to you last to act on the river. Your current best five are T-T-J-8-5. Which explanation gives a useful next study question?

  1. Which worse hands would call this size, which better hands would continue, and how does betting compare with checking?
  2. A pair is enough to justify a bet without considering any response.
  3. If the opponent eventually folds, the bet was necessarily better than checking.
Reveal answer 6 and compare the three explanations

A. Your hand's label is only one input. A value-bet explanation needs worse hands that actually call, and it must account for stronger continuations. B skips those responses. C judges a decision from one ending and ignores whether the same pot might have been won by checking.

If winning the pot became the whole argument: revisit worse hands that actually call. Say what your bet is trying to get from which hands, then compare that with the check. You can identify a missing response assumption without claiming to know the best action.

Changed situation, optional arithmetic: In a restricted teaching model the pot is 20 and both players have 100 behind. The only opposing alternatives are 9♣ 9♦ and 8♣ 8♦, each with probability one half at this decision. Against a bet of 6 the worse nines always fold and the better eights always call. There are no raises, fees or side pots. A check ends betting. Compare the average chips received minus new chips paid for betting and checking.

Reveal retry 6

Checking averages 10; betting averages 7. You beat the nines and lose to the eights' three of a kind. Checking gives (20 + 0) / 2 = 10. Betting wins the existing 20 on a fold and loses the new 6 on the only call: (20 - 6) / 2 = 7. Your returned bet on a fold is not extra profit.

Betting is 3 chips worse than checking within this supplied two-hand model. Without arithmetic, the key observation is that the worse hand gives up and the better hand is the only paying caller. This is an invented comparison, not a measured opponent range or an optimal strategy for the full game.

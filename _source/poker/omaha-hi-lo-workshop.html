Making a qualifying low answers only the first question. Can it win the low half outright? Can another player tie it? Can the same holding win high? This workshop separates those questions before attaching a percentage to a decision.

Build two legal hands, independently.

In standard four-card Omaha hi-lo, use exactly two hole cards and three board cards for each five-card hand. The high and low selections may differ. A qualifying low has five distinct ranks of eight or lower, with the ace low; straights and flushes do not spoil the low. When nobody qualifies, the high winner takes the whole pot. The official rules describe these requirements.

Before comparing players, write their best high and best qualifying low separately. Three low cards on the board are not enough if their ranks repeat. A board containing 2-2-7-K-Q provides only two distinct low ranks. No choice of two hole cards can turn it into five different low ranks.

A quarter of the pot is not half a win.

Fictional allocation: a 240-chip pot is split equally into high and low. Player A wins high outright; A and B tie for the best low. A receives 120 + 60 = 180, while B receives 60. The 60 is one quarter of the whole pot, not half. First identify eligibility for each pot if someone is all-in for less.

Now suppose each player contributed 120 to this heads-up pot. B receives 60 but has a net result of -60. Being awarded some chips is not the same as profiting. Conversely, quartering does not automatically make every individual call wrong: a hypothetical guaranteed quarter of a 200-chip final pot is worth 50; paying an additional 40 for it has incremental chip EV of +10. That artificial calculation assumes no further action, no rake and certainty about the share.

Do not carry the word “guaranteed” into a real hand where another holding, a side pot or an unknown card can change the award. The example distinguishes an entire hand's result from a decision made after earlier contributions are already spent.

Counterfeiting can leave a legal low that no longer wins.

Fictional board change, checked from the legal card combinations

Board: 3 of clubs, 5 of diamonds, 7 of hearts, king of clubs, 2 of hearts. A holds ace of spades, 2 of spades, queen of diamonds, jack of diamonds. B holds ace of hearts, 4 of hearts, queen of hearts, jack of spades.

On the 3-5-7 flop, A can make 7-5-3-2-A, better than B's 7-5-4-3-A. After the river 2, A can still use the original three flop cards for the same seven-low. B can instead use A-4 with board 2-3-5 to make 5-4-3-2-A. A's low still qualifies, but B now beats it.

As a separate version of the example, replace A's queen with the 4 of clubs. That extra low card gives A an alternative pair of hole cards, A-4, allowing the same wheel as B on this river. This is counterfeit protection in this example, not a promise that an extra low card protects every board or wins high.

Count expected shares rather than only wins.

For a closed-action calculation, combine possible awards. A deliberately simplified model with a 20% chance of scooping, 40% chance of half, 20% chance of a quarter and 20% chance of nothing has expected pot share 0.20 + 0.40/2 + 0.20/4 = 45%. The probabilities sum to one. They are invented teaching inputs, not measured Omaha frequencies.

Do not calculate high and low as independent events by default. The same board and cards determine both. A hand can share low while making a strong high, or miss both simultaneously. Future betting also means showdown share alone may not price the entire decision. Start with the math reference, then inspect the actual award conditions.

A useful after-play routine.

Record the full board with suits, each known hole card, action amounts and pot eligibility. Enumerate the legal two-plus-three selections for high and low. Mark unknown opponents as ranges rather than inventing their holdings. Ask which runouts alter the best low, which improve high, and which merely make the hand look stronger without increasing its expected share.

For a new player, getting the award right is a better first task than buying a chart and memorizing colors. Use the mixed-game overview to choose the correct rules before moving to a different variant. This workshop is not an opening-range recommendation.

Check your understanding.

Can a board of 2-2-7-K-Q support a qualifying Omaha low?

No. It offers only two distinct low board ranks; the hand requires three distinct low ranks from the board.

If you tie only the low half heads-up, what fraction do you receive?

One quarter of the whole pot, assuming a qualifying low and no separate side-pot complication.

Does being counterfeited necessarily mean you have no qualifying low?

No. In the worked example A retains a seven-low, but B improves to a wheel and wins low.

Why has the dealer called the floor?

The dealer runs the hand; tournament staff resolve rulings that need a decision beyond ordinary dealing. A floor call is not proof that anyone cheated. An unclear declaration, exposed card or disputed amount can all require clarification. The relevant event rules and complete action matter more than a short video clip.

This is a viewer’s guide, not instructions for challenging a ruling. An edited account may omit the exact words, timing or previous action that staff considered. Describe what is visible and leave room for information outside the edit.

Words and chips both matter

Under TDA rules, in-turn declarations and chip actions can bind a player. A silent single oversized chip facing a bet normally means a call, not a raise. A player needs to make a raise intention clear before or as required by the applicable rule. Staff determine ambiguous situations.

A big chip does not automatically mean a big raise

In a fictional hand, a player faces 600 and silently places a single 1,000 chip forward. Under the stated TDA overchip rule, that is a call for 600; 400 is change. “Raises to 1,000” would be a different claim about what happened, and legality would depend on the preceding betting sequence.

Listen to the dealer’s announcement and any recorded declaration. A graphic assembled afterward should not overrule the actual account.

A live hand belongs to its player

TDA rules prohibit discussing live or mucked hand contents, advising play, or reading a hand that has not been tabled. Helping read properly tabled cards or pointing out a pot-award mistake at showdown is different. The timing and what is exposed matter.

As a spectator, save strategic comments for after the hand. Saying “I folded a king” can change another player’s information even though your own cards are no longer live. A friend does not become an allowed adviser just because they are standing behind the player.

Long decisions, clocks and chip counts

“In the tank” means taking time over a decision. A clock request asks staff to apply a decision deadline under the event’s rules; it is not evidence of a weak hand. Shot-clock events may instead have a regular time limit and time-extension procedures.

A neat, countable stack helps others understand the situation. Small stacks of high-denomination chips can be worth more than visually large piles of low-denomination chips. Do not infer an exact wager from chip color or pile height without knowing the denominations.

Vlog footage is not blanket permission to film

Device, filming and outside-assistance policies differ by event. TDA rules restrict devices during a live hand and disallow strategy tools at the table. PokerStars Live also publishes additional restrictions. Neither seeing a camera in one video nor knowing the player establishes permission at a different venue.

For this site, private or unapproved footage should stay private. A hand can be explained afterward with an explicitly reconstructed account and illustrations. A reconstruction is not proof that the original hand was recorded, and another player’s name is usually unnecessary for understanding the decision.

Watching the vlog does not require wagering. Enjoying the story, understanding the cards and discussing a completed hand are worthwhile on their own. These guides are for that viewing and study context, not for operating a tool while a real hand is underway.

Check your reading

Does a floor call prove misconduct?

No. It establishes that a ruling or clarification was needed. Judge neither a player nor staff from an incomplete edit.

A single 1,000 chip silently faces a 600 bet under TDA rules. What does it normally mean?

A call of 600 with 400 change, not automatically a raise to 1,000. Recorded verbal declarations or other details can matter to the actual ruling.

Can a spectator tell a friend what to do because the spectator is not playing?

No. One-player-to-a-hand restrictions apply to outside advice too. Save analysis for after play and observe the event’s spectator rules.

“I have a good hand” describes your cards. “I want a worse hand to call this amount” explains a reason to bet. Learning the difference makes both your own study notes and a commentator's explanation easier to question.

1. Name the response you want

A value bet aims to get called by a worse hand. A bluff aims to make a better hand fold. These describe an intention relative to the opponent's possible hands, not just the strength of your own two cards. The same pair can be a reasonable value candidate in one assumed situation and an unhelpful bet in another.

When no bet is facing you, checking keeps you in the hand without adding chips. Acting last on the river after the opponent checks, your check completes betting and takes the hand to showdown. Checking earlier in the order does not prevent another player from betting. All river comparisons below put you last to act, heads-up, after a check.

Exercise 1: “I want to see where I am.” What is missing from that reason to bet?

Which hands will respond, and why that response helps. A call does not reveal one exact hand; a raise does not prove that you are beaten. Paying for a reaction can grow the pot without answering your original question. Rewrite the reason as “I think these worse hands can call” or “I think these better hands can fold,” and state what supports that belief.

A bet may produce information, but information alone does not establish that betting is better than checking. If you cannot name a useful response, preserve the uncertainty and compare the check. This is a review prompt, not a rule that you must always check when unsure.

2. A worse hand must actually call

One board, four alternative opponents

You hold queen of hearts and jack of diamonds:

Q♥J♦

The complete board is queen of spades, eight of clubs, five of hearts, three of diamonds and two of clubs:

Q♠8♣5♥3♦2♣

Your best five are Q-Q-J-8-5. The pot is 40 before your decision. Both players have 100 behind, with no fees, side pots or tournament payouts. The opponent has checked. A check by you ends betting. For a bet, the teaching model allows only a fold or call, with no raises.

The four rows below are alternative exact holdings for one opponent, not eight cards held together. For this exercise only, give each alternative equal probability at this decision. That is a supplied assumption, not something established by listing four hands. In real play you do not see these hidden cards.

Exercise 2: Which two holdings are the targets of this value bet?

Q♣ T♦ and 9♥ 9♦. Your Q-Q-J-8-5 beats queen-ten's Q-Q-T-8-5 and the nines' 9-9-Q-8-5. The model says these worse hands call. The eights make 8-8-8-Q-5 and beat you. Ace-king makes A-K-Q-8-5 and loses, but its assumed fold adds no opposing chips.

You win a showdown against three of the four alternatives, yet only two supply a worse call. Counting every hand you beat as a paying customer would confuse hand strength with betting value. The stronger hand calling is a cost you must include, not evidence by itself that the value-bet intention was mistaken.

Exercise 3, optional arithmetic: Is betting 10 better than checking in this restricted model?

Yes, by 2.5 chips on average under the supplied responses. Measure chips received minus new chips paid from this decision onward. Earlier contributions are already in the 40-chip pot. Checking wins 40 against three alternatives and receives 0 against the eights: (40 + 40 + 0 + 40) / 4 = 30.

With a bet of 10, a winning call returns a 60-chip pot after you paid 10, giving 50. A losing call costs 10. A fold returns the 50-chip pot including your own 10, giving 40, not 50. The average is (50 + 50 - 10 + 40) / 4 = 32.5. The difference from checking is 32.5 - 30 = 2.5.

These are decision-point values, not full-hand profits. The comparison covers a check and this one bet in this intentionally restricted model. It does not prove 10 is an optimal size or that an actual opponent supplies these responses.

Now change only one assumption: both worse pairs fold to the same bet of 10. The eights still call and ace-king still folds. The cards, pot and equal starting weights stay the same.

Exercise 4: You still beat three hands. Does that preserve the benefit of betting?

No. Checking now has the higher value in this model. Three folds each return the existing 40 net of your own returned bet, while the only call loses 10: (40 + 40 - 10 + 40) / 4 = 27.5. Checking still averages 30. Betting is 2.5 chips worse than checking, even though it wins the pot in three of the four alternatives.

The useful lesson without arithmetic is that a bet which drives out all the worse hands and receives only a better call can damage your result. “I usually have the best hand” is not a complete value-betting argument. Which hands continue, and how often, can reverse the comparison.

3. A larger bet can lose its customers

Return to the first response model. Consider a bet of 20 into the same 40-chip pot. For this new size, suppose both worse pairs fold, the eights call and ace-king folds. We changed the opponent's responses with the price; we did not prove they must behave this way.

Exercise 5: Does twice the bet automatically mean twice the value?

No. In this supplied 20-chip model, three folds return 40 each and the losing call costs 20. The average is (40 + 40 - 20 + 40) / 4 = 25. That is less than the check's 30 and the first model's 10-chip bet value of 32.5.

This does not establish that small bets are always better. If worse hands continued to call the larger size, the comparison would change again. State the bet size and the assumed responses together. A useful study note asks which hands keep calling as the price increases, instead of scaling the expected reward while pretending every other input stays fixed.

Raising a pot after a fold can look successful on video. Success at making someone fold is different from getting the most value from a hand. Likewise, losing after a value bet does not automatically invalidate its earlier reasoning. Review the information and assumptions available before the cards were exposed.

4. A bluff needs better hands to fold

A different fictional river

You have J♥ T♥, jack-ten of hearts, on K♠ 9♣ 5♥ 3♦ 2♣. The opponent's alternative in this model is Q♥ Q♣. Your K-J-T-9-5 loses to their Q-Q-K-9-5. There are no ties or cards still to come.

The pot is 30, both players have 100 behind and the opponent checks to you last to act. The model excludes fees, side pots and raises. Checking loses at showdown. Suppose a bet of 10 makes this better hand fold one quarter of the time and call three quarters of the time. That frequency is supplied for teaching, not measured.

Exercise 6: What does the bluff gain on a fold, and what does it risk on a call?

A fold gains the existing 30; a losing call costs your new 10. Your own bet is returned inside the pot when the opponent folds, so the gain is not 40. At the assumed frequencies, the average is (1 / 4) × 30 - (3 / 4) × 10 = 0. Checking also gives 0 from this decision because your hand always loses this restricted showdown.

For this zero-showdown-value bluff, break-even folds are 10 / (30 + 10) = 25%. This is the required fold frequency, not your hand's equity, the opponent's call price or the frequency with which you should bluff. Less folding makes this bet worse than checking; more makes it better within the model.

Do not carry the 25% to a different size. A 30-chip bluff into 30 needs 30 / (30 + 30) = 50% folds under the same no-win-when-called conditions. Before recommending either size you would need a defensible response model, not just a correct denominator.

Betting a weak hand is not automatically a useful bluff. If you expect only worse hands to fold, those folds do not show that you persuaded a stronger hand to give up. On the river, explain which better hands are the target and what evidence supports their folding. A dramatic single fold cannot establish that frequency.

5. A draw offers a second route, not a guarantee

Move back to the turn

Your A♥ J♥ on K♥ 8♥ 3♣ 2♦ has ace high and a heart-flush draw. Against the illustrative K♣ Q♦ it is currently behind a pair of kings. A river 6♥ would make your ace-high flush; a river 7♣ would leave you behind. Neither river is known at the turn decision.

Exercise 7: If you bet this draw, must the opponent fold for you ever to win?

No. You might win immediately if a better hand folds, or improve and win after a call. A bet with these two possible routes is commonly called a semi-bluff. On the 6♥ river, your A-K-J-8-6 hearts form a flush and beat the king pair. On 7♣, your A-K-J-8-7 remains ace high and loses to K-K-Q-8-7.

This explains the term without establishing that betting is the best turn action. A full comparison needs the pot, size, responses, future cards and later betting. The river pure-bluff formula deliberately assumed no wins when called; do not reuse it unchanged for this draw. Having an extra route to win is not proof that the price is worthwhile.

Protection, also called denying equity, describes making another hand fold its chance to improve. For example, a pair can benefit when two higher unpaired cards give up before the river. That purpose can overlap with getting worse hands to call. It does not mean that a bet prevents an opponent from calling or guarantees that a made hand stays best. Return to made hands and draws if those two labels become mixed together.

Leave with a reason you can test

Close the answers and explain the first board without doing the arithmetic. Your pair beat three alternatives, but the bet's benefit depended on two worse hands calling. When those hands folded, checking became better in the restricted comparison. The useful skill is connecting your intended response to particular possible hands.

  1. Describe: my hand, the board, the pot, the size and who still has a turn.
  2. Target: which worse hands might call, or which better hands might fold?
  3. Compare: what happens if I check, and what can happen after a call or raise?
  4. Question: which response or frequency did I assume, and what would change it?

Keep one sentence in your own notes: “This bet aims to ___ against ___; it depends on ___; checking would ___.” An honest blank identifies the next study question. No answer form or progress record is collected here.

Facing a preflop raise: fold, call, three-bet or four-bet?

The same ace-jack suited can face very different decisions when the cutoff opens, the first player opens, or the big blind re-raises. Start with the action that actually happened. Then count the price, remaining players and stacks before comparing ranges or studying a chart.

For after-play study. This is a practical no-limit Texas hold'em decision guide. All hands, player actions and stack sizes are fictional teaching fixtures. No actual opponent tendencies, solver solution, tournament payout model or universally best preflop range is claimed. 1. Which decision is actually available?

Think of a preflop betting round as a sequence, not a menu of four interchangeable buttons. Posting the big blind is the initial live wager. An open-raise raises that wager; a three-bet re-raises the open; a four-bet re-raises the three-bet. These names count betting levels, not multiples of someone's chip amount. Review how the first raise changes the situation if this terminology is new.

A call matches the current total street wager, less any live chips you already posted or contributed. A raise to 9 BB means your total current-street contribution will be 9 BB. It does not mean adding 9 BB to your previous contribution. If the players behind you can raise, calling now does not guarantee that the pot stays at that price.

When you are in a blind, the chips already posted change the incremental price of that same raise. The blind-defense guide works through a button open from both seats, plus a squeeze over an open and a caller.

For tournament procedure, the official Poker TDA rules set out minimum full raises, short all-ins and when betting reopens. Cash rooms and online games have their own applicable rules. This guide uses ordinary full raises, not edge-case all-in rulings. If chips or verbal declarations are ambiguous at a table, ask the dealer or floor.

2. Facing an open: work the numbers before choosing

Fictional six-handed cash hand A

Blinds are 0.5/1 BB, no ante, and all players start with 100 BB immediately before posting. Ignore rake for these accounting illustrations. The first two players fold. The cutoff opens to 2.5 BB. You are on the button with A♥ J♥, having posted nothing. The small blind and big blind have yet to respond. No opponent range or read is supplied.

The pot facing you is 4 BB: the cutoff's 2.5, the small blind's 0.5 and the big blind's 1. Here are three possible actions. The 8 BB three-bet is an illustrative sizing, not a recommended strategy.

Pot accounting at the button decision in hand A

Fold0 BB4 BBThe hand continues without you

If you three-bet to 8 BB, both blinds fold, and the cutoff calls, the cutoff adds 5.5 BB more, making a heads-up pot of 17.5 BB. The two continuing players have each invested 8 BB and have 92 BB behind. The resulting stack-to-pot ratio (SPR) is 92 / 17.5 ≈ 5.26 at the flop. That is one conditional branch, not the inevitable outcome of three-betting.

If instead the big blind raises over your call or the cutoff four-bets over your three-bet, you face a new price and a new decision. Your first action did not purchase the whole hand. See the SPR reference for the formula and position and effective-stack definitions for multiway situations.

3. Range versus range is the real strategy question

Before studying whether the button should fold, call or three-bet, ask what the cutoff could plausibly open. A known opening range is not supplied in hand A. Even if you know your exact two cards, you cannot derive a reliable action frequency from the ranks alone. A real model needs the opener's position and opening range, your response options, the blinds' responses, stacks, rake and a model for future play.

Folding ends your exposure to this pot. Calling keeps more of your range in the pot but may leave you with several opponents or a further raise. Three-betting changes the price and asks other players to continue against a different action; its possible value depends on which hands continue, which fold and what happens later. None of these descriptions alone identifies the highest-EV choice.

A study tree may allow several re-raise sizes, or remove calling entirely as a simplification. Those trees can produce different outputs. 4. Facing a three-bet: the four-bet is a different decision

Fictional six-handed cash hand B

Again use 0.5/1 BB blinds, no ante, no rake for the example and 100 BB starting stacks. Earlier players fold. You open the button to 2.5 BB with A♣ Q♣. The small blind folds, leaving its 0.5 BB in the pot. The big blind, who posted 1 BB, three-bets to 9 BB total. Action returns to you. Both of you have enough chips for every size discussed below. The big blind's three-betting range is unknown.

Before you respond, the pot is 12 BB: your 2.5, the folded small blind's 0.5 and the big blind's 9. The cost to call is not 9 BB: you have already put in 2.5. It is 9 − 2.5 = 6.5 BB.

Three ways the button can respond in hand B
ResponseYou add nowPot after your actionIf the big blind calls
Fold0 BB12 BBNot applicable
Call to 9 BB6.5 BB18.5 BBPreflop action closes; both have 91 BB behind
Four-bet to 22 BB total19.5 BB31.5 BBBig blind adds 13 BB; pot becomes 44.5 BB

The minimum ordinary full four-bet here is to 15.5 BB total: the three-bet raised the wager from 2.5 to 9, a 6.5 BB increase, so the next full raise must increase 9 by at least 6.5. A four-bet to 22 BB is legal with the stated stacks. This does not make 22 BB strategically optimal. Special short all-ins, live-chip declarations and betting re-opening follow the venue's rules, including TDA Rules 45 and 49 in applicable tournaments.

If the big blind calls that 22 BB four-bet, each player has 78 BB behind and the flop pot is 44.5 BB, giving SPR = 78 / 44.5 ≈ 1.75. If you call the 9 BB three-bet instead, each has 91 BB behind against an 18.5 BB pot, or SPR ≈ 4.92. These are different future betting environments. A smaller SPR is not proof that a four-bet is right.

There is a tempting but incomplete calculation: 6.5 / 18.5 ≈ 35.1%. That is the showdown-equity threshold for a hypothetical heads-up model where you call, there is no rake or further betting, and showdown follows. In real preflop play, players make postflop decisions and do not realize all raw showdown equity equally. Do not treat 35.1% as a standalone preflop calling rule. The pot-odds workshop teaches the clean model before adding later streets.

5. Change the depth and keep the same arithmetic honest

Return to fictional hand A: cutoff opens to 2.5 BB, you three-bet the button to 8 BB, the blinds fold and the cutoff calls. At 100 BB effective the flop pot is 17.5 BB and each of you has 92 BB behind. Now run the same opening and three-bet sizes with both of you starting at 25 BB, with the blinds again folding and all players able to post fully.

The flop pot is still 17.5 BB, but each continuing player has only 17 BB behind, so SPR = 17 / 17.5 ≈ 0.97. The same label, “button facing a cutoff open,” conceals a major difference in what further bets can cost. A chart built for 100 BB does not settle the 25 BB decision.

Tournaments add more changes: antes, uneven stacks, potential short all-ins, bounties, escalating blinds and sometimes payout-sensitive objectives. A cash-game example cannot establish a profitable tournament shove, call or four-bet. Use the short-stack decision guide and the tournament-equity guide when studying those conditions.

6. Pause before revealing the arithmetic

In hand A, what is the pot after your button call, before either blind acts?

6.5 BB. Add 2.5 BB from the cutoff, 2.5 BB from you, 0.5 BB from the small blind and 1 BB from the big blind. It is not yet a guaranteed heads-up pot, because both blinds remain in the hand until they act.

In hand B, how much more do you put in to call a three-bet to 9 BB?

6.5 BB, not 9. Your earlier 2.5 BB raise is already part of your current-street contribution. The pot becomes 18.5 BB if you call, and the preflop round then ends because the other remaining player is the big blind who made the last raise.

In hand B, is “four-bet to 14 BB” a normal full raise?

No. The last full raise increased the total from 2.5 to 9, an increment of 6.5 BB. The next full raise must go to at least 15.5 BB with these stacks. An unusual short all-in or a disputed chip declaration can have different procedural consequences; ask the room rather than applying this example to a floor ruling.

Does the 35.1% figure prove A♣ Q♣ should call the big blind's three-bet?

No. It assumes an immediate showdown after your call and omits future betting and rake. You also have no verified big-blind three-betting range. It identifies a mathematical reference point, not a solved preflop decision or a read on the opponent.

7. Keep a reusable after-play decision record

Write the following before consulting a solver or chart. You can copy it into private notes; this page has no form, tracking or paid tool requirement.

  1. Game and objective: cash or tournament, seats dealt in, blind and ante structure, known rake or fees, and whether the question is chip EV or modeled payouts.

  2. Seats and stacks: your position, the opener and re-raiser positions, exact starting stacks and contributions already made. List other players who can still act.

  3. Action history: every open, call and re-raise to a total, in order. Note whether the last raise was a full raise.

  4. Immediate prices: pot now, extra chips to call, proposed raise total, and pot if each relevant opponent calls or folds.

  5. Ranges and uncertainty: which observed facts support each range, what is merely an assumption, and which cards or earlier actions rule out combinations.

  6. Compare like with like: seek a study reference with matching positions, stacks, rake, action tree and objective. Record a mismatch instead of presenting its result as an exact answer for your hand.

For a longer route, continue with reading preflop charts, counting ranges and a decision lab that separates facts from assumptions. You can learn these distinctions without wagering. Safer-play and support resources are available if playing stops being recreational.

Four-card Pot-Limit Omaha high only. 1. A lone ace of spades is a blocker, not a flush

Standard Omaha high requires exactly two of your four hole cards plus exactly three community cards for the best five-card high hand. For a flush, this means the chosen two hole cards and three chosen board cards must share the suit. Four or five board spades cannot substitute for a second spade in your hand. This rule is stated in the official PokerStars Omaha rules and the WSOP Online Omaha rules.

Fictional monotone flop, but no hero flush

Hero: A♠ K♥ Q♦ J♣. Flop: T♠ 8♠ 3♠. Hero has only one private spade, A♠, so no legal spade flush. However, no rival can hold A♠, because hero physically holds it. The ace blocks the rival's ace-high spade flush on this board; it does not block every lower spade flush, make the hero's current hand strong or establish a profitable bluff.

A rival holding K♠ 7♠ among their four private cards, for example, could make a legal king-high spade flush using those two spades and T♠ 8♠ 3♠ from the flop, assuming those physical cards are available. That flush beats the hero's non-flush hand in the example. A blocker is card removal, not immunity. The preceding PLO two-plus-three workshop demonstrates the same hand-selection rule with straights.

3. Four-card opponents need a four-card denominator

Suppose the opponent's four private cards are dealt uniformly at random from those 45 unknown physical cards, before we use their actions to weight any holdings. There are 45 choose 4 = 148,995 possible unordered four-card holdings in each information set. On a three-spade flop, a rival can already make a spade flush if their four-card holding contains at least two spades, not necessarily exactly two.

With A♠ in the hero's hand, the rival can have two, three or four spades drawn from nine available. The exact count is C(9,2) × C(36,2) + C(9,3) × C(36,1) + C(9,4) = 25,830 four-card holdings. With no hero spade, it is C(10,2) × C(35,2) + C(10,3) × C(35,1) + C(10,4) = 31,185. Every four-card holding appears once, even if it contains three different qualifying pairs of spades.

These are possible deals, not measured fold frequencies, not a player's probability of continuing with a flush and not proof a blocker bluff works. 4. The nut flush is made. A set can still redraw to a full house

Now begin a different fictional hand. The turn board is Q♠ 8♠ 3♠ 7♦. Hero holds A♠ K♠ J♥ 7♥ and has the nut spade flush, made legally from A♠ K♠ and Q♠ 8♠ 3♠. The rival's four exposed cards are Q♥ Q♦ 2♣ 4♣, giving a set of queens through Q♥ Q♦ with board Q♠. The opponent does not yet have a full house, and their clubs do not create a flush on this board.

All eight private cards and four turn cards are known. The unknown river count is 52 − 12 = 40. A river that pairs one of the board ranks makes the rival a full house, except a remaining queen makes four of a kind. Count the exact available physical cards, not merely the four ranks.

Why only two sevens? The board shows 7♦, and hero has 7♥. Neither physical card can reappear. The remaining sevens are 7♣ and 7♠. A second version changes hero's final hole card from 7♥ to T♦, while keeping A♠ K♠ J♥, the entire board and the rival's Q♥ Q♦ 2♣ 4♣ unchanged. That changes one identified blocker, not the 40-card denominator. With 7♥ now available, the rival wins on 10 / 40 = 25% of rivers, and hero wins on 30/40 with no ties. The difference is exactly the additional 7♥ river.

The nut flush on the turn was a true current best hand in the stated board texture. It was never an unbeatable hand on the river. The two-plus-three requirement continues to apply to full houses and quads. The hand-ranking guide explains why a full house beats a flush; the earlier PLO redraw workshop counts overlapping straight and flush threats using different cards.

5. A paired river does not automatically beat a flush

Return to the same turn board and hero as in the 9-of-40 case, but replace the opposing hand with Q♥ J♦ T♣ 9♣. This opponent has one queen in the hole and a queen on the board, a pair, not a set. On an 8♥ river the board contains two eights, and the opponent can make two pair from the queen and the board eights. They cannot turn one hole queen plus one board queen and two board eights into a full house using exactly two hole cards.

Same paired river, different legal result

Board: Q♠ 8♠ 3♠ 7♦ 8♥. Hero A♠ K♠ J♥ 7♥ still has the ace-high spade flush. The new opponent Q♥ J♦ T♣ 9♣ has at most two pair on that river and loses. Exhaustively checking all 40 rivers for this different fully exposed rival produces 40 hero wins, zero rival wins, zero ties. This is possible because the rival has no two-spade private combination and no legal full-house construction in this fixture.

The set scenario's 9 winning rivers do not transfer to this pair scenario. Board texture is only one input; the opponent's exact hole cards or justified range determine whether a paired river actually makes a higher-ranked hand. A one-card change to the board can be important without being a guaranteed reversal. The hold'em flush-and-redraw lab studies the analogous reasoning with a different legal-card rule.

6. Bring the exact count back to an actual decision carefully

  1. Name the game. Verify standard four-card Omaha high, exactly two hole cards and three board cards, and the actual betting structure. Do not import Omaha hi-lo split-pot equity. The Omaha hi-lo workshop has a different winning-share question.

  2. Name the information set. A physical A♠ blocker is known when it is in your hand. A face-up rival hand belongs to a showdown replay, not an ordinary earlier decision. If rival cards are unknown, do not subtract them as known dead cards.

  3. Count complete opponent holdings where that is the question. Four-card PLO permits an opponent to hold two, three or four cards of the target suit. A count of possible two-spade pairs is not automatically a count of distinct four-card hands.

  4. Evaluate both legal best fives on each remaining river. A pair on the board can supply the second rank in a full house against a set. Against a rival with only one matching private rank, the same board pair may still lose to the made flush.

  5. Do not confuse a showdown probability with the price of a call. Future betting, which opponents can raise, your actual pot eligibility and a justified opponent range determine whether any payment makes sense. Use the pot-odds workshop, multiway pot guidance and uncertainty framework before making a strategic claim. For tournament prize implications, consult tournament equity.

7. Eight revealable checks

You hold only A♠ among four private cards on a three-spade flop. Do you have a flush?

No. Standard Omaha high requires two private spades and three spades from the board. A♠ alone blocks a rival's ace-high spade flush but does not make your own.

Does holding A♠ rule out every opposing spade flush?

No. An opponent may still hold two other spades, such as K♠ 7♠, and make a legal lower spade flush. The ace prevents a rival from having that specific ace, not from holding any suited cards.

How many physical spades are unseen with the A♠ blocker and T♠ 8♠ 3♠ flop?

Nine. Thirteen total spades minus the three board spades minus the hero's A♠. Forty-five total cards are still unknown.

Why is C(9,2) = 36 not the number of four-card opponent holdings capable of a flush?

An opponent receives four hole cards and can hold two, three or four spades. You must choose the remaining hole cards too, and not count the same four-card holding multiple times. The exact eligible total is 25,830 of 148,995 possible four-card holdings under that uniform deal model.

If the hero instead holds A♥ K♥ Q♦ J♣, how many opponent holdings contain A♠ and another spade?

6,699 of 148,995, about 4.50%. Require A♠, then choose three of the other 44 cards with at least one spade: C(44,3) − C(35,3).

Why can Q♣ beat a nut flush when the rival holds Q♥ Q♦ on the Q♠ board?

The rival makes four queens with Q♥ Q♦ and board Q♠ Q♣, then a fifth board card. The high-hand ranking of quads is above a flush. It is one physical river, not four separate ranks.

With hero A♠ K♠ J♥ 7♥ against Q♥ Q♦ 2♣ 4♣, why are only two sevens live?

The board already has 7♦ and hero holds 7♥. Only 7♣ and 7♠ can fall. The total rival-winning rivers are 1 queen + 3 eights + 3 threes + 2 sevens = 9 of 40.

Does 8♥ automatically beat the hero's flush against the alternative opponent Q♥ J♦ T♣ 9♣?

No. That rival has only a pair of queens before the river; on 8♥ they can make two pair, not a full house, and the hero's flush still wins. All 40 face-up river comparisons leave the hero ahead in this separate fixture.

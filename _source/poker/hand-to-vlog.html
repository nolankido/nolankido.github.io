The creator workbench

After-play creator work. A proposed editing workflow with one actual public-page editing case. Hand examples and suggested narration are fictional, not accounts of a released episode. Recording permission and tournament rules must be checked separately.

Give the viewer a question worth staying for.

“I had ace-queen” supplies cards. “Which worse hands would bet?” supplies a question. Establish what the player is deciding before explaining the answer. The cards then have a purpose in the story.

Separate footage, notes, recollection, and later study. A useful sequence does not require a dramatic result. The actual editing case below shows a small presentation change without claiming a new poker insight.

Select a decision, not just the largest pot.

For each candidate hand, ask whether it has a clear situation, a decision that can be explained, enough reliable detail to reconstruct and a reason for a viewer to care. Mark each as ready, needs verification or leave out. This is an editorial screen, not a numerical quality score.

A smaller, well-recorded hand may be more useful than a dramatic incomplete one. A recollection should not acquire exact graphics simply because the edit needs them.

Keep one primary question. Supporting hands should develop it rather than become a collection of unrelated all-ins.

Build the fact sheet before the voiceover.

Write positions, blinds and ante, relevant starting stacks, known cards and street-by-street actions. Record whether each amount is a bet, a raise to a total or an additional call. Reconcile the pot after each street and account for uncalled refunds and side pots.

Privately label each detail: visible in footage, noted at the time, remembered later, or unknown. Surface uncertainty that affects the explanation. A precise graphic does not make a remembered amount exact.

A useful uncertainty label

A fictional note says “about one-third pot.” Keep that approximation if trustworthy; do not invent “2,400.” If the missing amount could change the conclusion, keep that limitation beside the analysis.

Keep filenames and timecodes private. Use neutral participant labels such as button or big blind. Remove identifying documents, messages, and unrelated personal details.

Use three clearly different voices.

What happenedRecorded action and clearly marked recollection. “The button raised to 2.5 BB.”
What I thought thenYour actual remembered reasoning, without importing cards or analysis learned afterward.
What I think nowLater calculations, alternative ranges and corrections, with their assumptions visible.

Suggested fictional narration: “At the table I thought a missed draw was possible. During review I realized I had not estimated how often it would actually bet.” That distinguishes the original assumption from its later review.

An edit can compress time without inventing causation. Label a reaction recorded later when it would otherwise appear to be the immediate reaction to a hand. Do not splice an unrelated player's expression into a decision sequence as evidence of a tell or motive.

Show only the information available at that moment.

Reveal the board one street at a time. Keep the focal player's cards, positions, pot and current bet legible before adding extra statistics. Avoid showing an opponent's eventual cards or a known-card equity graphic before the story reaches the point when they became known, unless the sequence is explicitly framed as a later reconstruction.

For “30% equity,” identify the information set: a revealed hand, assumed range, or particular model. Without a defensible calculation, omit the number.

For a hand with an uncertain pot, use a visibly approximate label. For a side pot, explain which pot the focal player can win. Check the incremental call before animating a raise-to amount as fresh money.

Review the sequence on a phone-sized screen with sound off. Can the reader identify the decision? Then listen without looking. Does the narration explain the stakes without requiring constant visual decoding? These are practical editorial checks, not formal accessibility certification. Captions should be reviewed for card ranks, suit names and poker terms rather than trusted blindly.

Behind the edit / Actual website example

The same hand. A clearer starting point.

This case documents an edit to this site's published hand-review guide. The page edit is real; the hand is fictional. It is not a recovered tournament note, a vlog screenshot, or evidence of a player's result.

The guide already recorded the button holding king-queen of spades on a queen-eight-three flop, 30 big blinds effective at the start, and the action in order. Its arithmetic was already consistent. The editorial question was narrower: could someone arriving at the decision find the acting player, current pot, and extra call without first reading the whole explanation?

Before: inside the action ledger

The earlier published paragraph read:

The pot is now 14.5 BB: 5.5 + 2 + 7. The button faces 5 BB more to call, not 7 BB.

The information was present. Finding the decision required reading it within the chronological account.

The snapshot now sits before the cards and full ledger. It gives the reader an entry point, not a replacement for the recorded action.

What changed, and what stayed put

The edit promotes three existing facts into labeled fields and adds a direct link to them. The full pot ledger stays below: 5.5 BB before the flop bet, 2 BB from the button, and a raise to 7 BB from the big blind. The extra call remains 7 minus the button's 2, or 5 BB. No cards, bets, opponent range, or result were added to make the presentation feel complete.

“Pot before the response” identifies the moment the number describes. “Additional amount to call” distinguishes the missing chips from the opponent's total wager. The 30 BB effective stack remains labeled as a starting stack, rather than being relabeled as chips remaining. Those labels explain what a number means, not just how large to draw it.

What this edit does not establish

This was a presentation change, not the discovery of a miscounted pot or a strategic mistake. The example still supplies no opponent hand or eventual result. A clearer price does not settle whether calling, folding, or reraising is best. The earlier and revised explanations stop at the same unanswered decision.

The fields are ordinary page text, so readers can select, enlarge, and print them rather than relying on text inside a picture. Whether the change actually helps new viewers remains a question for willing readers; no audience test or improvement in comprehension is claimed here.

A proposed episode structure.

Opening: establish the question and enough context to care, without requiring a spoiler. Orientation: explain the tournament format and relevant stakes. Development: use a small number of hands or moments that change the situation. Review: revisit one assumption. Close: state the result honestly and name what remains unresolved.

This is a starting structure, not a mandatory duration or platform-performance claim. Use travel footage for atmosphere or progression, not padding.

Start with one coherent question, one documented hand, accurate graphics, reviewed captions, and a truthful ending. Add complexity only when it helps.

The companion page should add something.

Choose one addition the video does not already provide: a checked pot ledger, a precise guide reference, a useful chapter index, or a later correction. Publish one companion for the episode and a separate hand review only when it adds distinct analysis.

One package, one source record

Keep the verified facts, unknowns, actual remembered thinking, approved public image, and final video details together in a private packet. Give the owner one consolidated approval pass. Raw footage, draft narration, and completed worksheets stay outside the public repository.

No finished video? An approved written story can stand alone. Do not label it an episode, invent a runtime, or use planned timestamps. No complete hand? Narrow the observation or choose a better-documented example.

Keep outcomes in the existing spoiler disclosures when appropriate. A closed disclosure is a reading choice, not privacy. Do not duplicate the whole voiceover.

Before publication, verify that any linked video is public and approved; chapter times match the released edit; graphics agree with the hand record; fictional examples are labeled; and any venue, music, footage and participant permissions are resolved. Remove license plates, private correspondence, account details and precise overnight locations from public material. The site's contact form is not a place to collect raw private footage.

Use Behind the edit for a process case and After the session for later review. Link to the review method or graphic key only where useful.

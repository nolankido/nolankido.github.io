The creator workbench

A proposed editing workflow, not an account of a published episode. Example narration and scenarios are fictional. Recording permission and tournament rules must be checked separately.

Give the viewer a question worth staying for.

A poker hand becomes easier to follow when the audience knows what the player is trying to decide. “I had ace-queen” supplies cards. “I could name the hands that beat me, but I could not tell which worse hands would bet” supplies a question. The second gives the cards a purpose.

This workbench is for turning footage, notes and later study into a story without pretending they are the same kind of evidence. It does not require a dramatic result. A modest pot, a correctly abandoned idea or an honest uncertainty can carry a useful sequence.

Select a decision, not just the largest pot.

For each candidate hand, ask whether it has a clear situation, a decision that can be explained, enough reliable detail to reconstruct and a reason for a viewer to care. Mark each as ready, needs verification or leave out. This is an editorial screen, not a numerical quality score.

A large pot with unknown positions and missing action may be a poor instructional centerpiece. A smaller hand with exact sizes can be more useful. Sometimes an incomplete hand still belongs as a brief recollection, but it should not acquire exact graphics simply because the edit needs them.

Keep one primary question for the episode: learning to read a table, handling changing stack depth or checking an assumption after play. Supporting hands should develop that question. A collection of unrelated all-ins can be exciting while still leaving the audience unsure what the episode was about.

Build the fact sheet before the voiceover.

Write positions, blinds and ante, relevant starting stacks, known cards and street-by-street actions. Record whether each amount is a bet, a raise to a total or an additional call. Reconcile the pot after each street and account for uncalled refunds and side pots.

For each detail, retain a private evidence label: visible in footage, written in a contemporaneous note, remembered later or unknown. These labels need not all appear on screen, but uncertainty that affects the explanation should. An exact-looking graphic does not make a remembered amount exact.

A useful uncertainty label

Suppose a fictional note records a flop bet as “about one-third pot” but not the chip amount. Do not invent “2,400” to fill the overlay. Use “approximately one-third pot” if the approximation is trustworthy, or omit the size and explain what is missing.

If changing that amount could reverse the strategic analysis, do not deliver a precise verdict. The missing detail is part of the conclusion.

Keep file names and timecodes in your private working record. Use neutral participant labels such as button or big blind. Do not include another person's full name, messages, identifying documents or unrelated personal information merely to make the account feel specific.

Use three clearly different voices.

Suggested narration labels for a reconstructed hand
VoiceWhat belongs there
What happenedRecorded action and clearly marked recollection. “The button raised to 2.5 BB.”
What I thought thenYour actual remembered reasoning, without importing cards or analysis learned afterward.
What I think nowLater calculations, alternative ranges and corrections, with their assumptions visible.

For a fictional example, compare “I knew the missed draw would bluff” with “At the table I thought a missed draw was possible. During review I realized I had not estimated how often it would actually bet.” The second sentence contains a learning point and does not turn hindsight into foresight.

An edit can compress time without inventing causation. Label a reaction recorded later when it would otherwise appear to be the immediate reaction to a hand. Do not splice an unrelated player's expression into a decision sequence as evidence of a tell or motive.

Show only the information available at that moment.

Reveal the board one street at a time. Keep the focal player's cards, positions, pot and current bet legible before adding extra statistics. Avoid showing an opponent's eventual cards or a known-card equity graphic before the story reaches the point when they became known, unless the sequence is explicitly framed as a later reconstruction.

A statement such as “30% equity” needs a denominator and an information set. Is it versus the opponent's revealed hand, an assumed range or a particular model? If there is no defensible calculation, omit the number. A clean graphic without a percentage is preferable to a fabricated one.

For a hand with an uncertain pot, use a visibly approximate label. For a side pot, explain which pot the focal player can win. Check the incremental call before animating a raise-to amount as fresh money.

Review the sequence on a phone-sized screen with sound off. Can the reader identify the decision? Then listen without looking. Does the narration explain the stakes without requiring constant visual decoding? These are practical editorial checks, not formal accessibility certification. Captions should be reviewed for card ranks, suit names and poker terms rather than trusted blindly.

A proposed episode structure.

Opening: establish the question and enough context to care, without requiring a spoiler. Orientation: explain the tournament format and relevant stakes. Development: use a small number of hands or moments that change the situation. Review: revisit one assumption. Close: state the result honestly and name what remains unresolved.

This is a starting structure, not a mandatory duration or a claim about platform performance. A strong sequence may need more breathing room; a weak sequence does not improve merely because it fills a target runtime. Travel footage should establish atmosphere or progression rather than become padding between unrelated hands.

Keep a minimum viable edit: one coherent question, one well-documented hand, accurate graphics, reviewed captions and a truthful ending. Add complexity only when it improves comprehension or the story. Finishing an honest episode is more useful than indefinitely collecting features for an ideal one.

The companion page should add something.

Use the website for a short premise, chapter links, a hand's assumptions, later corrections and links to a relevant guide. Keep outcomes inside the existing spoiler disclosures when appropriate. Do not merely duplicate the entire voiceover in another format.

Before publication, verify that any linked video is public and approved; chapter times match the released edit; graphics agree with the hand record; fictional examples are labeled; and any venue, music, footage and participant permissions are resolved. Remove license plates, private correspondence, account details and precise overnight locations from public material. The site's contact form is not a place to collect raw private footage.

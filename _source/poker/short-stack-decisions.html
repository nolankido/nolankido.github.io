Playing 10–20 big blinds in live tournaments

At 15 big blinds, “What can I shove?” is only one question. You may be first in, facing a raise, completing the small blind or considering a call for your tournament life. Start by defining the available actions and what you are trying to maximize: chips or modeled prize equity.

After-play study, not an at-table aid. This expanded guide focuses on 10–20 BB live tournament decisions. Calculated examples are fictional and show their assumptions. No personal hand, new solver run or universally correct range is claimed. Sources checked October 15, 2026.

If you want the shortest route, record the situation, compare what changes at 20, 15 and 10 BB, then work through the chip-value versus ICM call. Use position and stack terminology if effective stacks are unfamiliar. The short-stack input record is for a private review after play.

Two players can hold the same cards with the same nominal stack and face different decisions. A button entering an unopened pot is not in the same position as a big blind responding to a raise and a call. A useful chart matches the game being studied, including the actions that were allowed to happen.

Separate the action tree from the objective

A decision tree describes what players can do and what can happen next. A push/fold model deliberately removes some branches. In HoldemResources’ heads-up example, the small blind can only shove or fold; the big blind facing a shove can only call or fold. That chart answers the restricted two-player game. It does not establish that a smaller raise or a limp would be worse if those actions were available. 1

Chip expected value, or chip EV, values outcomes in chips. ICM, the Independent Chip Model, estimates tournament prize equity from stacks and payouts. Changing the objective is separate from expanding the tree. HRC’s free Nash ICM calculator explicitly remains a push-or-fold calculation; its configurable tree documentation also describes broader preflop and postflop analysis. 2 3

“The chart shoves” and “this other model raises small” need not be contradictory. First check whether the models offered the same choices. Then check whether they valued the results in the same units. Neither a mixed frequency nor a tool’s recommendation is a confidence score about an unknown live opponent.

Record a state, not a stack label.

Write the blind level, ante type, seats dealt in, button location, action so far and stack convention. “15 BB before posting” differs from “15 BB behind after posting.” Keep the original chip counts so a conversion can be checked. Identify every opponent who can still enter the pot, not only the person whose action caught your attention.

A smaller-stack contrast, retained from the original workshop: a player begins with 8,000 chips at blinds of 500/1,000 with a 1,000 big-blind ante. They are the big blind and can post both in full. The starting stack is 8 BB; after posting the live blind and ante, 6,000 chips remain behind. The live blind counts toward a call. The ante does not. Short-posting and ante-priority procedures require the event’s rules.

Effective stacks are opponent-specific. With 17 BB against players starting with 8 BB, 16 BB and 55 BB, the amounts initially contestable between you and each are 8, 16 and 17 BB, before accounting for forced contributions. Then account for dead antes and live posts. An opponent who started with fewer chips can cover your remaining live stake after you pay a big-blind ante; assess elimination risk from the actual post-ante state. In a multiway all-in, different contribution levels can create side pots; use the side-pot lab for refunds and eligibility.

Separate three meanings of “players remaining”: players still to act in this hand, players seated at this table, and players left in the tournament. They affect different parts of the decision. Nine-handed under the gun has eight players behind; a six-handed first-to-act seat has five. The same position label does not make those situations identical. Tournament field size and payouts answer another question again.

A big-blind ante is posted once per hand.

At a six-handed table with a 0.5 BB small blind, a 1 BB big blind and a 1 BB big-blind ante, the initial pot is 2.5 BB. Do not multiply that big-blind ante by six. With a 0.1 BB ante from each of six players instead, the initial pot is 2.1 BB. A nine-handed table using that individual ante would begin with 2.4 BB.

At unchanged blinds, a player completing a full six-hand orbit pays 2.5 BB in the first structure or 2.1 BB in the second, assuming every obligation is posted in full. This is bookkeeping, not a survival clock: blinds rise, tables change and hands can win or lose chips before the orbit ends.

Keep forced contributions in the ledger. An ante is money in the pot, not a live bet toward the amount to call. That distinction can alter an all-in price. The current Poker TDA rules also address big-blind-ante situations and restrict strategy tools at the table. Confirm the rules of your event and use these materials away from play. 4

Compare risk and reward without mistaking it for strategy

Fictional boundary model: nine-handed no-limit Hold’em; action folds to you in the cutoff; you start with 20 BB before forced bets and have posted none; all opponents start with at least 21 BB; blinds 0.5/1 BB; a 1 BB big-blind ante is fully posted by the big blind. No other contributions, bounty or deductions. The objective is chip EV. Compare a raise to 2 BB with a shove of 20 BB.

For this calculation only, assume each action wins the initial pot when everyone folds and loses its entire additional investment whenever anyone continues. There is no showdown equity, later betting or refund in that losing branch. These artificial assumptions isolate risk and reward; they are not a model of how either action actually plays.

The pot starts at 0.5 + 1 + 1 = 2.5 BB. Under this zero-equity model, the break-even joint fold probability is risk / (risk + reward):

Zero-equity fold thresholds in the fictional 20 BB setup
Additional investment Joint folds needed to break even
Raise to 2 BB 2 / 4.5 = 44.44%
Shove 20 BB 20 / 22.5 = 88.89%

The two actions will not necessarily receive the same responses. A small raise can face a call or reraise and a real shoving hand may retain equity when called. These numbers therefore do not select a preferred action. They show why removing the small-raise branch changes the question.

Ante sensitivity, with a separate 15 BB starting stack: keep the same cutoff, opponents covering, 0.5/1 blinds and artificial losing-when-called model. A 15 BB shove needs 15 / 16.5 = 90.91% joint folds with no ante, versus 15 / 17.5 = 85.71% with a 1 BB big-blind ante. The nominal stack is unchanged while the immediate reward differs.

If several opponents remain, the relevant event is everyone required to fold, not any one player’s fold rate. Responses are conditional on earlier actions and card removal. The multiway guide explains that calculation. Do not interpret these toy thresholds as measured live-player frequencies.

Do not mix these four families.

Choose the decision family before consulting a reference
Family What must match
First-in decision All earlier players folded. Count everyone still to act and retain their stacks.
Facing an open Record the opener’s total size, assumed range and the players behind. A rejam is a shove after someone has raised.
Calling an all-in Count what is still owed and the relevant range. Calling cannot make the existing shover fold.
Limped or multiway pot Prior contributions, more ranges and possible side pots create a different game.

At 15 BB, “shove first in” and “call a shove” are different decisions even with the same cards. The opener can win immediately through folds. A caller needs an adequate expected share of the contested pot under the relevant objective. Neither a familiar hand name nor the fear of becoming shorter supplies the missing range.

What changes at 20, 15 and 10 BB?

Treat the depths as study checkpoints, not switches that activate universal rules. Position, antes, opposing stacks and the prior action remain part of every checkpoint.

Poker Academy’s September 17, 2026 article reports nine-handed, chip-EV, 1 BB big-blind-ante solutions with 2 BB opens, and a 2.5 BB small-blind raise option. Its examples show small early-position opens at 20 and 15 BB, both raises and shoves on the 15 BB button, and some small button raises even at 10 BB. It also includes small-blind limps. These are provider-reported examples, not new solves or recommended live-field frequencies. The public summary is not a complete export of every opponent stack and downstream setting. 5

At 20 BB, compare the branches you would lose by using push/fold. Can a small raise win the pot, get called by worse or induce a response you can profitably continue against? If you intend to fold to a shove, which ranges make that plan reasonable? A non-all-in raise is not automatically a mistake because you may later fold. Nor is raise/fold automatically appropriate with every playable hand.

At 15 BB, start with the decision family and the players behind. An early-position first-in decision, a button open, a small-blind completion and a rejam over a raise are separate studies. A short blind can shove over your raise; a covering player can threaten your tournament life. Write the response plan before describing a smaller raise as “keeping options open.” It also creates new decisions.

At 10 BB, shoving becomes a more prominent study option without becoming the only legal action. A 2 BB open uses one fifth of a 10 BB starting stack before forced bets are considered. That makes the remaining call price important, but it does not mean every hand is committed. Count the additional call and compare against an assigned range. Avoid turning a low threshold into proof that your hand reaches it.

In the small blind, distinguish completing from opening elsewhere. If action folds to you, you already have a live half-blind invested in a standard 0.5/1 structure. Completing costs another half-blind and leaves the big blind an action. A cheap completion is not a free showdown. The range that reaches that decision and the big blind’s options matter.

For a useful comparison, keep everything except the selected variable fixed. If the table, ante, stack convention and permitted sizes all change between two screenshots, the difference cannot be credited solely to moving from 20 to 15 BB.

One call, two objectives: chips and classic ICM

Complete fictional setup: four players remain in a standard no-limit Hold’em tournament. Finishing prizes are $50, $30, $20 and $0. Starting stacks before posting, in the order small blind, C, you in the big blind, D, are 40, 30, 20 and 10 BB. Blinds are 0.5/1 BB with no ante. C and D fold; the small blind shoves. No bounty, deal, re-entry, satellite prize, deductions or other contributions apply.

You posted 1 BB and have 19 BB left to call. The small blind covers your 20 BB contribution. Its excess 20 BB is uncalled and returned if you call; it is not part of the contested award. There are no further betting decisions after a call. The two-outcome calculation below explicitly assumes no ties. Your cards and the shover’s range are unspecified because we are calculating a required win probability, not assigning one to a hand.

Chip EV: after calling, each player has contributed an effective 20 BB, making a 40 BB contested pot. Relative to folding now, the extra call costs 19 BB. The break-even win probability is 19 / 40 = 47.5%. Chips already posted are not charged again.

Classic ICM: the Malmuth–Harville model assigns finish probabilities from chip shares, successively removing each possible higher finisher. It then weights those probabilities by the prizes. HRC identifies this as the algorithm used by its separate ICM equity calculator. This prize model does not forecast individual skill, future position or blind changes. 6

Four-player ICM branches with 100 BB conserved
Choice or outcome Stacks in the stated order Your modeled prize equity
Fold 41, 30, 19, 10 BB $22.946148
Call and win 20, 30, 40, 10 BB $33.603175
Call and lose 60, 30, 0, 10 BB $0, fourth place

On a narrow screen, scroll this comparison sideways. The amounts are calculated for this fictional payout structure.

The fold branch preserves the small blind’s 40 BB starting stack plus your surrendered 1 BB. Winning returns its uncalled excess and leaves you with the 40 BB contested pot. Losing transfers your full 20 BB to the covering player. All branches conserve 100 BB.

With loss value zero and no ties, calling has modeled prize equity q × $33.603175. It must match folding’s $22.946148. Using unrounded values gives q = 68.2857%, approximately 68.3%. A hypothetical 60% win probability would exceed the 47.5% chip threshold but fall below this particular ICM threshold. That is not a recommendation for any named hand.

The exact hero finish probabilities are available in the reproduction script. It uses only Python’s standard library, enumerates the finish orders and prints the branch values, thresholds and chip-conservation checks. It is an arithmetic example, not a poker solver, range generator or at-table assistant.

Changing the short stack, prizes, action or bounty can change the comparison. With a single winner-only residual monetary prize after a deal, classic ICM reduces that residual value to chip share; locked awards are constants. Do not import the usual payout ladder into a hand without checking the actual deal. Conversely, “ICM matters” does not establish a fold when the state is unspecified. For your own difficult short-stack hand, capture those missing details and what you actually thought at the time. Keep later analysis separate. A showdown supplies one holding; it does not verify the entire range that should have been assigned before the cards were shown. Use recording hands to preserve an honest record.

Give every chart a small compatibility label.

Record table size and position convention; all relevant stacks and posting conventions; blinds and antes; prior action; the chip-value or prize-value objective; permitted actions; and the source or version. A prize-sensitive comparison also needs payouts, remaining-field information and other prize mechanics. A tool with postflop support still needs its postflop settings to match the study question. 3

Label the reference matching inputs, approximate with named differences, or not comparable. Matching inputs does not certify solution accuracy. A screenshot with missing settings cannot provide an exact reference.

A six-max, no-ante, first-in 5 BB chip-value chart is not an exact answer for a five-handed 4.5 BB big-blind-ante spot near a payout jump. Likewise, shove/fold labels at 4 and 5 BB do not specify a 4.5 BB answer. Do not silently round a difficult spot into certainty or interpret a mixed frequency as confidence.

Stud hi-lo versus razz: which five of seven cards count?

You receive seven cards in either game. In Stud hi-lo eight-or-better, one player can win high while two others tie a qualifying low. In razz, a nine-low may win the entire pot. Why can the same two seven-card holdings award the full pot to opposite players when the game changes? Work from the exact cards, visible upcards and rules before declaring a winner.

Scope: Ordinary seven-card Stud Hi/Lo Eight-or-Better (Stud8) and seven-card razz with ace-to-five low, as described in the cited PokerStars game rules. All cards, exposed states and pots in this guide are fictional. Every seven-card showdown is complete and mutually exclusive from the other examples. The 300-chip and 200-chip awards assume all shown players are eligible for the same pot, no rake, fees, side pots or odd-chip procedure, and no further betting. Early-street exercises show only the information declared visible at that time. The examples are not Nolan's hands, solver solutions, population data, win-rate estimates, or live betting recommendations.

1. Stud8 and razz do not share one pot rule

Each player ordinarily receives seven personal cards: two down and one up on third street, one additional upcard on each of fourth through sixth streets, and a final card down on seventh. At showdown, use the best five-card subset of the seven. There are C(7,5) = 21 candidate subsets. There is no shared Omaha-style board and no rule requiring exactly two hole cards plus three community cards. The Omaha hi-lo comparison makes that distinction explicit.

These rule differences are documented by the official PokerStars Stud Hi/Lo rules and official PokerStars razz rules. Aces are low for ace-to-five low; straights and flushes do not penalize that low. A paired five-card low is inferior to an unpaired five-card low, but it is still ranked in razz if no better subset is possible. In Stud8, it is not a qualifying low.

Compare low hands from the highest rank downward. For example, an unpaired 7-5-4-3-2 beats 7-6-3-2-A: the first comparison is the tied highest seven, then five beats six. Never sum ranks or assume having an ace alone makes a low stronger. The lowball rules overview explains why this is not the same as deuce-to-seven.

A's ace-high spade flush beats B's wheel straight and C's trip kings for high. A and B tie for the best low, 5-4-3-2-A. A can use 7♠ for the high flush but substitute 5♥ for the low wheel. From the 300-chip pot, the high half pays A 150 and the low half pays A and B 75 each. Final awards: A 225, B 75, C 0. That is a three-quarter share for A and a quarter for B, not evidence about net profit or any past wagering decision.

In Omaha hi-lo, independently selected high and low hands follow the exactly two-plus-three construction. In this Stud8 case, each best five comes from the player's personal seven cards. 3. A low straight flush can scoop Stud8

Different fictional showdown, 200-chip pot. A holds A♥ 2♥ 3♥ 4♥ 5♥ Q♠ K♦. B holds 6♣ 7♣ 8♦ 9♦ T♦ J♥ Q♣. A's best high is the five-high straight flush, and A's best low is the same wheel. B's best high is a queen-high straight. B has only three qualifying ranks at or below eight in seven cards and cannot make a five-card eight-or-better low. A receives all 200 chips through the high win and sole qualifying low.

This is a deliberately unusual but valid way to demonstrate a scoop. It is not a claim about the frequency of straight flushes or about which starting three cards to play.

5. Equal razz lows share a pot; suits do not break the tie

In a separate 200-chip razz showdown, A holds A♠ 2♣ 3♦ 4♥ 5♠ 9♥ K♦ and B holds A♥ 2♦ 3♣ 4♠ 5♥ T♣ Q♣. Both can choose a five-card wheel. They share the same best five ranks, despite different suits, so each receives 100 chips. Suit ordering can determine a third-street bring-in with tied upcard ranks in these cited rule sets. It does not break a low-hand tie at showdown.

6. Third and fourth street: the exposed cards change who acts

At the beginning of third street, each player receives two downcards and one upcard. With the fictional exposed cards below, no downcards are supplied or inferred. For Stud8, the lowest upcard brings in; for razz, the highest upcard brings in. In each game the source's suit order breaks an upcard-rank tie, not a showdown hand tie.

Third street, only the upcards are known

A 2♣, B Q♥, C Q♦, D 7♠. In Stud8, A with 2♣ is the bring-in. In razz, B with Q♥ brings in: B and C both show a queen, and hearts outrank diamonds for this bring-in tie. No private cards are revealed by this exercise.

At fourth street, the first actor is determined from the visible exposed hand, not merely from the prior bring-in or a fixed button. Use a different face-up state: A shows 2♣ 8♦, B shows Q♥ 9♣, C shows Q♦ 7♣. All six upcards are distinct.

Stud8 starts with B's queen-nine, the strongest high exposed two-card hand in this example. Razz starts with A's eight-two, the lowest exposed two-card hand. In the official game descriptions, fifth and later street order likewise follows the relevant exposed high or low hand. This exercise does not model the all-in ante exception, tied exposed-hand seat-order procedure, or a rare shared final card when a stud table exhausts its deck. Consult the current house rules for those cases.

7. Visible cards reduce the unknown deck, but do not prove a draw wins

Unlike Omaha's shared flop, the upcards in stud reveal individual players' cards. You know your own downcards and your own upcards; you see opponents' upcards, but not their hidden cards. Do not write an opponent's later revealed downcards into an earlier street's information set.

Separate fictional six-card information sets: Your own three known cards are A♥ 4♥ 7♣. Three other players show 3♠ 5♦ K♣. There are 52 − 6 = 46 unexposed physical cards, including three remaining threes. In a different version, one opponent's visible K♣ is instead 3♦. There are still 46 unknown cards, but now only two threes remain in that unknown deck. These cards are not available for a future deal, but they still belong to their exposed players.

The visible ranks change a simple count, not the likelihood that an actual low wins. Additional downcards, folds, other visible streets, action and future cards can change the relevant information. Do not label these two or three threes as winning outs without checking full opponent possibilities. 8. A compact after-play review routine

  1. Write the variant: Stud8, razz, Stud high, Omaha hi-lo or another format. Do not import an eight-or-better condition into razz.
  2. Preserve street visibility: distinguish your three initial cards, other upcards and unknown downcards. Record the visible cards when the decision happened.
  3. Rank all twenty-one five-card subsets: for Stud8, choose high and qualifying low independently; for razz, choose the best ace-to-five low even if it is higher than eight or paired.
  4. Check pot eligibility: with no Stud8 qualifier, high receives the whole eligible pot. If a low qualifies, split the eligible pot into high and low shares, then share ties within each half. Razz awards the full eligible pot to its best low.
  5. Describe limits: what was exposed, what was hypothetical, what remains unknown, and whether an actual venue's bring-in, side-pot or chip-handling rules change the settlement.

This is a study checker for completed, exposed scenarios, not a real-time assistant or a replacement for floor decisions. 9. Check yourself before opening the answers

Does Stud8 require three cards from a shared board, like Omaha hi-lo?

No. At an ordinary seven-card showdown, each Stud8 player chooses any best five from their own seven. Omaha hi-lo uses exactly two private cards and three community cards for each high and low selection.

Why does A receive 225 chips in the three-way Stud8 example?

A wins the 150-chip high half with a flush and shares the 150-chip low half with B, taking 75 more. Thus A gets 225 of 300. C gets nothing.

Can A use the 7♠ for high and the 5♥ for low in the same seven-card Stud8 hand?

Yes. The best five for high and the best qualifying five for low can be chosen separately. A's flush and wheel are both legal five-card subsets.

If nobody makes an eight-low or better in Stud8, is half the pot left unclaimed?

No. When no player has five distinct ranks of eight or below, the best high hand wins the whole eligible pot.

Why does B's nine-low win the same-card razz comparison?

Razz has no eight-or-better qualifier. B has a legal unpaired 9-4-3-2-A low, better than A's unavoidable pair in its best five-card selection.

Does the heart suit beat the diamond suit for the low-pot tie at showdown?

No. Suits can break third-street bring-in rank ties under the cited rules, but they do not rank otherwise equal low hands at showdown. Two wheels tie in the razz example.

Who brings in the fictional third street with A 2♣, B Q♥, C Q♦ and D 7♠?

In Stud8, A shows the lowest card. In razz, B shows the highest card; the Q♥ has the higher suit for the Q♥ versus Q♦ tie.

Does exposing a second three among six known cards change the count of unknown threes?

Yes. With one exposed three, three threes are unexposed; with two exposed threes, two remain. Both separate scenarios have 46 physically unexposed cards. No winning probability is established by that count.

Describe the board before judging the hand

A board has several features at once: its ranks, suits, pairs and connections. Rainbow describes three different flop suits, two-tone means two cards share a suit, and monotone means all three share a suit. Those labels do not tell you whether the ranks are connected.

Disconnected and rainbow

Flop

K♠7♥2♦

There is no immediate flush draw and little straight connectivity. “Dry” is a description of relatively few draws, not a guarantee that nobody has a strong hand.

Connected and two-tone

Flop

9♥8♥6♣

A player could already hold a straight, a set, a pair or a draw. Two hearts in a player’s hand make a flush draw, not a completed flush.

Monotone

Flop

A♥9♥4♥

Two hearts in a player’s hand complete a flush. One heart gives only four hearts so far. The board’s three matching suits are not themselves a five-card flush.

Paired

Flop

Q♣Q♦5♠

The board contributes a pair of queens to everyone’s possible five-card hand. Another queen can make trips; a pocket pair of fives makes a full house.

A draw needs a specific improvement

An open-ended straight draw can complete at either end. A gutshot needs an internal rank. A backdoor draw needs both the turn and river to cooperate. Count physical cards, not attractive-sounding hand labels. Then ask whether those cards would actually win against the opponent.

Do not count the same card twice

Fictional player’s cards

J♠T♠

Flop

9♠8♦2♠

Nine unseen spades would complete a flush. Four queens and four sevens would complete a straight. But the queen of spades and seven of spades appear in both lists. That leaves 9 + 8 − 2 = 15 distinct cards that complete at least one of these draws on the next card.

Fifteen is not a guarantee of winning: an opponent may have a better flush or improve later. This count assumes only these five cards are known and the standard deck is in use.

Holding A♠ J♠ on T♥ 6♦ 2♠ is different. There are only three spades among the five known cards, so a flush needs a spade on both later streets. Calling that a nine-out flush draw would be wrong. The math guide explains why completing a draw and winning are different events.

A good hand can change relative strength

The “nuts” means the best possible hand in the stated situation. A hand can be best now without being unbeatable by the river. A “scare card” changes plausible strong hands; it does not prove that an opponent holds them.

Flop

K♠7♠2♦

A leads with three kings. A 3♠ turn gives B an ace-high flush. A 2♣ river pairs the board and gives A a full house, kings full of twos. The lead changes twice without either player changing their hole cards.

Check your reading

Is every rainbow flop dry?

No. The suits can all differ while the ranks connect strongly. For example, 9♣ 8♦ 7♠ allows straights and straight draws despite being rainbow.

The flop is A♥ 9♥ 4♥. A player holds K♥ Q♣. Is that already a flush?

No. Those cards supply four hearts, not five. The player needs another heart on the board to complete a flush.

Does a draw completing on the turn settle who wins?

No. A different player might hold a higher version of the same hand, and the river can change the ranking again. Compare the final best five cards at showdown.

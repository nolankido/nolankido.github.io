Three-bet sizing: build the pot you can explain

You face a 2.5 big-blind open. Should the next raise be to 8, 10 or 12 BB? The numbers are not simply different bets. Each size changes the caller's price, the chips still behind, and the stack-to-pot ratio on a possible flop. Start with the accounting, then study which ranges might use each size.

Educational review away from live play. All hands, actions, cards and stack sizes in this guide are fictional and made for arithmetic practice. The examples assume a six-handed no-limit hold'em cash game with 0.5/1 BB blinds, no straddle, no ante and no rake in the calculations. They do not establish any solver-approved size, recommended personal range, opponent fold frequency or profitable action. No purchase or wagering is necessary.

1. Say what the chips mean before studying a size

The big blind starts the round as a live wager of 1 BB. An opener who raises to 2.5 BB has a total of 2.5 BB committed on this street, not 2.5 BB added to a previous open. A player who three-bets to 10 BB makes their total contribution 10 BB. A caller who already has 2.5 BB invested owes another 7.5 BB to match that raise. The amount to call is a difference, not the size printed on a chip stack.

When a full opening raise goes from 1 to 2.5 BB, the increase is 1.5 BB. Under ordinary no-limit full-raise rules, the next full raise must add at least that amount, so the minimum three-bet here is to 4 BB total. The 8, 10 and 12 BB raises below all exceed the minimum. The next raise after a three-bet is a four-bet. The 2026 Poker TDA rules, Rule 45 covers minimum full raises and the complications of short all-ins in covered tournaments; a live room or online platform's applicable rules control its games.

Before comparing numbers: a raise-to size may differ by position, stack depth, open size, callers, ante, rake, allowed action tree and the hands assigned to each response. Even two charts displaying “10 BB” may represent different games. Start with the fold/call/three-bet/four-bet action guide if the sequence itself is new.

2. Three possible button three-bets, counted to the flop

Fictional hand A: cutoff opens, button considers a three-bet

Six players are dealt in. Everyone starts with 100 BB before posting; blinds are 0.5/1 BB. Under the gun and hijack fold. Cutoff opens to 2.5 BB. You are the button holding A♥ J♥, with nothing invested yet. The small blind has 0.5 BB posted and the big blind has 1 BB posted; neither has voluntarily acted. No opponent range or read is known. Ignore rake and assume no ante for this arithmetic exercise.

The pot before your action is 4 BB: cutoff 2.5 + small blind 0.5 + big blind 1. The options below are independent branches. In each branch you three-bet, both blinds fold, and then the cutoff calls. If either blind continues or the cutoff four-bets, these figures are no longer the flop pot for that other history.

Read the middle row carefully. Your 10 BB raise contributes 10 new big blinds, so the temporary pot becomes 4 + 10 = 14 BB. After the blinds fold, the opener still has 2.5 in the pot and must add 10 − 2.5 = 7.5 BB to call. If they do, the flop pot is 10 + 10 + 0.5 + 1 = 21.5 BB. Both continuing players began with 100 and now have 90 behind. The heads-up stack-to-pot ratio (SPR) is 90 / 21.5, approximately 4.19.

The smaller and larger branches use the same formula: the flop pot is twice the three-bet total plus 1.5 BB of dead blinds, provided only the opener calls. The remaining stack is the original stack minus the amount each continuing player contributed. These are accounting identities, not arguments that a larger three-bet always earns more.

Price is not strategy. Increasing the raise makes the opener call more chips and decreases the flop SPR if they call, but also exposes more of your own stack if they continue or re-raise. The cutoff's actual range, your range and the blinds' willingness to continue determine what a size achieves. Without those inputs, there is no justified ranking of 8, 10 and 12 BB.

3. A single-raised pot is a different future betting problem

Now use the same hand A setup, but suppose you call the 2.5 BB open rather than three-betting. If both blinds fold, the flop pot is 6.5 BB: 2.5 + 2.5 + 0.5 + 1. Each continuing player has 97.5 BB behind, so SPR = 97.5 / 6.5 = 15. Contrast that with the 10 BB three-bet-and-call branch: a 21.5 BB pot with 90 BB behind and SPR about 4.19.

4. Raise to 10 BB in position versus out of position

Keep the same six-handed table, 2.5 BB cutoff open and 100 BB starting stacks, but change which player three-bets. This isolates how posted chips and the surviving seats affect pot geometry. It does not isolate the strategic effect of position, because the cutoff faces different possible opposing ranges and the blinds have different action options.

In-position branch: the button three-bets to 10, both blinds fold, cutoff calls. We already counted a 21.5 BB flop pot and SPR ≈ 4.19. The dead money is 1.5 BB from both blinds, and the button acts after the cutoff on later streets.

Out-of-position branch: the button folds. The small blind, who has already posted 0.5 BB, three-bets the same cutoff open to 10 BB total, adding 9.5 BB now. The big blind folds. Cutoff calls another 7.5. The pot is 10 + 10 + 1 = 21 BB: 10 each from the cutoff and small blind, plus the big blind's folded 1 BB. Each player has 90 BB behind, so SPR = 90 / 21 ≈ 4.29. The small blind acts before the cutoff after the flop.

5. A four-bet changes the accounting again

Return to the button three-bets to 10 BB branch of fictional hand A, with the two blinds folding. Instead of calling, suppose the cutoff four-bets to 24 BB total. This is a different branch. The cutoff already contributed 2.5 BB, so the new four-bet adds 21.5 BB. The pot before the button responds is 35.5 BB: cutoff 24 + button 10 + dead blinds 1.5.

The previous full raise went from 2.5 to 10 BB, an increment of 7.5 BB. Therefore the ordinary minimum full four-bet would be to 17.5 BB total. Raising to 24 BB is legal with these 100 BB stacks, but it is an illustrative action, not a standard or recommended four-bet size. Special short all-ins may alter which options are available under the relevant rules.

If the button now calls another 14 BB, each continuing player has contributed 24 BB. The flop pot is 24 + 24 + 1.5 = 49.5 BB; each has 76 BB behind; and SPR = 76 / 49.5 ≈ 1.54. If the button folds instead, the cutoff is awarded the 35.5 BB pot but has a net gain of 11.5 BB from the button and blinds, before any deduction. Calling 14 BB is not automatically correct simply because this pot is large.

In other words, the original 10 BB three-bet did not buy the flop. A four-bet creates an entirely new price and potentially a much lower SPR. The relevant decision must include range assumptions and the possibility of folding, calling or re-raising subject to legal action. For the earlier point in the decision tree, read responding to a three-bet and four-bet.

6. The same size at 40 BB is not the same situation at 100 BB

Re-run the button's 10 BB three-bet and cutoff call, now with both continuing players starting the hand at 40 BB, with both blinds folding and all forced bets fully posted. The flop pot remains 21.5 BB, but each player has just 30 BB behind. That gives SPR = 30 / 21.5 ≈ 1.40 instead of 90 / 21.5 ≈ 4.19 at 100 BB. A chart based on 100 BB stacks is not a substitute for examining the 40 BB decision tree.

At shorter stacks, a raise can consume a much larger share of the chips still available. Tournament blinds and antes, effective stacks, bounty payouts and bubble pressure introduce further differences. This 40 BB cash illustration says nothing about an optimal tournament shove or ICM call. For those separate problems, use short-stack decisions and tournament equity.

7. Turn a size into a reviewable hypothesis

Studying the arithmetic tells you what different actions cost, not which action earns the most. A three-bet can win when everyone folds, get called by one or more players, or face a four-bet. Each result depends on the opponents' ranges and the action tree. A genuine strategy study must compare those possibilities, not read a single SPR as a command.

For an intentionally severe toy calculation, imagine the button's 10 BB three-bet wins exactly the 4 BB pot already there if everyone folds, and loses every chip of the 10 BB raise if anyone continues, with no later showdown or betting value. The break-even all-fold frequency would then be 10 / (10 + 4) ≈ 71.4%. That is a threshold within that deliberately unrealistic binary model, not a forecast of actual folds or the profitability of any hand. The blinds can act, real hands can realize equity after a call, and the opener may four-bet. Compare the exact assumptions in the poker math reference before using such a calculation.

Rather than apply a universal “three times” rule, use a consistent notation for your hand and inspect an appropriately configured training reference. 8. Five quick checks before you look at the answer

Cutoff opens to 2.5 BB; button three-bets to 10 BB. How much does cutoff add to call?

7.5 BB. The cutoff already committed 2.5 BB. Calling a total of 10 costs 10 − 2.5, assuming no intervening raise.

Button three-bets to 12 BB; blinds fold and cutoff calls. What is the flop pot?

25.5 BB. Cutoff and button each contribute 12. The folded small blind adds 0.5 and the folded big blind adds 1. The pot is 12 + 12 + 1.5 = 25.5, and each continuing player has 88 BB behind when they started with 100.

Why is the 10 BB small-blind three-bet/call flop pot 21 BB, not 21.5 BB?

Only the big blind's 1 BB is dead. The small blind is one of the two continuing players, whose full 10 BB contribution is already counted. Add cutoff 10 + small blind 10 + folded big blind 1 = 21 BB.

In the 10 BB three-bet branch, is a cutoff four-bet to 16 BB an ordinary full raise?

No. The three-bet raised the total from 2.5 to 10, an increment of 7.5. The next ordinary full raise must reach at least 17.5 BB. Short all-in exceptions and betting-reopening rulings depend on applicable rules.

Does SPR 1.40 at 40 BB prove that three-betting to 10 BB is better than calling?

No. SPR describes the resulting stack relative to a conditional flop pot after both opponents' actions are known. You still need ranges, folds, calls, possible four-bets, rake and future play to evaluate the original decision.

9. Preserve the preflop story for later study

  1. Game and stacks: number dealt in, blind/ante structure, rake or drop, effective starting stacks, cash or tournament objective.

  2. Action in order: each fold, opening raise, cold-call, three-bet, four-bet or all-in, with every raise written to a total.

  3. Chip ledger: pot before your action, your existing contribution, additional chips you put in, dead contributions and all possible next callers.

  4. Branch-specific pots: one pot if everyone folds, one if the opener calls, another if someone behind calls or re-raises.

  5. Postflop geometry: only after a particular branch is fixed, divide the smaller remaining stack by the pot to find SPR. Name who acts first.

  6. Evidence and assumptions: separate observed opponent behavior from guessed ranges, and note any mismatch between the actual hand and a study chart.

The turn decision in a three-bet pot: price the next street

You three-bet before the flop. Your opponent calls, and the turn arrives. A check-back on the flop leaves a 21.5 BB pot; a 5 BB flop bet and call leaves 31.5 BB instead. The new card matters, but so does the betting history. Follow both paths through two distinct turn cards, then count the cost of checking, betting, calling and facing a raise.

Educational study away from live play. Every board, holding and action is fictional. This guide models a six-handed, 100 BB effective no-limit hold'em cash hand with 0.5/1 BB blinds, no ante, straddle, rake, fee or side pot. Bet sizes are legal arithmetic examples, not opponent reads, solver outputs, mandatory lines or recommended frequencies. No gambling account or money is needed.

1. Begin with the same 21.5 BB three-bet pot

Fictional preflop setup, carried from the prior lesson

Six players have 100 BB each before posting. Small blind posts 0.5 BB, big blind posts 1 BB; under the gun and hijack fold. Cutoff opens to 2.5 BB total. Button three-bets to 10 BB total. Both blinds fold, and cutoff adds 7.5 BB to call. The flop pot is 10 + 10 + 0.5 + 1 = 21.5 BB. The cutoff and button each have 90 BB behind, giving a starting flop SPR of 90 / 21.5 ≈ 4.19.

Cutoff is out of position and acts first on the flop, turn and river. Button, the preflop three-bettor, acts last on those streets while both remain in the hand. In this guide the button's illustrative hole cards are A♥ K♥ only to teach how a board changes a made hand. We do not assert that button three-bets those cards at any particular frequency, or that cutoff's call reveals an exact range.

This lesson continues the fully counted first-flop decision. If your preflop pot was not 21.5 BB, or the button faced a check-raise on the flop, do not reuse the numbers below. A flop raise creates a different pot and remaining stack.

2. Two different turns change the same hole cards differently

The two boards below are alternative fictional hands. Neither is the river of the other. The hole cards A♥ K♥ are kept constant for one player so the card comparison is clear. Both turn cards are compatible with the known cards, with no duplicate card used in any single hand.

Board A: A♣ 7♦ 2♠, turn K♠

Four community cards

A♣7♦2♠K♠

A♥ K♥ changes from a pair of aces on the flop to two pair, aces and kings, on the turn. The second spade creates a possible spade draw for someone holding two spades, but two spades on the board do not by themselves complete a flush. An opponent might hold a set or a different two-pair hand; neither is proven by the card alone.

Board B: 9♠ 8♠ 7♦, turn 6♠

Four community cards

9♠8♠7♦6♠

A♥ K♥ is still ace-high, without a spade. The board has three spades, so a player with two spades in the hole has a completed flush. A player with one spade has four spades in total and could make a flush if the river is a spade. A hand containing a ten or a five can make a straight using 6-7-8-9; the board's four-card sequence is not yet a five-card straight for everybody.

These card facts are not a betting chart. Board B creates far more possible made straights and flushes than Board A, but which player holds them more often depends on the actual preflop and flop ranges. A player who never reached the turn with a particular combination cannot suddenly have it because the board looks favorable. Use the board-reading guide and combination counting before claiming a numerical range or nut advantage.

3. Keep the card change separate from the pot change

On the flop, cutoff checked in both histories. History C: button checked back. History B5: button made a 5 BB continuation bet and cutoff called 5 BB. These histories are independent; a checked flop cannot also contain a 5 BB call. For either of the two boards, the mechanical turn ledger is as follows.

Two possible flop histories, applicable to either turn board
Flop historyFlop added, eachTurn pot before actionRemaining stack, eachStart-of-turn SPR
C: CO checks, BTN checks0 BB21.5 BB90 BB90 / 21.5 ≈ 4.19
B5: CO checks, BTN bets 5, CO calls 55 BB31.5 BB85 BB85 / 31.5 ≈ 2.70

History C did not spend anything on the flop, so each player still has 90 BB. History B5 added 5 + 5 = 10 BB to the 21.5 BB pot, leaving 90 − 5 = 85 BB behind each. The turn card does not put chips into the pot. That is why the same two ledger rows apply on either Board A or Board B, even though the ranges and hand strengths may be very different.

At the start of the turn cutoff acts first again. They may check or bet. If cutoff checks, button can check behind or bet. If cutoff bets, button cannot check, because there is an outstanding wager. The earlier flop line describes the history, not a rule that dictates the next bet.

4. History C: a delayed turn bet after both checked the flop

Start from the 21.5 BB turn pot with 90 BB behind each. Imagine Board A's K♠ arrives and cutoff checks again. Button could check behind, leaving 21.5 BB and 90 BB each for the river, or bet on the turn. If the earlier preflop aggressor checked the flop and now bets the turn after a check, this is often called a delayed continuation bet. The label does not tell us whether the bet is for value or as a bluff.

For a single arithmetic example, button bets 8 BB into 21.5 BB. Cutoff faces 21.5 + 8 = 29.5 BB already in the pot and owes 8 BB to call. If cutoff calls, the pot becomes 29.5 + 8 = 37.5 BB. Both players have 90 − 8 = 82 BB remaining; river SPR is 82 / 37.5 ≈ 2.19. If cutoff folds, there is no 8 BB call to add. A different size or a raise would have a different ledger.

Now reverse who bets first, without changing the starting pot: after button checked the flop, cutoff leads 8 BB on the turn. This can be described as a turn probe bet. Button faces the same immediate pot of 29.5 BB and would have to call 8 BB to make 37.5 BB. The numbers after a call match the delayed-bet example, but the information and choices do not: button now responds to a lead instead of choosing a first bet after a check. Button may fold, call or legally raise; checking is not available. GTO Wizard's turn-probe discussion explains why prior checks affect subsequent range assumptions. Its specific solver tree is not this hand.

Neither an 8 BB delayed bet nor an 8 BB probe is established as best here. On Board A, A♥ K♥ makes two pair, but which worse hands pay 8 BB? On Board B, the same cards are ace-high, but does a bet make any better hands fold? The board, opponent's possible range and action history matter independently of the 8 / 21.5 ≈ 37.2% illustrative size.

5. History B5: check back, or make a second barrel?

Now use only the history in which button bet 5 BB on the flop and cutoff called. The turn begins with a 31.5 BB pot and 85 BB behind each. Suppose cutoff checks. Button can check behind, preserving 31.5 BB and 85 BB stacks for the river, or bet again. A bet following a flop continuation bet is commonly called a second barrel. There is no automatic requirement to fire again just because the flop bet was called.

Compare two fictional turn bets, 12 BB (approximately 38.1% of 31.5) and 24 BB (approximately 76.2%). These amounts are convenient for teaching how a bet affects the river, not recommendations for Board A, Board B or A♥ K♥.

Turn branches after a 5 BB flop bet and call; cutoff checks turn
Button actionPot facing cutoffPot if cutoff callsRemaining stack, each after callRiver SPR after call
Check back31.5 BB, no wager31.5 BB85 BB85 / 31.5 ≈ 2.70
Bet 12 BB43.5 BB55.5 BB73 BB73 / 55.5 ≈ 1.32
Bet 24 BB55.5 BB79.5 BB61 BB61 / 79.5 ≈ 0.77

When button bets 12, the pot before cutoff responds is 31.5 + 12 = 43.5 BB. A 12 BB call yields 31.5 + 12 + 12 = 55.5 BB and leaves 85 − 12 = 73 BB behind each. For a 24 BB bet and call, the river pot is 31.5 + 24 + 24 = 79.5 BB and the remaining stack is 85 − 24 = 61 BB.

In the simplified model where cutoff calls and all future betting stops, no fees apply and ties are ignored, cutoff's direct-showdown break-even shares are 12 / 55.5 ≈ 21.62% for the small bet and 24 / 79.5 ≈ 30.19% for the larger bet. These fractions are not measured equities of any holding. One river card still remains, and the players can face more betting on that street. Actual turn-call profitability depends on equity realization, opponent responses and the possibility of further action.

On the connected Board B, the 6♠ can improve some opponent combinations dramatically. On Board A, K♠ improves the illustrative A♥ K♥ to two pair. Neither observation alone establishes a second barrel for 12 or 24. 6. A second barrel can face a turn check-raise

Return only to History B5, cutoff checks, button bets 12 BB. In a separate branch, cutoff check-raises to 36 BB total on the turn rather than calling 12. That adds 36 BB from cutoff on this street. The ordinary minimum full raise after a 12 BB bet would be to 24 BB; 36 BB is legal under these 85 BB remaining stacks. The raise is an invented action for accounting practice, not a prediction of cutoff's cards.

The middle pot is 31.5 + 12 + 36 = 79.5 BB. Button already bet 12 and therefore needs 36 − 12 = 24 BB more to call. If called, both players contribute 36 on this turn; the final pot is 31.5 + 36 + 36 = 103.5 BB. Each started the turn with 85 and has 85 − 36 = 49 BB left, for river SPR 49 / 103.5 ≈ 0.47.

Folding would not add the 24 BB call. A legal re-raise creates another branch. Checking is unavailable while facing a raise, and the 12 BB already wagered is not a credit against the 36 BB raise beyond the subtraction just performed. A check-raise does not, by itself, prove a made flush or straight on Board B or a set on Board A. A range-based evaluation needs to consider bluffs and value, not only the strongest possible hand.

7. A turn size changes what a river bet could cost

The term geometric betting describes planning bet sizes over multiple streets so that pot and stack growth are coordinated. It is useful to calculate the shape of that possibility without assuming a player should take it. Return to the two called second-barrel branches, each one ending with different pot and stack numbers.

Mechanical river bet capacity after each independent turn call
Turn bet and callRiver potEach stack availableA hypothetical full-stack river bet as % of pot
12 BB called55.5 BB73 BB73 / 55.5 ≈ 131.5%
24 BB called79.5 BB61 BB61 / 79.5 ≈ 76.7%

A 24 BB turn bet is about 76.2% of 31.5 BB. If called, a hypothetical 61 BB river shove into 79.5 BB is about 76.7% of that new pot. The percentages are similar because of how the arithmetic compounds. If a 12 BB turn bet is called instead, all 73 BB remaining would be about 131.5% of the smaller 55.5 BB river pot. This illustrates different remaining bet sizes, not a recommendation to shove or a guarantee that any river bet is profitable.

The river may bring a spade, a pairing card, a straight-completing card or a blank, depending on the board. Each can change who has strong hands and which worse hands call or better hands fold. River betting has no chance to draw another shared card after a call. Use the turn-and-river basics for that distinction, and the math reference if pot versus stack has become unclear.

8. Ask what the other player can actually reach the turn with

The numbers above prove what actions cost if they occur. They do not prove that any action occurs at a profitable frequency. In History B5, cutoff called the flop c-bet; in History C, cutoff had no flop bet to call. That difference may affect the hands that plausibly remain for both players. It does not allow us to infer a particular opponent's complete strategy without preflop and flop assumptions.

9. Six questions to check before revealing each answer

Both players check the flop in the 21.5 BB pot. Does the turn start with 31.5 BB because someone previously three-bet?

No. Preflop contributions were already counted in 21.5 BB. A checked flop adds nothing. Each player has 90 BB behind, and cutoff acts first on the turn.

On A♣ 7♦ 2♠ K♠, what is A♥ K♥, and is it the only strong possible hand?

Two pair, aces and kings. That does not rule out sets, other two pairs or future improvements for an opponent. The card comparison does not determine the entire range or mandate a bet.

After a 5 BB flop bet and call, cutoff checks the turn and button bets 12 BB. What is the pot if cutoff calls, and how much remains behind?

55.5 BB pot and 73 BB behind each. Start with 31.5 BB, add 12 from button and 12 from cutoff. Each started the turn with 85 BB and spent 12. River SPR is 73 / 55.5 ≈ 1.32.

Same flop history, but button bets 24 BB and cutoff calls. How does the river geometry differ?

79.5 BB pot and 61 BB behind each. Add 31.5 + 24 + 24. The remaining 61 BB would be a 76.7%-pot river bet if a player elected to bet it all; that is a size calculation, not advice to do so.

Button bets 12 BB on the turn; cutoff check-raises to 36 BB total. Does button owe 36 more, or 24 more?

24 BB more. Button already committed 12 on this street, so the matching amount is 36 − 12. A call makes the pot 103.5 BB and leaves 49 BB each. Checking is not legal when facing this raise.

On 9♠ 8♠ 7♦ 6♠, does the board alone prove a flush, and does a 21.62% turn call price prove an unspecified hand should call?

Neither. The board has only three spades; a player needs two more in the hole to have a flush now. The 12 / 55.5 ≈ 21.62% figure is an immediate-showdown threshold under the stated no-fee, no-tie, no-future-betting model, not measured equity or a complete turn strategy.

10. Write down a turn decision that can be studied later

  1. Exact history: blinds, positions, starting stacks, full preflop sequence, flop card suits and ranks, checks, bet-to totals and calls.

  2. Turn snapshot: the new card, exact pot, each remaining stack, which player acts first, and the best five-card hand if known.

  3. Independent branches: what happens on a check, a bet and call, a fold, or a raise. Record the amount more to call rather than adding an entire raise-to total twice.

  4. Possible river: what comes next if the turn action closes, how much remains, and what plausible river cards change the ranges.

  5. Limits: separate observed actions, revealed cards, invented range hypotheses, and any tool's particular settings. Do not treat your result as evidence that the line was optimal.

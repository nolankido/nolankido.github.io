Nolan Kido / A practical AI method

AI Works Human Decides

My approach to implementing AI in real life is simple: give it bounded work, then keep authority and accountability with the people affected. A convincing answer is not yet a useful result. This is a working standard, not a claim that every project succeeds.

  • AI Works. Use it for a defined part of the job, such as a draft, an organization task, or code with a testable outcome. Set permitted inputs, boundaries, and a stopping point first.
  • Human Decides. People choose the purpose, review the facts and consequences, authorize commitments, and can reject the output. Delegating production does not delegate responsibility.
  • Proof Matters. Check important claims independently and include setup, review, corrections, and maintenance when deciding whether AI actually helped.

Follow the four checkpoints: Decide, Brief, Check, Measure. The free worksheets and tools below can help with each stage. No AI runs in the browser tools themselves.

Inspect a recorded example: a website repair that passed 347 tests but still contained an HTML defect shows why independent checks matter. It documents one public repair and its limitations, not a claimed business outcome or proof of broad AI expertise.

The method applies to personal workflows and small teams, but it cannot replace domain-specific safeguards or professional review when consequences are high. About Nolan Kido explains the broader interests behind it.

1. What the source material actually said

The inputs were three existing public guides, not someone's private or unstructured notes. A weekly reset that does not become another job says to check commitments with their real sources, choose one manageable action, and allow breathing room. Before you ask AI what to do, decide what matters separates values, constraints, facts, and assumptions. What should you decide personally? keeps promises and priorities with the people responsible for them.

The brief for this pass: Turn these published ideas into one brief weekly-reset note, usable on paper, that keeps verified commitments distinct from proposals. Do not infer appointments, deadlines, priorities, another person's consent, or the user's real circumstances. Preserve the option not to use AI.

Inspect the source passages and the recorded brief

Source review: October 27, 2026. These excerpts were checked against a fixed copy of the published guides. They document this follow-up review. The original October 17 outline and editing record remain below.

  1. S1. Keep confirmed commitments separate. From the weekly-reset guide, “Check the commitments that have a real source”:

    Copy only what affects the coming week. Keep verified dates separate from ideas and requests that have not been agreed.

  2. S2. Choose one action. From the weekly-reset guide, “Reduce the list before organizing it”:

    Then choose one next action that is concrete enough to begin.

  3. S3. Do not manufacture a deadline. From deciding what matters, “State the decision without smuggling in the answer”:

    Do not create an artificial deadline simply to make the situation easier to summarize.

  4. S4. Keep responsibility for promises. From what to decide personally, “Separate expression from intention”:

    The tool should not decide what you feel, what responsibility you accept, or what you are promising.

  5. S5. Use a stopping point. From the weekly-reset guide, “Stop when the page has done its job”:

    The reset is finished when you can see the important commitments, understand the next action, and identify what you are deliberately leaving alone.

2. Inspect the initial draft and the corrections

First AI-assisted outline:

  1. Collect everything from the week.
  2. Sort it into urgent tasks, later tasks, and enjoyable activities.
  3. Create a day-by-day plan and assign an owner to each task.
  4. Share the plan with everyone involved.
  5. Review the plan every evening.

Editorial correction: A complete day-by-day plan and nightly review were extra obligations, not requirements of the source. Assigning owners and sharing a plan could turn unconfirmed proposals into commitments. The revision therefore removes those instructions, replaces assignment with confirmation, and ends after one useful next action and room to breathe.

  1. “Collect everything from the week.”

    Check: S1 limits the note to relevant, confirmed commitments.

    Correction: Copy only what affects the coming week. Put proposals on a separate “not confirmed” line.

    Still for a person: Check the original calendar or message. This example establishes no actual appointment.

  2. “Sort it into urgent tasks, later tasks, and enjoyable activities.”

    Check: S2 asks for one concrete next action. An urgency category does not establish what matters.

    Correction: Keep one manageable action and room for something enjoyable. Leave the priority to the reader.

    Still for a person: Decide what fits the week and the energy actually available.

  3. “Create a day-by-day plan and assign an owner to each task.”

    Check: The supplied material contains no actual dates or accepted responsibilities. S3 rejects invented deadlines; S4 keeps promises with people.

    Correction: Remove the requirement for a day-by-day timetable and unconfirmed assignments. Leave missing dates and responsibilities unresolved.

    Still for a person: Confirm any date or shared responsibility with its source or the person involved.

  4. “Share the plan with everyone involved.”

    Check: S4 requires a person's own intention behind a promise. A draft does not establish consent to its contents.

    Correction: End with a private note. Sharing or assigning work is a separate decision.

    Still for a person: Decide whether anyone needs the note, what is appropriate to share, and what they have actually agreed.

  5. “Review the plan every evening.”

    Check: S5 ends the reset when it has done its job. It does not require a new nightly routine.

    Correction: Stop after confirming commitments, choosing an action, and leaving room.

    Still for a person: Decide whether a later review helps. Count the effort of making and checking the note.

This is the draft written for this public editorial exercise, not a reconstructed output from a separate AI service or an alleged mistake made during an actual person's planning session.

3. The one-page result

Weekly reset, small enough to finish

  1. Check what is real. Look at the calendar or original message. Write only confirmed commitments that affect the coming week. Keep proposals in a separate "not confirmed" line.
  2. Choose, do not maximize. Name one thing that matters this week and one manageable action you can actually begin. A smaller action is a valid choice.
  3. Leave room. Note one person or activity you would like to make room for, without turning it into a performance target.
  4. Confirm and stop. Check any promise, time, or shared responsibility with the source or person involved. Do not assign work to someone else through this note. Close the page when it is good enough.

Still unknown: actual dates, other people's choices, personal capacity, and whether this routine will help a particular reader. Leave those blank until checked.

A sheet of paper is sufficient. AI can help reorganize a de-identified list, but it cannot verify a calendar, set your values, or authorize a commitment. Use the original free worksheet if you want blank prompts rather than this worked reduction.

4. Make the decision yours

AI Works Human Decides: the assistant can shorten the guidance and expose assumptions. You decide whether the resulting note fits your circumstances. Accepting some of it, changing it, or leaving it unused are all available choices.

  1. Keep: Which part would help with one task you actually have? Name that part and its purpose.
  2. Confirm: Which date, commitment, assumption, or other person's choice still needs an original source or a conversation?
  3. Leave out: What creates work without a useful result? Write a brief reason for your choice and what would make you revisit it.

Save the task and decision record (.txt)

Use the existing workbook's source/version field, answer-review record, and final task decision. Keep your completed copy in your own notes. Leave observed time and results blank until you have them; a simpler paper note may be enough.

5. What this exercise proves and does not prove

The finished note was compared with the three cited public sources. The correction removes implied scheduling, assignment, and review obligations that those sources do not support. This is evidence of a traceable editorial transformation, not a measured personal productivity benefit, learner study, or verified improvement in anyone's life. Editorial decisions were assisted by AI; a separate human reviewer has not signed off, and no participant used the plan.

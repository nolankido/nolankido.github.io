Start with the use

What would make this answer worth using?

To check an AI answer, identify the specific claim you might rely on, find evidence outside the answer, and test whether that evidence supports the claim under the right conditions. Recalculate numbers from the inputs; open citations and read their relevant passages; test code with a case that could fail. Record what remains unresolved before using or sharing the result.

Before checking, name what you intend to do with the result. A draft you will edit, a figure you will publish, and code you will run need different evidence. Circle the parts that could change an action: a date, amount, promise, instruction, or conclusion. Start there.

A second AI answer can suggest questions to investigate. Agreement between answers still leaves you needing evidence outside those answers. This guide gives you six ways to obtain it.

A quick first pass: claim, evidence, decision

  1. Name one claim and the intended action. “The meeting is confirmed” matters differently from “This sounds like a friendly invitation.”
  2. Find an independent check. Open the original notice or record, recompute from source inputs, or run a test with an independently known expected result. Another chatbot agreeing is not confirmation.
  3. Compare the exact claim with the evidence. Look for dates, conditions, units, missing people, uncertainty, and differences between a proposal and a commitment.
  4. Choose an evidence status before acting. Mark the claim supported, contradicted, or unresolved; write what was actually checked, and leave consequential work paused when the required evidence is missing.

Worked mini-check, fictional: An AI answer says “The Sunday meetup is confirmed.” The only supplied source says “Send meetup questions to the organizer.” It says nothing about confirmation. The confirmation claim is unresolved, not contradicted: the available evidence establishes neither that it is confirmed nor that it is unconfirmed. Ask the organizer or find a current event-status notice before relying on it.

What would change the status? A current organizer notice saying “The Sunday meetup is not confirmed” would contradict the confirmation claim. A current notice saying “The Sunday meetup is confirmed” would support it for the purpose and time checked. In either case, record the actual notice and its date. Do not turn missing evidence into a negative fact.

A different question: is a summary faithful? If the source says “We may meet Sunday if the weather allows,” summarizing it as “The Sunday meetup is confirmed” changes a conditional possibility into a commitment. That summary is not faithful to its supplied source and needs correction, regardless of what another source might say. A faithful version is “A Sunday meetup is possible, weather permitting.” The summary method examines this separate check.

Use more than one check when needed. A summary may also contain facts and calculations. Faithfully summarizing a mistaken source preserves its mistake. A valid calculation may use the wrong inputs. This is a review method, not an automatic accuracy test or permission to act.

01 / Facts & citations

Open the source. Then check the claim.

A real link and an impressive document title do not establish that the sentence beside them is true. Review each important claim against the relevant passage.

  1. Existence: find the document on its publisher's site. Match its title, author or organization, and date. If you cannot locate it, record it as unverified.
  2. Support: locate the passage, table, or result that supports the exact claim. Read the nearby qualifications. Separate what the source says from what the answer infers.
  3. Fit: check the population, setting, assumptions, and limits. A result for one task does not automatically apply to yours.
  4. Currency: for a changing fact, check the current official source and record the date you checked. An authentic historical document can be the wrong source for today's rule.
Try the citation check: a real source, an unsupported number

Fictional answer: “NIST's 2024 Generative Artificial Intelligence Profile says an answer is 99% reliable if a second chatbot agrees.”

Material to inspect: NIST AI 600-1, section 2.2, Confabulation (printed page 6; PDF page 9). The cited report exists. This passage discusses confident errors and misleading generated reasoning or citations; it does not provide the claimed percentage or agreement rule.

Review result: the source is authentic, but this passage does not support the claim. Remove the unsupported number and rule. A supported statement is narrower: NIST describes how generated explanations and citations can accompany an incorrect answer and encourage misplaced trust.

That is a finding about this claim and passage, not proof that every statement in the answer is wrong. Search the actual document for the alleged result before deciding whether another passage supports it.

Keep as unresolved: an inaccessible source, a missing supporting passage, or a fact whose current status you cannot establish. Do not turn a plausible explanation into a checked fact.

02 / Numbers & calculations

Recompute it, including the work that disappeared.

Write down the inputs and their units before repeating the arithmetic. Then use a calculator, spreadsheet, or small independent example to reproduce the result. Check whether the calculation answers the question you actually asked.

  1. Name the inputs, units, time period, and missing costs.
  2. Recompute with a method you can inspect rather than copying the answer's formula unchanged.
  3. Try a simple boundary: zero uses, one use, a smaller amount, or a known result. Explain why the output should change.
  4. Keep the result conditional on the inputs. Arithmetic alone does not verify estimated time, prices, or future outcomes.
Try the time-saving check: faster drafting, smaller overall savings

Fictional plan: a usual task takes 20 minutes and will be done 12 times. With AI, allow 30 minutes of setup, 6 minutes of drafting and 7 minutes of review per use, plus 18 minutes of additional repairs across the period.

Incomplete answer: “You save 168 minutes.” That subtracts drafting time alone: 12 × (20 − 6).

Usual workflow: 12 × 20 = 240 minutes.

AI workflow: 30 + 12 × (6 + 7) + 18 = 204 minutes.

Estimated saving: 240 − 204 = 36 minutes.

Independent way to inspect it: 30 setup + 72 drafting + 84 review + 18 repairs = 204. The arithmetic checks the estimate; these fictional inputs do not establish actual savings.

03 / Summaries

Keep conditions, ownership, and uncertainty intact.

Put the source beside the summary. Trace each consequential statement back to a passage. Check what was omitted as well as what was added.

  1. Find the source for dates, amounts, commitments, and named responsibilities.
  2. Look for changed certainty: proposed becoming confirmed, possible becoming required.
  3. Check dependencies and boundaries: “if,” “unless,” “subject to,” and “draft only.”
  4. Restore omissions that would change a reader's next action. Then review the repaired summary again.
Try the meaning check: a plan that depends on the weather

Fictional source: “We may meet outdoors on Sunday if the weather allows. Jo will check the forecast on Saturday; nobody has agreed to notify the group yet.”

Flawed summary: “Jo will tell everyone about Sunday's confirmed outdoor meeting.”

Repair: “An outdoor meeting on Sunday is possible, depending on the weather. Jo will check the forecast on Saturday. Responsibility for notifying the group is still unassigned.”

The repair preserves the condition and distinguishes checking the forecast from agreeing to send a message. It does not verify the weather or obtain anyone's permission.

04 / Messages & writing

Check the commitments, not just the tone.

Read the draft as its recipient would. Polite wording can still invent an agreement, a deadline, a personal experience, or a promise you never intended to make.

  1. Match names, dates, amounts, and factual statements to the supplied material.
  2. Underline promises and requests. Confirm who is being asked to do what, and by when.
  3. Read it aloud. Replace phrasing you would not use or cannot stand behind.
  4. Keep approval of the wording separate from approval to send or publish it.
Try the commitment check: an inquiry becomes a confirmed meeting

Fictional instruction: “Ask Sam to suggest two times for a short call next week.”

Flawed draft: “Thanks for confirming our call on Tuesday at 10. I'll send the contract beforehand.”

Repair: “Hi Sam, could you suggest two times for a short call next week? Thanks.”

The original instruction supplied neither a confirmed meeting nor a contract promise. A more professional tone cannot fix that change in meaning. Review the final draft, then decide whether to send it.

Define the output and action boundary in an AI task brief. For the reader's experience, compare two ways of giving the same instruction.

05 / Code & websites

Test a claim that could fail.

State the intended behavior before choosing tests. Use an isolated copy and sample data. Include a normal case, a boundary, and a failure or recovery path, with expected results worked out separately.

  1. Write a concrete before-and-after claim. Identify the inputs and expected behavior.
  2. Choose tests that distinguish the intended rule from a plausible wrong rule.
  3. For a website, check the actual page, keyboard use, narrow screens, error states, and relevant behavior with scripting unavailable.
  4. Check the published result separately from the local test. Keep the evidence tied to the version you tested.
Try the boundary check: a percentage mistaken for a fixed amount

Fictional requirement: return a price after a percentage discount. This deliberately wrong JavaScript treats the percentage as an amount:

function discountedPrice(price, percent) {
  return price - percent;
}

At a price of 100 and a 20% discount, both the intended rule and the wrong rule return 80. That one passing example hides the defect.

At a price of 50 and the same 20% discount, the expected price is 40: 50 × (1 − 20 / 100). The wrong function returns 30. This case distinguishes the rules.

A corrected formula alone would still leave questions about input validation, rounding, and the intended price format. The small exercise is a test-design lesson, not production pricing code.

06 / Personal advice

Separate facts from the life you want.

An answer can organize a choice without knowing which tradeoffs you want to make. Check its factual assumptions separately from its priorities.

  1. Mark factual claims you can verify, and predictions you cannot yet know.
  2. Name the objective the answer is optimizing. Ask whether that is yours.
  3. Notice whose interests or constraints are missing. Bring relevant people into the actual conversation.
  4. Use the answer to form better questions. Keep the decision and responsibility with the people affected.
Try the priority check: a higher salary becomes the whole decision

Fictional answer: “Choose the role with the higher salary. It is clearly the better offer.”

Missing material: working hours, the commute, stability, care responsibilities, interest in the work, and which compromises the person is willing to make.

Better next step: verify the terms of each offer, write down the person's priorities, and compare the tradeoffs. The salary difference is a fact to check; “better” depends on a purpose the answer has not established.

Explore what to decide yourself, or choose the purpose before asking for advice. For decisions with serious consequences, obtain review from someone with the relevant knowledge and responsibility.

Keep the evidence

Leave a record you can inspect later.

Write the claim, the evidence outside the answer, the check you actually performed, and what remains unknown. Name the use you are considering. A checked total does not certify a document; a repaired draft does not authorize sending it.

Finish with a specific next step: use the reviewed part for a named purpose, revise it, obtain more evidence, or leave it unused. If you cannot review the important parts adequately, a simpler method may be the better choice.

What the three evidence statuses mean

Supported for a stated purpose
A relevant, independent source or test supports the exact claim under the conditions you checked. Record the source, date, version, and any limits. This does not certify the entire answer.
Contradicted
The evidence disagrees with the claim. Correct or remove it; do not quietly substitute a different assertion without checking that assertion too.
Unresolved
A source is absent, unclear, outdated, or insufficient for the proposed use. Missing support is not itself a contradiction. State what would resolve it, or do not rely on the claim.

Keep the status proportional to the consequence. A low-stakes brainstorming draft may tolerate explicit uncertainty. A legal, medical, financial, safety or irreversible action calls for suitably qualified independent review. A prompt, citation, or checklist cannot provide that authorization.

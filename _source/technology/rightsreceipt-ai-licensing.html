PayPal AI Hackathon / Public project

Can AI decide whether someone may use a creator's image?

No. AI can help turn a vague request into questions about where, how, and for how long an image may be used. It cannot establish that the seller owns the image, accept terms on either person's behalf, determine whether an invoice was paid, or issue legally valid permission on its own. Those are separate decisions and sources of evidence.

I built RightsReceipt to make those boundaries explicit. The project was submitted to the PayPal AI Hackathon on Devpost. Its public, MIT-licensed source repository contains a self-run judge demonstration and a separate local AI diagnostic. The public demonstration video introduces the workflow. These links let a reader investigate the project beyond my description here.

Evidence status, November 13, 2026: Public submission and source are independently linkable. The submitted project's reported local tests use scripted people and injected payment-like responses, with a separate real classifier inference on synthetic requests. They do not demonstrate a genuine completed PayPal Sandbox transaction, verified paid webhook, production reliability, copyright ownership, or a real-world license.

One invented license / Specific terms

A $425 proposal is a test fixture, not a sale.

Imagine the fictional creator Maya Chen receiving a request from the fictional buyer Cedar & Tide to use an image called Harbor After Rain. A message such as “Can we use this in an ad?” does not establish the channel, territory, duration, alterations, exclusivity, attribution, sublicensing, or AI-training rights. Paying any amount would not fill in those gaps.

The local demonstration starts with human-reviewed sample terms. A deterministic creator pricing policy calculates USD 425.00 in integer cents, rather than asking a language model to invent a rate. A creator approval and a separate buyer acceptance attach to the exact proposed scope. In the rehearsal, injected provider replies stand in for payment events so application behavior can be tested without a financial transaction.

Why use a fictional example? It exposes a difficult workflow in a form other people can reproduce. It also prevents a plausible-looking receipt from being mistaken for proof that a real customer paid or a creator authorized commercial use.

Design / Seven authority boundaries

Seven claims need seven different checks.

My design question was not “Can an AI fill out a license?” It was “What evidence must be true before any part of this deal advances?” The stages below describe the intended workflow and the boundary of the published demonstration.

The distinction matters especially between “receipt exists” and “permission is proven.” A SHA-256 integrity check can help detect changed record bytes. It is not a digital signature, government registration, or independent proof that the purported creator owns the rights. This project models evidence handling; it does not replace legal or identity checks.

Actual inference / Synthetic inputs

A real model can run and still propose the wrong answer.

RightsReceipt has two local demonstrations that should not be confused. The application rehearsal runs licensing handlers with scripted participants and injected provider responses. Its rights terms are prepared fixtures, not newly inferred by AI. The separate read-only AI diagnostic executes a bundled, hash-checked supervised lexical classifier and a source-span extractor on project-authored synthetic buyer requests.

In the publicly reported diagnostic, two synthetic requests generate twelve field suggestions each, or 24 field comparisons. Those are checks on the demonstration inputs, not 24 independent real customer cases or an accuracy percentage. The classifier's numeric margins are not calibrated probabilities.

The revealing failure: one example includes a request for “Germany only,” yet the classifier still proposes the United States and Canada as a resolved territory. The diagnostic flags the conflict for human review rather than turning that label into authorized scope. This is the kind of visible wrong answer I want in a case study: it shows why a working model is not equivalent to a reliable decision-maker.

What would change that assessment? A separate, labeled evaluation set with new phrasing, ambiguous and adversarial requests, errors classified by their consequences, and a clear human escalation policy. The submitted two-request diagnostic does not provide those results. The code and instructions are in the pinned public judge guide.

Payment state / Simulation versus provider evidence

A successful local invoice rehearsal is not a paid invoice.

The published project reports three application-handler journeys: a normal simulated licensing path, recovery from an interrupted invoice-send response, and repeat/reset checks. It also reports three database resets and zero external requests from the local smoke. These are meaningful software checks because a lost response could otherwise lead an app to send a second invoice or assume an unconfirmed status. They remain local, injected-provider evidence.

A genuine payment claim would need more: a real Sandbox buyer payment, an authenticated PayPal invoice-status retrieval referring to the same invoice, and a correctly verified paid webhook. The public repository contains code and instructions intended to support that integration, but its submission explicitly states that the complete separately paid Sandbox journey has not been independently demonstrated.

This is not just caution about one app. PayPal's developer documentation describes subscribed webhook listeners and message-signature verification. A simulated callback or return from a browser payment page cannot establish that an authenticated provider recognized a settlement. Without that evidence, a real permission should not be activated automatically.

Public source / Inspect and reproduce

Run the bounded tests yourself, without a PayPal account.

The public source is a sanitized, single-commit snapshot. The commands below pin the snapshot analyzed for this article. Use Node 22.13 through 22.x, as specified by that repository, in a clean checkout. No secret, npm install, paid AI API, or PayPal transaction is required for these local demonstrations.

git clone https://github.com/nolankido/rightsreceipt-hackathon.git
cd rightsreceipt-hackathon
git checkout 646182a95faf6ab5448f10927ded1650ff38bdf5
node scripts/judge-console-real-smoke.mjs
node --no-warnings --experimental-strip-types scripts/judge-ai-proof.mjs

The first command runs a local browser-console smoke with real application handlers and simulated payment responses. A passing result must still say paymentEvidence: "simulated_only". The second performs actual local classifier inference on synthetic requests and must report paymentEvidence: "none". For a human-operated view, run node scripts/judge-local-console.mjs and open the printed localhost URL. Review the judge instructions for expected outputs, limitations, and failure behavior.

Verification provenance: The Devpost submission reports that the public checkout completed the smoke and model proof on Node 22.16.0 on November 10, 2026. This website article relies on that public report and source inspection; publication of this article is not an independent rerun of the project's Node 22 verification or a confirmation of current remote operation.

What the project establishes / What comes next

The useful result is a workflow with visible stopping points.

RightsReceipt is evidence of a publicly submitted, inspectable prototype and a precise design principle: the tool can prepare a proposal, but each authority must be checked separately. It does not establish that someone used the service to license an image, that the classifier is accurate in the wild, that the public site currently runs the full product, or that a real PayPal payment and permission grant occurred.

Before responsibly using this for a real licensing business, I would require several additional gates:

  1. Identity and rights. Establish that creators and buyers are authorized and that the offered work may actually be licensed. Do not treat uploaded content as ownership proof.
  2. Independent language evaluation. Test rights-field extraction on new, labeled requests. Treat contradictions, silence, and low confidence as unresolved, not defaults.
  3. Real payment acceptance. Demonstrate a separately paid PayPal Sandbox invoice with authenticated status and validated webhook evidence for the same transaction.
  4. Security, privacy and legal review. Evaluate access, data retention, record authenticity, and enforceability before inviting real people or money into the workflow.

These are future validation requirements, not completed milestones. The page offers no legal advice and makes no claim about copyright ownership.

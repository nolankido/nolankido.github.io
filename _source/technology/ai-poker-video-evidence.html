AI in real life / Poker and storytelling

A good story still needs an honest source.

I play tournament poker and publish first-person videos. That puts two decisions next to each other: what I think about a hand afterward, and what I can fairly tell a viewer happened. AI can help turn scattered notes into a clear scene. It cannot make the missing parts of a hand history appear.

This is a worked AI-assisted editorial proposal using the already published Portland Meadows Episode 1 companion, its public structured scene record, and a small offline checker. The hand recollections come from an earlier version of the narration. The current YouTube cut has not been independently reviewed shot by shot for this article. None of the candidate captions below is claimed to appear in that upload.

This is the practical meaning of AI Works Human Decides for my poker videos: let an assistant organize and shorten the story, use independent checks for narrow facts and arithmetic, and keep any release or strategic conclusion conditional on evidence a person can actually review.

Public source / Before asking AI

The hand record is not a complete video transcript.

In the 6-4 offsuit hand, I recalled shoving from the small blind with 13 players remaining and 10 paid. I reported about 148,000 chips with a 15,000 big blind, and the big blind called holding A-K suited. A four came and the hand won. In the four-handed sevens hand, I reported about 585,000 chips at a 30,000 big blind, a button open to 75,000, a reshove with 7-7, and a call with A-T offsuit. My sevens held.

The figures support two simple divisions: 148,000 ÷ 15,000 ≈ 9.87 big blinds and 585,000 ÷ 30,000 = 19.5 big blinds. The button's raise to 75,000 is 2.5 big blinds. These calculations assume the reported stack figures apply before posting; their exact timing is not independently established. A correct quotient is not a solver recommendation.

The result graphic needs another distinction. The event's advertised first prize was $5,100, but I reported receiving $2,900 after a four-way deal. The latter is a gross reported payout, not profit after expenses. A headline or graphic that casually swaps those amounts would mislead a viewer even if every other number were formatted beautifully.

This article's draft / AI-assisted writing

Ask for captions, not missing facts.

For this worked editorial pass, I bounded the assistant's job with the following brief. It uses only the public source record named above, not private footage or an unseen solver output:

Propose three short on-screen captions for the two Portland Meadows hands and the four-way deal. Use only facts explicitly reported in the linked public companion and arithmetic that can be recomputed. Keep reported amounts qualified. Do not supply unknown suits, a complete board, a current-video chapter time, an opponent range, net profit, or a solver verdict. Label the result as a proposal awaiting review of the actual video and graphics.

The AI-assisted candidates for this article are below. They are not transcriptions of overlays in the existing video, and they are not a recovered transcript from a previous model service.

  1. Near the money: 13 left, 10 paid. Small-blind 6-4 offsuit shove. About 9.9 BB from the reported stack.

    Source: first-person hand account. The big-blind figure is conditional on when the 148,000 stack was measured.

  2. Four-handed: Pocket sevens, about 19.5 BB. Button opens to 2.5 BB; I reshove.

    Source: reported sevens hand. The draft omits invented suits, board cards and a claim of optimal play.

  3. At the deal: Advertised first: $5,100. My reported post-deal payout: $2,900 gross.

    Source: the companion's result ledger. No net-winnings claim is supported.

These are editorially cautious starting lines, not accepted final graphics. They still need a person to check the current audio, displayed cards, pacing, typography and the context in which each line would appear. AI produced an organized candidate; this exercise did not measure whether that saved time. The source and the final cut have to earn the words.

Reproduce / Narrow independent check

The offline gate challenges claims, not video frames.

The separately published scene-claims JSON contains three conservative structured proposals for these same two hands and the payout. The standard-library Python checker compares those fields with its narrow source-backed rules. It is not a parser for the prose captions above and does not open YouTube or analyze an edit.

Both checks were rerun locally during this article's preparation. The three existing structured scenes returned zero targeted flags. The checker's built-in self-test caught seven seeded conflicts across four deliberately incorrect examples, then returned zero flags for their corrected versions. Those four before examples were constructed for testing, not observed mistakes in a released video or errors generated by another model.

To repeat the narrow result, save the two linked files to one folder. On Windows with Python 3.10 or later, run:

py -3 poker-vlog-fact-gate.py --self-test
py -3 poker-vlog-fact-gate.py --check portland-meadows-ep1-scene-claims.json

On macOS or Linux, substitute python3 for py -3. Neither command calls an AI model, sends a network request, writes an edit, accesses a private project or approves a video. The underlying structured source record explicitly marks current-cut timing, graphics inspection, event date and human release approval as unverified.

The lesson in a green check is deliberately modest: these selected fields did not trigger the chosen rules. The program cannot tell whether a later text edit matches its JSON, whether the voiceover says something different, or whether a spectator's card appears in the actual footage.

Human review / Claims on hold

The archived version 7 timestamps are not chapter times for the current public cut.

Does catching seven seeded conflicts certify the current video?

No. It certifies neither the present audiovisual edit nor the completeness of the rule set. It only shows that seven deliberately planted conflicts triggered this checker. A person must inspect the actual release.

Does winning with 6-4 offsuit prove the shove was good?

No. The outcome is reported. Exact stacks, calling ranges, payouts and a valid solver model are not. A favorable showdown is one observation, not a strategy proof.

Could a tidy AI-generated graphic still be misleading?

Yes. A beautifully rendered board whose cards were never verified, a shifted dollar amount, or a retrospective thought presented as an in-the-moment calculation can make the edit less trustworthy.

Reuse / Any consequential draft

Make a five-line decision receipt before publishing.

  1. Source: Which exact public record, passage or observed frame supports this statement?
  2. Calculation: What can be recomputed independently, and what timing or definition does it assume?
  3. Unknown: Which suit, board card, range, payment, permission or outcome is still unresolved?
  4. Check: What was actually tested, with which input and tool? What did the test not inspect?
  5. Decision: Keep, revise or hold the claim, with a named human review still required where appropriate.

That receipt works beyond poker. In my independent review-reply audit, three historical model-written drafts for fictional customer situations had no targeted flags in a narrow screen, while three deliberately damaged controls were flagged. Even there, unflagged did not mean approved. A checker could miss a reimbursement offer or misread a quoted warning. A business owner still needs to decide what may be promised. Here, a poker editor must decide which facts and visuals the footage truly supports. Different tasks require different checks, but the line between a candidate and authorization is the same.

For a reusable scene-by-scene form, use the existing truth-to-screen worksheet. For the decision at the table, use the poker hand-review method. Both ask for the information available at the relevant moment, rather than supplying a more confident story afterward.

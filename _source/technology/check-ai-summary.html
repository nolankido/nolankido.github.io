Read / Trace / Repair

A shorter paragraph can make a promise nobody made.

Check the important claims against the exact source, then check what the summary leaves out. A fluent paragraph is not the same thing as a faithful one.

This exercise shows how to review a short summary of planning notes. Read the source and deliberately flawed draft, try to identify the problems, then open the six explanations. Finish with a repaired version and a checklist for your own work.

Fictional teaching example. The people, event, notes, and drafts below are invented. The flawed draft was written to demonstrate errors; it is not a captured response from a particular AI model, a benchmark, or a report of Nolan's personal results.

01 / The reference

Keep the original within reach.

Task: summarize these notes for the organizers deciding what still needs to happen. Do not turn proposals into commitments or create a public invitation.

  1. S1. Draft plan for a neighborhood repair afternoon. This is a proposal, not a confirmed event.
  2. S2. The library room has been requested for 14 November, 2 to 4 p.m.; booking confirmation is still pending.
  3. S3. Mara can bring basic hand tools if transport is arranged. Nobody has volunteered to arrange it.
  4. S4. We discussed a $5 materials contribution. No amount has been agreed and attendance will not depend on payment.
  5. S5. Sam offered to draft an invitation after the room is confirmed. They have not agreed to send it.
  6. S6. Keep volunteers' phone numbers in the private organizer list; do not add them to the public invitation.
  7. S7. No sign-up deadline has been decided.

These numbered passages are the complete source for this exercise. In real work, also record the source's version and scope. A summary of one page should not quietly become a summary of an entire document.

02 / Try it yourself

Which claims would change someone's next action?

Deliberately flawed draft, not instructions to follow:

The repair afternoon is confirmed for 14 November, 2 to 4 p.m. Mara will bring tools. Everyone must pay $5. Sam will send the invitation. Sign up by 10 November.

Before opening the answers, compare the event status, each person's commitment, the cost, and the deadline. Then look for a restriction missing from the summary entirely. No answers are entered or collected on this page.

Some copied details are right: the proposed date and time match S2. That does not rescue the invented confirmation. Review a sentence at the level of each claim, not as a single all-right or all-wrong unit.

03 / Claim-level review

Six problems, each tied to the source.

Open any check. The links take you to the exact passage, not an unrelated page that merely discusses the topic.

1. Is the event confirmed?

Problem: certainty changed. The draft says the event is confirmed. S1 calls it a proposal; S2 says booking confirmation is still pending.

Repair: describe the date and time as proposed and keep the room-confirmation condition. Do not invent a booking reference or assume that requesting a room reserves it.

2. What did Mara actually agree to?

Problem: a condition and an unassigned task disappeared. S3 says Mara can bring tools if transport is arranged. Nobody has volunteered to arrange it. The draft's unconditional promise hides a dependency the organizers still need to resolve.

Repair: retain the transport condition and say that arranging transport is unassigned. Do not assign it to Mara merely because she offered the tools.

3. Is $5 a required payment?

Problem: a discussion became a requirement. The number appears in S4, but no amount has been agreed. The source also explicitly says attendance does not depend on payment.

Repair: call it a discussed contribution, not a fee. Checking the number alone would miss the more consequential change in what it means.

4. Did Sam agree to send the invitation?

Problem: both the action and its timing changed. In S5, Sam offered to draft an invitation after confirmation. Drafting is not sending, and a future condition is not current authorization.

Repair: keep the original action and dependency. Ask who will approve and send the invitation rather than filling in the gap.

5. Where did 10 November come from?

Problem: an unsupported deadline. S7 says no deadline has been decided. The draft invents a date. A plausible interval before the event is still an invention.

Repair: state that no sign-up deadline is set. A deadline might be a useful suggestion later, but it must not appear as an agreed fact.

6. What important restriction is missing?

Problem: a meaningful omission. S6 keeps phone numbers out of the public invitation. The draft does not leak a number, but it drops a restriction relevant to the organizers' next step.

Repair: preserve the restriction in this organizer-facing summary. Not every omitted detail is a defect: judge omissions against the task and audience. A public invitation should not include the private organizer list in the first place.

04 / A bounded result

One repaired version.

Proposed repair afternoon: 14 November, 2 to 4 p.m., subject to room confirmation. Mara can bring tools if transport is arranged; transport is unassigned. A $5 contribution was discussed but not agreed, and attendance is not conditional on payment. Sam offered to draft an invitation after the booking is confirmed, not to send it. No sign-up deadline is set. Keep volunteer phone numbers out of the public invitation.

This is longer than the flawed draft because the conditions matter. The goal is not minimum word count. It is enough context for the intended reader to avoid the wrong next action.

What this establishes: a corrected representation of the supplied fictional notes. What it does not establish: that a real room is available, the notes themselves are true or current, anyone has approved the plan, or an invitation may be sent. Source fidelity, external truth, and permission are three separate checks.

05 / Use the method

A review sequence for your own summary.

  1. Fix the purpose and scope. Name the audience, the source version, and what the summary will help them decide. Locate a readable original before generating anything.
  2. Split consequential sentences into claims. Check who, what, when, how much, under which conditions, with what certainty, and with whose permission.
  3. Find the exact support. Record the relevant passage, not just the document title or a link. Mark unsupported and contradicted claims instead of smoothing them into plausible language.
  4. Review the omissions. Look for exceptions, dependencies, unresolved questions, and sharing restrictions that would change the reader's next action.
  5. Repair, then compare again. A rewrite can introduce a new error. Check the changed claims against the source and keep unresolved points visible.
  6. Decide separately whether to use it. Verify external facts and current details where needed, obtain approval, and match the review depth to the consequences. Stop or ask when you cannot resolve an important claim.

For a short source, a direct read and your own note may be less work than generating and checking a summary. For a long source, record which sections you actually reviewed. A small spot-check is not evidence that the entire summary is accurate.

An optional prompt for a claim comparison
Compare this draft only with the source passages I provide.
For each material claim, quote the exact supporting passage and its label.
Mark it supported, contradicted, not supported, or missing a condition.
Check numbers, dates, people, conditions, certainty, and permissions.
List consequential omissions separately. Do not fill gaps with guesses.
Propose repairs separately from the original source and draft.

This asks for a useful comparison; it does not make the comparison independent or reliable. Check the quoted passages yourself. Repeating a confident answer or asking the same tool to approve its work is not a substitute for opening the source.

An explanation can be correct and still leave its reader stranded. The craft is not only in what the writer knows, but in how much unnecessary work the reader is asked to do.

The same instruction, two ways

Imagine a fictional guide for using a decision worksheet. Its purpose is to help someone keep a record of what they knew before making a choice, then return to that record afterward.

Preserve the baseline template. Instantiate a separate record prior to commitment, then append a retrospective without mutating the ex ante assumptions.

For a particular expert audience, this may be efficient. For someone opening a simple worksheet, it creates several new tasks before the original task can begin.

Keep one blank copy. Fill in a separate copy before you decide. When you know the result, add a review to that same copy. Leave the original reasoning in place so you can compare what you expected with what happened.

The second version is longer, but asks the reader to infer less. It names the objects, puts the actions in order, and explains the reason for the final instruction. Brevity and ease are not always the same thing.

Notice where the work went

The useful edit was not simply replacing long words with short ones. The writer had to decide what the reader already knew, which action came first, and what might be misunderstood.

“Add a review” alone leaves a question: add it where? “Leave the original reasoning in place” answers another: should the old explanation be cleaned up now that the outcome is known? A generous explanation anticipates the confusion that would otherwise become the reader’s private problem.

This is a design choice as much as a sentence-level choice. A heading, a label next to an example, or a little space between stages can do explanatory work without adding another paragraph.

Do not simplify away the thing that matters

Clarity is not permission to flatten a difficult idea. “Just trust the process” would be shorter than either instruction and much less useful. “Never change your mind” would be clear and wrong for the purpose.

The distinction that matters is between preserving an earlier record and refusing to revise a belief. The record stays; the understanding can change. A good simplification keeps that shape intact.

Similarly, technical terms can be generous when they save a knowledgeable reader from repeated explanations. The standard is not whether every reader could understand every sentence immediately. It is whether the intended reader has a reasonable path through it.

Leave a little life in the writing

An explanation does not need to sound like an appliance manual to be useful. A small example, an unexpected comparison, or a sentence with some rhythm can give the reader a reason to stay.

But decoration should not require a second explanation. A metaphor that makes the writer feel clever while making the subject harder to follow has moved the effort in the wrong direction.

There is also room for restraint. A reader may need one useful distinction, not the entire history of the subject. Giving them a place to stop can be an act of care.

Treat understanding as an invitation

A helpful draft leaves room for “I do not follow that part.” It does not make a question feel like evidence that the reader failed an entrance exam.

Before calling an explanation finished, try asking: What does the reader need to bring? What have I made them guess? What can they now do, see, or question that was harder before?

Those questions apply to a diagram, a set of instructions, an essay, or an ordinary conversation. The form changes. The invitation can remain.

Do the work that makes the reader’s next step easier. Keep the nuance that makes the explanation true.

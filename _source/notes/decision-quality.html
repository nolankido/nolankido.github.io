A good result is welcome. It is not, by itself, a complete explanation of whether the decision that produced it was good.

Separate two questions

The first question is whether a choice was reasonable given the information available at the time. The second is what actually happened. When uncertainty is involved, those questions can have different answers.

Consider a simple, illustrative decision. You are planning an outdoor event. A forecast gives a substantial chance of rain, and moving indoors costs little. Choosing the indoor option can be reasonable even if the afternoon turns out to be dry. The dry afternoon does not retroactively make the forecast say something else.

Likewise, staying outside and getting lucky does not show that preparing for rain would have been foolish. To judge the decision, we need the alternatives, the information, and the consequences, not just the weather at the end.

Write down the reasoning before the result

A short decision record can make the distinction concrete. State what you are trying to achieve, the options you considered, the evidence you have, and the uncertainty that remains. Then record why the chosen option is preferable and what would make you reconsider it.

This does not require an elaborate journal. A few honest sentences are more useful than a detailed explanation reconstructed after the outcome is known.

For the event example, the note might read: “Moving inside sacrifices the view but avoids a disruption if it rains. The forecast is uncertain, and the cost of changing venues now is low. We will move inside.” That explanation can be evaluated even after the weather is revealed.

Learn from outcomes without using them as excuses

Separating process from outcome does not mean ignoring results. Repeated surprises may expose a bad assumption, an unreliable estimate, or a missing alternative. A disappointing result can also reveal information that was genuinely unavailable earlier.

The useful review asks what was knowable, what was overlooked, and what has changed. It should be possible to admit both “the decision was reasonable” and “there is something to improve.” Conversely, a successful result should not shield a weak process from examination.

Match the process to the stakes

Not every choice deserves a formal analysis. For a reversible, low-consequence decision, a brief comparison and a willingness to adapt may be enough. For a costly or difficult-to-reverse decision, it is worth being more explicit about assumptions and downside.

The goal is not to eliminate uncertainty. It is to make the reasoning clear enough that a later review teaches something more useful than “do whatever worked last time.”

Judge the decision using the information available when it was made. Use the outcome to update that information, not to rewrite it.

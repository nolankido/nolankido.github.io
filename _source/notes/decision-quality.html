A good outcome is welcome. It does not, by itself, tell us whether the decision was reasonable. But “the process was good” should not become an excuse for refusing to learn.

Before the weather is known

Imagine a fictional outdoor gathering. Moving indoors now costs an extra $30. Staying outside costs nothing extra if it stays dry, but rain would cause a disruption valued at $100 for this simplified comparison. The estimated chance of rain is 40 percent.

Assume for the moment that both venues are equally pleasant, everyone can use either one, there are no other costs, and the aim is to minimize expected monetary cost.

Move inside: $30 for certain.

Stay outside: 0.40 × $100 + 0.60 × $0 = $40 in expected cost.

Under these assumptions: moving indoors has the lower expected cost.

The $40 is not a bill anyone will receive. In this simplified model, staying outside produces either a $0 or a $100 extra cost. Expected cost compares uncertain choices before their outcomes are known.

If the afternoon turns out dry, the indoor decision has not retroactively become irrational. It was made without knowing the weather. Equally, a dry afternoon does not prove that staying outdoors was the better policy.

Write the assumptions, not just the answer

A useful decision record would include the forecast estimate, the $30 alternative, the assumed disruption, and the fact that both venues were thought suitable. Without those details, a later review has little to examine besides the outcome.

It should also record what would change the choice. In this model, the two expected costs are equal at a 30 percent chance of rain. A lower credible estimate, a higher indoor price, or a different understanding of the disruption could change the conclusion.

This is not a demand to assign a probability to every ordinary choice. It is an example of making reasoning specific enough that it can be questioned. An uncertain guess written to two decimal places is still an uncertain guess.

Other people are not a footnote

The arithmetic depended on assumptions that might be wrong. Perhaps one venue is difficult for a guest to access. Perhaps people care deeply about being outdoors. Perhaps paying $30 means giving up something more important.

Those are not annoyances to hide in the calculation. They are part of defining the decision. Where a choice affects other people, a conversation may reveal more than another decimal place.

A worksheet should not be used to turn someone else’s preference into a score they never agreed to, or to claim that the “optimal” choice cancels their right to object. Some questions concern consent, fairness, or care, not just expected cost.

Review the result without rewriting the past

After the event, separate three questions: What happened? Was the original reasoning reasonable given what could be known? What should be different next time?

Suppose it stayed dry, but the indoor room turned out inaccessible to a guest. The lack of rain is a realized uncertainty. Failing to check the room’s accessibility may be an avoidable omission. Calling both “bad luck” would hide the useful lesson.

Conversely, a disappointing result does not prove that a foreseeable risk should always have been avoided at any cost. Decisions involve alternatives. The relevant comparison is with the options genuinely available, not an imaginary option that delivers every benefit without a downside.

Use enough process, not the most process

A reversible, low-consequence choice may need a brief discussion and permission to adjust. A costly commitment deserves more explicit reasoning. The point is to match the care to the consequence, rather than turn ordinary life into a sequence of applications to be approved.

The decision record includes a blank version, a completed fictional example, and a prompt for the conversation the numbers may be missing.

Judge the decision using the information available when it was made. Use the outcome to learn, not to rewrite the information or excuse an omission.

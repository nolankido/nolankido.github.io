The most interesting question about AI is not how much work it can do for us. It is which parts of the work we should still be willing to answer for.

My interest in AI is practical: making useful things, making difficult ideas clearer, and leaving more room for life outside the task. That makes delegation worth taking seriously. Doing everything yourself is not the only way to care about the result.

But there is a difference between handing over work and handing over responsibility. A draft can be generated. A calculation can be automated. A collection of possibilities can be assembled. Deciding what those things are for, and what you are willing to put your name behind, is a different kind of work.

I do not think the answer is to preserve a fixed percentage of manual effort. The better distinction is between work a tool can help carry and decisions that need a person who can explain them.

A good plan, for whom?

Consider a fictional example. Four friends have a free Saturday afternoon. Someone asks an AI tool to suggest a plan. The draft is coherent: an activity, a meal, a second activity, and a schedule that fits them together.

Assume, for the moment, that every practical detail has been checked. The places exist, the opening hours are correct, the costs fit the agreed budget, and the group could follow the plan. It still might be the wrong afternoon.

One friend wanted time to talk. Another would rather decide as they go. A third agreed to meet, not to have every hour assigned. Nothing in a fact check resolves those differences. A plan can be accurate and still misunderstand its purpose.

That is why the brief matters. “Fill the afternoon” and “Help us spend unhurried time together” are different requests. Before choosing a tool, someone needs to notice which problem is actually worth solving. And because this is a shared afternoon, that judgment should include the other people, not simply the person holding the phone.

The tool has not necessarily failed. It may have answered the question it was given. The error can be in what the person decided to ask.

Delegate a bounded job

There is a useful middle ground between “do everything” and “do nothing until I approve every sentence.” Give the tool a job with a purpose, boundaries, and a recognizable finish.

For the fictional afternoon, the request might look like this:

Help us compare two simple ways to spend an afternoon together. Leave most of the time open. Use only the preferences everyone has agreed to share. Separate suggestions from details that need checking. Do not book anything or message anyone. Give us a short comparison we can discuss.

This is not a magic prompt. It is a division of responsibility. The tool can assemble and compare. The people still choose the purpose, confirm the assumptions, and decide whether to make a commitment.

The same distinction can apply to a document or a piece of software. “Improve this” is an invitation to make many different choices. “Make this instruction understandable to a first-time reader, preserve these facts, and flag anything you cannot resolve” gives the work a direction and makes the remaining decisions visible.

A bounded task does not have to be a tiny task. You can delegate a substantial amount of production while keeping a few important boundaries clear. The aim is not constant supervision. It is knowing which decisions have actually been delegated.

Check more than correctness

A fact check asks whether a statement is true. A usefulness check asks whether the result serves its purpose. A permission check asks whether you are entitled to use the information or take the action. They are related, but none replaces the others.

In the afternoon example, correct opening hours do not mean everyone wants to go. In a family story, an accurate transcript does not establish permission to publish it. In an explanation, a defensible answer does not mean the intended reader can follow it.

These distinctions matter because “I checked it” can hide a very narrow check. Read the result as the person who will have to live with it. What are they being asked to understand, trust, disclose, maintain, or agree to?

Not every task needs the same review. A private brainstorm can tolerate guesses that would be unacceptable in a public claim. A reversible formatting change calls for a different level of care from an action that spends money or commits another person. Review should follow the consequences of being wrong, not the polish of the output.

For some jobs, a clear constraint and a small sample check may be enough. For others, you need the underlying source, an independent test, the affected person's approval, or qualified help. Where that check is unavailable, the responsible choice may be to narrow the claim or leave the action undone.

Keep the ability to disagree

There is a quieter risk in delegation: letting the first complete-looking version define what the task was supposed to become.

A long outline can make a short note feel inadequate. A full schedule can make an open afternoon look unfinished. A list of possible features can turn a small useful tool into a much larger obligation. Those are not necessarily improvements. They are choices about scope, often made before anyone has named them as choices.

It helps to write down a few things the result should not do. Do not add commitments. Do not make the reader learn a new vocabulary. Do not turn a private reflection into a public performance. Do not expand the project simply because expansion is available.

Then compare the result with those boundaries. Keep the parts that help. Change or discard the rest. Rejecting an attractive draft is not wasted effort if it clarifies what you meant to make.

This is also where taste enters the work. Two versions may both be accurate and useful, yet differ in tone, emphasis, or what they leave unsaid. The final choice can reasonably depend on the person, audience, and occasion. It does not need to pretend to be the single answer a machine ought to discover.

Finish with something you can stand behind

The case for using AI is not that human effort has no value. It is that effort can be spent where it contributes more. Someone who struggles with a blank page can begin with material to respond to. Someone with a clear purpose can explore alternatives without producing every version from scratch.

The useful outcome is not necessarily more output. It might be a shorter explanation, a simpler plan, a question asked before a commitment, or a decision not to build something at all.

That is the standard I want this site to express: be willing to use powerful tools, and equally willing to question what they produce. Be specific about what is known. Be clear about what is illustrative. Do not make the tool's confidence stand in for your own reasons.

Let AI do more of the work when it helps. Keep the judgment that makes the work worth doing, and the responsibility for what you finally choose to share.

A question to keep

Before you use or share the result, can you explain what it is for, what you checked, and which choices are still yours?
